Author: Pablo Kuschniroff, assisted by Claude Code. Version 4, 18 October 2026.

This document exists for one thing: so that the procedures and the manuals of the method stay clean. When, in the main document, in a step contract or in a lesson of the course, I claim something along the lines of "I learned this the hard way" or "I imposed this because the model applied it badly", I do not want to clutter the explanation with dates, commit identifiers and pending-item numbers. I put a short mark, [Ref. N], and whoever feels like investigating the case comes here and finds it with its date, its evidence and my reading of what happened. Whoever does not feel like it keeps reading without stumbling.

Two clarifications before starting. The first: the three systems these cases come from are real and are in production today, with real people using them. The clients are anonymised, and I call them "the Health Fund" (with its "Authorizer") and "the Association"; their systems I call "Autorizaciones" and "Kikan". The third repository is the tool where the method was born, yujin-forge, which is mine and carries its own name. The numbers, the dates and the commit identifiers are real; if one day I have the clients' permission, I will put the names in.

The second clarification matters more, and I repeat it in the main document because I do not want it to be misread: the method did not come only from the casuistry of failures. It came from two things that combined. On the one hand, from my own knowledge of systems engineering, which I have been applying since long before these models existed; most of what I demand in the steps is what I always demanded in a well-run project. On the other hand, from the fact that the incidents of these five months showed me, one after another, where the models apply those principles in a fragile way: where they say they tested and did not test, where they estimate with a straight face, where they fill a gap in the business rules with a whim, where they over-report. The cases that follow are, then, the proof of where it is necessary to tighten, not the origin of the whole method. Read them through that lens.

How to cite

In any document of the method, [Ref. 26] links to the entry "Ref. 26" of this file. The entries are grouped by source system and, within each group, in chronological order. At the end there are three complete chronologies, in case someone wants to see the whole sequence, and a table that maps each failure to the step that covers it.

A. yujin-forge: where the method was formed (May to October 2026)

Ref. 1 — The quality standards are born (25 May)

I wrote the first quality standards document, SQ.md, on 25 May (commit 2219f7a). It is the first "working agreement" between the human and the agent, and I wrote it because I had spent two weeks repeating the same things to the model in every session: that my time is sacred and is not spent on what it can resolve on its own; that no technical debt is left behind, whatever breaks is fixed at the root; that the documentation is kept up to date; that the scope is exact, no more and no less than what was asked. None of that is new in systems engineering. What was new was having to write it down for a collaborator who arrives every morning with no memory of yesterday. It is the origin of step 1.

Ref. 2 — The first anti-pattern register (27 May)

On 27 May (commit 73f4029) I opened GOTCHAS.md: a file where every error that repeated itself is written down in a fixed format: how it smells, what the root cause is, how it was fixed, and how to detect it next time before it hurts. I opened it because the model was making the same mistake in different sessions with no memory whatsoever of having made it before. It is the "anti-patterns" half of step 11.

Ref. 3 — The gates of the workflow, injected only when they apply (2 June)

On 2 June (commit 5b47475) I defined explicit gates in the workflow, numbered G1 to G9, and a documentation gate, G-DOC, which requires the documentation to be updated in the same commit as the change. And I took a decision that turned out to be important: the doctrine of each gate enters the agent's prompt only at the moment it applies, not always, because context is finite and whatever is injected in excess is paid for in attention. It is the direct precedent of the idea of a "step contract" that gets injected when the gate is crossed.

Ref. 4 — The testing policy is reformulated (4 June)

Until 4 June I insisted on "first the test, then the code" for everything. That day (commit a9f59c7, document testing-policy.md) I accepted that it does not work universally: by default the tests come after implementing, test-first stays as an option for small, well-defined scopes, the skeleton has to be green as a gate, and I added a protection against loops, a modal that stops everything when the agent goes round in circles without making progress. I tell it because it shows that the method also corrects itself when a rule of mine turns out to be too rigid.

Ref. 5 — Decisions are frozen before building (4 June)

Before a big sprint I wrote and numbered three structural decisions (D1, D2, D3) in a decisions document of 4 June. It was the first time I formalised something I had always done in classic projects: the decision is written before building, with a date, so that afterwards nobody argues it from memory. It is the precedent of step 6.

Ref. 6 — I eliminate the multi-agent team with a number in hand (5 June)

I had a team of five agents (director, analyst, programmer, tester, skeleton builder) that passed work among themselves. I ran a measured experiment: that team spent 60 dollars and 73 minutes and delivered something half-done; a single session with a single agent took 90 minutes and delivered something publishable. On 5 June (commit 7584f8b, documented in an internal paper) I eliminated the whole team. It is the first case of "retrospective with a number in hand, and redesign", and it is the reason why step 23 demands numbers and not impressions.

Ref. 7 — The plan becomes the list of pending items (6 June)

On 6 June (commit 4fc82c6, plan for the pending-items register) the work plan came to BE the queue of pending items: a canonical, numbered list, with commands to manage it, refreshed in every session. There stopped being a "plan" on one side and "tasks" on the other. It is the origin of step 5.

Ref. 8 — Verification honesty and workflow kinds (10 June)

On 10 June (commits 7ab9d8a and 2c552c0, documents verification-honesty.md and workflow-kinds.md) I made the doctrine mandatory in every prompt and wrote the rule of "verification honesty": verify that the net really turns red when it has to, declare the blind spots, and make claims proportional to what was checked. I also separated three workflow kinds: new project, correction and evolution. It is the origin of step 12 and of the "red" half of step 8.

Ref. 9 — Work discarded for not reading the map (12 June)

On 12 June a session started building without reading the project's knowledge graph, and the work of a whole afternoon was discardable because it contradicted decisions that were already written. From there comes the mandatory-reading rule of step 2: the map exists, but if reading it is not enforced, it does not act.

Ref. 10 — Autopilots of 24 and 48 hours (14 to 21 June)

Between 14 and 21 June I let the agent work alone for a day, and then two, with a written mandate: commit and push for every piece, without asking, and I received a summary. I tell it because it proves that the method withstands long autonomy when the working agreement and the pending-items register are well made; and because it was there that I learned to read summaries with distrust, which is step 12.

Ref. 11 — Diagnostic mode on other people's systems (July)

In July (document diagnostic-mode.md) the agent started entering clients' production systems in diagnostic mode: read-only, bounded, touching nothing. It is the first level of the scope of the resident agent of step 23.

Ref. 12 — Performance patterns taken from the Health Fund's production (22 July)

On 22 July (commit 873339c) I incorporated into the tool scale patterns extracted from the Health Fund's system: the two-transaction one and, above all, "measure before touching". It was the first time the method fed on a system that was already in production, and it is a precedent of step 7.

Ref. 13 — The agent becomes resident in the Health Fund's production (14 August)

On 14 August the agent was installed inside the client's system, as a resident assistant, with three rules: a mandatory map of the system before operating, "operational solution first" when something fails, and the ability to open tickets. It is the origin of the first half of step 23.

Ref. 14 — Sizing as a gate (10 and 11 October)

On 10 and 11 October (commits c602725 and 0bd1b05, document sizing.md) I formalised sizing before building, choosing among fixed options, and I recorded it as one more gate (G9). I formalised it late, after the Kikan blow I tell in reference 15, and that is precisely the lesson of step 3.

Ref. 15 — The asynchronous persistence interface from line one (13 October)

On 13 October (commit 7391cc5, document persistence-interface.md) I wrote the rule that the interface with the database is asynchronous from the first line, even if the first engine is synchronous. It is an "asymmetric" decision: it costs little if you are wrong and costs an enormous amount if you do not take it. I know because in Kikan I did not take it in time and changing it afterwards cost a whole stage of the re-engineering, E6, with three test suites moved to asynchronous. It is a case of step 3.

Ref. 16 — The orphan turn of one hour forty-three (14 October)

On 14 October the agent worked for one hour and forty-three minutes invisible to me, and made six commits in that span. It was a failure of the method, and an ugly one. I fixed it at the root in version 1.18.0 (commit b80a659): a silence watchdog that kills the turn if it stops giving signs of life, an inter-process leash so that the child dies with the parent, and 28 tests. It falls under step 12 (the report has to be visible) and step 14 (on doubt, the system does not go on).

B. Autorizaciones: the method under real load (June to October 2026)

Ref. 17 — The initial design that the repository overtook (11 June)

Autorizaciones started with a design document ("option C"). The repository overtook it very quickly: what was built was far more than what the paper said. The lesson I was left with, and which is in step 5, is that the specification is worth having as a starting point and the living plan is the pending items.

Ref. 18 — The first audit of queries without an index (23 June)

On 23 June I did the first audit of "full scans" (queries that read the whole table because there is no index) on a live system. The findings became pending items, not a filed report. It is the origin of step 13.

Ref. 19 — Lessons from Google's cloud (24 June)

The infrastructure mistakes of the first weeks on Google's cloud were written down as lessons so as not to repeat them. It is the first version of what later became the infrastructure manual of step 20.

Ref. 20 — The user manual is born together with the system (26 June)

On 26 June there was already a user manual, with the system half built. I did it that way on purpose: in classic projects the manual was written at the end, when nobody remembered anything any more; with a model that regenerates it for free, there is no excuse for it not to be born with the system. Step 20.

Ref. 21 — Second-layer backup (13 July)

Before trusting the system with real data I put two layers of backup in place: one local and another in Google's storage. Step 15 and the recovery manual of step 20.

Ref. 22 — PND-300: four rounds until the root cause (14 July)

A bug came back three times until the real cause was found, on 14 July. Each previous "fix" had been on the symptom. Here "the fix is at the root or it is not a fix", which was already in the working agreement, stopped being a phrase and became practice: the hypothesis has to be measured before touching. Steps 1 and 7.

Ref. 23 — Security audit (16 July)

On 16 July I did the first formal security audit, with findings turned into pending items. It was the one that convinced me to treat security as a periodic audit and not as a list that gets ticked once at the beginning. Step 13.

Ref. 24 — Internal manual and disaster recovery manual (26 and 29 July)

On 26 and 29 July (commits 6444ce6, pending item 442, and a30cee3, pending item 459) I left everything operational documented, deploying, recovering, maintaining, in a form "reproducible by hand, without AI". The criterion I used, and which is in step 20, is that if tomorrow there is no agent, a trusted person who does not program can follow the manual.

Ref. 25 — Fail-closed as a reflex (27 July)

On 27 July (commit e18406d, pending item 452): if a table does not exist or a lookup fails, the row simply stays without its button; the whole listing never breaks. On doubt, do not act and do not break. And the test for that correction was proved red "without the try/except", that is, it was demonstrated that without the protection the listing broke. Steps 8 and 14.

Ref. 26 — The counters that did not match: 1,355 versus 1,234 (6 and 7 August)

This is, for me, the most complete example of "verify with an instrument". A counter on the screen showed 1,355 approvals and the query to the database showed 1,234. On 6 and 7 August (commits ab3e529, pending item 517, and 1f5feb9, pending item 514) the root cause was verified directly on production in read-only mode, the difference was decomposed number by number (145 too many, 24 too few; 145 minus 24 is 121), a test was written and proved RED against the old criterion, and the diagnostic script stayed in the repository for the next time. Steps 8, 11 and 7.

Ref. 27 — Producer/consumer symmetry (6 August)

On 6 August (commits 50fe968, pending item 510, and 7251129, pending item 509) I imposed that the "reason for waiting" the user sees be computed with the SAME gates the robot decides with, and that the list of columns of the grid be defined by the server and consumed by the frontend. That way they cannot diverge. It is the rule "every list has a single owner" of step 11.

Ref. 28 — Panic button with a lock, dry-run mode for the approver, watchman with guards (7 August)

On 7 August (commit 0954740, pending item 524) the system's locks were in place: a panic button with a double safety, the auto-approver running first in dry-run mode (it does everything except approve, and logs what it would have approved), and a watchman that pushes what got stuck, with guards so as not to push twice. Step 14.

Ref. 29 — The domain rules written in the commit (10 August)

On 10 August (commit 0ae3ae6, pending item 550) the commit message says, verbatim: "regla Pablo: aprobada + saldo = vuelve" ("Pablo's rule: approved + balance = comes back"), "decisión Pablo 2026-08-10" ("Pablo's decision 2026-08-10"). The business decision stayed with a date and an owner, attached to the code that implements it. Step 6.

C. Kikan: the mature method (August to October 2026)

Ref. 30 — Start with the method already written, and the AUD-* series of audits (21 to 29 August)

Kikan was the first project that started with everything above already written from day one: an operational project file and pending items as the plan from the first commit (21 August). On 28 and 29 August I did a series of early audits (AUD-1, AUD-2…) that turned directly into 19 pending-item documents. Steps 2, 5 and 13.

Ref. 31 — Continuous integration from the first week; deployment as a package (28 August, 23 and 24 September)

The tests ran on GitHub from the first week (ci.yml, 28 August). The deployment arrived on 23 September (deploy.yml, commit bb801ed): a package, with the configuration living on the server and not in the repository, and the runtime version pinned. That the deployment arrived a month after the start is the mistake that motivates step 4.

Ref. 32 — The deployment is hardened blow by blow, one pending item at a time (30 September to 4 October)

Between 30 September and 4 October there were five pending items in a row just to make the deployment work properly: the public URL in both environments (f85c3c9), reloading the service before restarting and before rolling back (513c8d0, pending item 108), a fixture that reconciles instead of duplicating (076df76, pending item 134), the client's material outside the mirrored tree (9d76af8, pending item 130), and the test suite that no longer dirties the configuration that travels in the deployment (0bd20a8, pending item 107, 3 October). If the skeleton had been deployed empty in the first week, those five blows would have been taken with zero functionality at stake. Steps 4 and 15.

Ref. 33 — Promotion from UAT to production (4 and 8 October)

On 4 and 8 October (commits 4eb5ad7, pending item 027, and 7658e2e) an explicit circuit was put in place to move configuration and campaigns from the test environment to the production one. Step 15.

Ref. 34 — The schema derived from the code, with a lock (11 October)

In stage 1 of the persistence re-engineering (commits 6113319 and 68819ce) the inventory of tables and fields was derived from the code itself, with a test that protects it, and it was opened on the shared board so that I could review it. It is a list with a single owner, the code. Step 11.

Ref. 35 — The static ratchet, and the meter that could not see (11 to 13 October)

In stage 6 of the re-engineering (commits 06b2734, f6e5221, 2fc6239, 1d55a93) I put in a "ratchet": a counter of calls to the database without await, that is, without waiting for the result, which can only go down and never up. And something happened that is worth telling: it was discovered TWICE that the meter could not see parts of the code. First 70 calls, then 84, with the counter at zero. If the instrument is not verified, the number reassures and lies. Step 7.

Ref. 36 — The meters that lied and the five rules of section 11.b (13 and 14 October)

Verbatim from the commits of 13 October (880ac0f, f955620): "the E7.5 meter lied on a database with the ALTER already applied"; "it is 44, not 27". And a few days earlier, a plan claimed there were 121 loops of a certain kind and the one who executed measured 79. Out of all this I wrote section 11.b of the re-engineering document, with the five rules that today are step 7: a guarded meter; if the instrument does not exist, it gets built; it is audited in both directions; what is approximate is marked; and the one who executes verifies the numbers of the one who planned. With the sentence that sums it up: "three out of four assertions were wrong; where there was an instrument, exact".

Ref. 37 — Query budget and the ceiling tightened to the measured value (14 October)

In stage 8 (commits 5926b3f, 10ada00) every path of the application was left with a budget of database queries, measured and protected by a test, and the change of database engine was left as a switch per environment. In stage 9 (0730082, 7f5f1af) the ceiling was tightened to the measured value plus a small margin: 63 over 60. The budget is not an invented number. Step 7.

Ref. 38 — Retention per table, and an integrity report made by another session (14 October)

In stages 10 and 12 (commits f72b15a, b266f11) the real package started carrying the migration scripts, data retention was declared per table and bounded in production, and an integrity report made by a different Claude Code session, one that had not built the system, was absorbed as pending items. It is the auditor distinct from the builder of step 13.

Ref. 39 — The deployment only packages, and the quota that got burned (14 October)

The tests were run three times for every commit, between 16 and 27 minutes each time; in 16 days 2,000 minutes of GitHub Actions quota were consumed, and a day of 23 pushes finished burning it. On 14 October (commit 39497b0) I split the circuit: the tests run ONCE only, in continuous integration; the deployment waits for that result and only packages. From 16 to 27 minutes per commit, down to 11. Step 15.

Ref. 40 — Two administrator doors in twenty-four hours (15 October)

On 15 October two administrator doors were created in a single day (pending items 223 and 257). An external review detected it; I withdrew one (commits 69bdab2, 20fdbe7, 1558ee4) and a single way in remained. I tell it as evidence that the method withstands "undo quickly" without drama, and of why the audit has to be done by someone who did not build. Steps 13 and 14.

Ref. 41 — The quality audit: a vulnerability, a huge component, a project file of 9,661 lines (15 October)

The quality audit of 15 October found a known vulnerability in a dependency, a screen component of 2,679 lines, eight modules duplicated between the web version and the mobile one, and a CLAUDE.md that had grown to 9,661 lines and 789 kilobytes, a file that at that point nobody reads, not even the model. From there comes the rule of step 2: the project file is the programme, not the history, and the casebook goes separately. Steps 2, 11 and 13.

Ref. 42 — Secrets pasted in the chat (14 and 16 October)

On 14 October a database key was printed in a transcript, and on 16 October some API keys showed up pasted in the chat. Neither of the two was "seen by anyone", and both have to be rotated all the same, because a secret that has been in a chat, in a log or in a commit is no longer a secret. Step 15.

Ref. 43 — Three reports that were not what they said (14 to 16 October)

Three small and very illustrative cases of step 12. On 14 October an authentication error was reported by the tool with the label "success". On 16 October the server's CORS was derived only from the first domain name, and the configuration assistant of the test environment was giving a 500 error that the report did not mention. And the mobile application lost its tabs under conditions the report declared verified. All three are corrected with the same rule: separate what was verified from what is believed and declare the blind spots.

Ref. 44 — The test suite that died from memory and looked like a failed test

A whole test suite "died" from lack of memory (commit 1d7a20b), and the report read it as a failing test. Until the complete output was looked at, it was not understood that no test had failed: the process had crashed. It is the reason why step 8 asks for the counts of the red and the green, not just the verdict.

D. What I imposed in session, and what I rejected

Ref. 45 — Four corrections I made in session that later became rules (October)

There are rules of the method that did not come from a commit but from corrections I made to the agent in the middle of a session, and which I formalised afterwards. I cite them because they show the second factor of the method's origin, my own engineering judgement against the model's fragile application. "A guarded meter, not grep": a number that feeds a decision is measured with a program that has a test, not with a text search. "Measure the hypothesis, not only the symptom": when I bring a cause in the form of an impression, it has to be measured before building, because I tend to be right about the symptom and the cause tends to be right next to it. "Validate the limits with the log, not with the prose": a refusal by the model does not prove the limit is enforced; you have to force the invocation and read the log. And "compare the published version first": faced with "it is still broken", look at which version is running where I see the problem before diagnosing again. Steps 7 and 12.

Ref. 46 — The projects I rejected, and where the exclusionary criteria come from (17 October)

The two exclusionary criteria of step 0 do not come from the projects I accepted but from the ones I rejected. I rejected legacy technologies without an accessible repository or with repositories that do not follow a standard, because without a repository there is nowhere to leave the evidence. And I rejected technologies that cannot be tested end to end, where the screens cannot be executed and each element of the interface accessed with its different methods while validating that the change reached the backend. I made clear at the time, and I stand by it, that NAC3 is not mandatory: what is mandatory is testing from the execution of the screens.
