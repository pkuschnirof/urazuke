1. What is missing today, and why

Step 9 already measures the code, and it does so with a gate that lets nothing through: a single formatter, size limits (three hundred lines per file, forty per function), complexity per function, duplicated code, dead code, dependencies declared and used, coverage with a ratchet and mutation testing where there are real users. The number is written in docs/CODE_QUALITY.md. That is solved.

What is not solved is the other half of the product. I checked the corpus of steps: there is not a single mention of usability, of people/profiles or of functional grouping. The interface holds today on two things: the screenshot the owner looks at for ten seconds and the manual test cases. Both are good and both are human, expensive and forgetful. A system can pass the quality gate with honours and be unusable: everything green, and the person cannot find where to charge.

And there is a second-layer problem that is technical but shows up in the interface: when the same product is used by several profiles (administration, operations, queries, customer), the functionalities are enabled in three different places - the database, the menu and the route guard - which are written at different moments and drift out of sync on their own. The symptom is always the same: someone sees a button that is not theirs, or worse, someone who does have the permission cannot find the screen.

The two heuristics that follow attack those two things with the same form: a short catalogue of verifiable statements, a few instruments that measure them on their own, and a declared exception path for when the project needs to step out.

2. The model: three layers, one single form of rule

The temptation is to make a list of rules and tie it to the stack of the first project. That ages badly: the React rule does not apply in Angular, the Angular one does not apply in a server with templates, and in two years half the list is dead. The proposal separates three layers.

Layer 1 - Agnostic core (not negotiable). Statements about outcomes, not about tools. "There is a single formatter and nobody argues with it" applies equally in TypeScript, PHP or Go. The core is enumerated with stable identifiers and does not change from project to project.

Layer 2 - Adapter per stack (configurable). For each rule of the core, each frontend family declares with what it is measured and with what threshold. A React project with Vite, a Next one, an Angular one, a server with templates, a mobile one and a command-line one are six different adapters of the same core. Everything the user asked for as "configurable per frontend" lives here.

Layer 3 - Project decisions (declared). The concrete thresholds and the exemptions. They are not invented: they are asked of the owner and they stay written, just like the sizing of the architecture step. If a threshold is not declared, the adapter's applies and it is recorded as such.

Every rule is always written with the same six-field sheet:

FieldWhat it contains
idStable identifier (CQ-07, UX-12). Never reused.
StatementA single sentence, in the imperative, about an observable outcome.
WhyThe concrete damage it avoids. If there is no damage, the rule is superfluous.
How it is verifiedThe instrument: automatic gate, matrix query, end-to-end test or manual case.
SeverityBLOCKS (the gate goes red and the block does not close), WARNS (opens a pending item), RECORDS (only stays in the document).
Applies toProfiles where it rules. Outside them it does not run, and that is not an exception: it is scope.

The exception is declared, never omitted. A rule is switched off with a four-field record: identifier, reason in one line, who authorises it (the owner, not the agent) and review date. A rule switched off without a record is a failure of the gate, not a decision. It is the same criterion the method already uses to refuse a limit raised "in order to pass".

A rule can have faces, and one single face is switched off (D-078). A face is a cut of the same statement with an identifier of its own -- CQ-19.1, CQ-19.2 --, append-only like the ones of the rules and never recycled. They exist for a mechanical reason and not a drafting one: without an identifier a single one cannot be switched off, and a rule that can only be switched off whole pushes towards switching off too much. The switch-off record is the one above, with the identifier of a face in the place of the identifier of a rule, and a record whose identifier resolves neither to a rule nor to a face is a dangling switch-off, which is red. Three things here are not negotiable. A rule with any face switched off is never reported as fulfilled: the report says "CQ-19: three of four faces, one switched off with its reason" and never a percentage alone, which is the criterion of the observable switch-off applied inside a rule. The count of faces is derived from the data and not written, like any figure that lives next to the name of a collection. And the scoreboard of section 12 counts faces and not rules for a rule that has them, or the percentage lies. Today CQ-18 has five checks with no identifier, so it is the only rule with faces that cannot be addressed: giving them identifiers is adding and not renaming, and it is separate work.

3. The four instruments

No heuristic is worth anything if its verification is an opinion. There are four, and every rule declares which one it belongs to.

  1. Automatic gate. It runs on every push and at block close. It returns green or red. It is cheap, it is repeatable and it does not argue. It measures everything that can be counted.
  2. Matrix query. The functionality-by-profile matrix (see B2) is a declarative artifact; the menu, the routing and the permissions are derived from it. Checking the matrix is checking the interface. It is the piece that turns part of usability into something a machine can verify.
  3. End-to-end test per profile. For each declared profile, a script that enters as that profile and compares the set of reachable functionalities against the row of the matrix: neither less nor more. It is the test that catches the two typical desynchronisations (the button that is left over and the screen that is missing).
  4. Manual case. What no machine judges: whether the grouping is understood, whether the task is completed without help, whether the text is in the user's language. It is run with people and it is recorded, as step 10 already mandates.

Rules CQ-12 to CQ-18 are the ones that make "code quality" mean something on the front end. Without them, the project can have ninety per cent mutation and a screen that computes the total with its own arithmetic inside a component: the worst of both worlds, because the duplicated calculation passes every backend test.

The two rules of the receipt, and where they came from (D-078). CQ-19 and CQ-20 entered on 2026-11-01 and they did not come from an idea: they came from eleven failures measured in an experiment that was measuring something else. All of them had the same shape -- an instrument that could not look and reported it the same way as health, or that looked at less than it believed it had looked at -- and they appeared through eleven different paths, which makes it very unlikely that this is the defect of one program. What makes them two rules and not eleven is that the same question can be asked of all of them: what is the denominator of this figure, and what does this instrument report when it could not look? CQ-19 is about what an instrument reports; CQ-20 is about the privilege it runs with, which is part of the verification and not an operational detail. Each one enters with its faces, which is the shape section 2 describes.

#### The four faces of CQ-19

idFaceStatement
CQ-19.1The denominatorEvery measurement that walks a set declares how many elements it expected to walk next to how many it walked, and fails closed when it walked fewer. Telling the zero that means "there are none" from the zero that means "I could not look" is not enough: what has to be told apart is the "fewer because I did not look at all of it", which is the one that reads like health.
CQ-19.2IndependenceNo instrument depends on the resource it measures. When that cannot be avoided, its report declares what it depends on, and the failure of that dependency is reported as "I could not look" and never as health.
CQ-19.3The universe, in both directionsEvery filter, search or selection declares the universe it ran over: "I found nothing" and "I did not run" are two different results and are not written the same way, and a filter that returned too much was not applied either. A figure that decides something is measured twice, in two different ways, before it is used.
CQ-19.4The lax siblingWhen a check has a counterpart in the chain, either they are the same instrument with the same configuration or the difference is declared: what each one measures, which one rules, and what can pass one and fail the other. A local check more permissive than the one that decides is a green that lies.

How it differs from the receipt of step 8. They are the same concept over different objects, and that is why they carry the same name: the receipt of step 8 is an artifact a plan produces -- the red with its exact command, its version, its numbers and its exit code, pasted as it came out --, and the receipt of the instrument is the condition a figure brings alongside it in order to be readable at all. One is demanded once per lock; the other, every time a figure decides something. Said the other way round, which is how it is checked without thinking: if what you have in front of you is a test, ask for the receipt of step 8; if it is a figure, ask for the one of CQ-19.

#### The two faces of CQ-20

idFaceStatement
CQ-20.1The undeclared effectA command presented as read-only runs without permission to write, and the privilege it ran with is declared next to its result. An instrument that writes while saying it does not leaves the owner deciding over a state the instrument itself changed.
CQ-20.2The probe that is only harmless when it failsA probe that exercises a destructive operation in order to check that it is blocked runs where its statement cannot land, and its report proves the state was left identical by counting it before and after. A probe whose harm depends on the block working does not verify the block: it bets on it.

The pairing of CQ-20 with the red routine of step 8 reads on its own: the red first demands that a test can detect; CQ-20 demands that on detecting it does not break.

The rule of reach, and why it is one and not three (D-087). CQ-21 entered on 2026-11-03 and it did not come from an idea either: it came from three defects measured in the same experiment, in its second batch, and all three had a shape that fits in one line -- a correct artefact that never reaches the one place where it has an effect. The test case that was written and nobody executed. The corrected case that nobody ran again. And the doubled port that no test ever crossed, which is the one worth half the price: one arm of the experiment built its view on a port with its double, which is what the method pushes you to do so that the view is verifiable, and that port listened for a single kind of event while four of the five controls emitted another one. Four dead controls in a browser, with eighty-one test files green on top of them, and among those one that asserted exactly what was broken, green because it walked the listeners of the fake node and called them by hand. In those eighty-one files there was not a single button press. What makes them one rule and not three is that the same question can be asked of all of them: how far did this run reach, and which part of the artefact was left uncrossed?

And the wording has to be credited, because it is not mine. Mine asked for "at least one test that crosses it for real, against the real implementation", and the arm accepted it and showed with its own code that it is not enough: one of its tests already ran the real implementation, against a fake document that invoked the listener without looking at the event. It crossed the real adapter and still never saw the defect. The wording that stayed is theirs, and it is the one in CQ-21.1: the other side has to be the real other side. The cost was measured by them too, which is the objection this face is going to get: one run, fifteen point six seconds, zero new packages.

#### The four faces of CQ-21

idFaceStatement
CQ-21.1The real other sideEvery port with a test double has at least one run that crosses it to the real other side, and the other side is whatever the port is doubling: if what is there is a browser, an operating system, a database or the network, there is no way to satisfy this without it, and no quantity of faithful doubles replaces it.
CQ-21.2Written is not runA verification artefact nobody ran counts zero and not half a verification, and its figure travels to the report where the decision is made and not only to the file where the artefact lives: a figure somebody has to go and look for stops nobody.
CQ-21.3The case comes back wholeAn execution reports which step it reached, and the steps it did not reach neither passed nor failed: when what failed is fixed, the case runs again whole and leaves a new execution row later than the fix, and a correction does not close with the verification of the step that failed.
CQ-21.4The red reached its assertionA red proves what it says it proves only if the run got as far as the assertion: a red that is an exception -- the mutated code does not compile, the file does not parse, the environment did not start -- falsified nothing, because the assertion neither passed nor failed, and declaring a mutation caught on that red is giving a verdict about a step the run never reached.

How it differs from CQ-19.1, which is the question the first reader is going to ask. They are two axes and not the same thing said twice. CQ-19.1 looks at the width: of the elements an instrument expected to walk, how many it walked. CQ-21 looks at the depth: how far down the layers, or how far along the steps, one run reached. And the port case separates them on its own: the eighty-one files ran all eighty-one, so the denominator was perfect and the depth was zero. A figure can have its denominator complete and never have touched the layer where the defect lives. Said the other way round, which is how it is checked without thinking: if the doubt is how many out of how many, it is CQ-19.1; if the doubt is how far, it is CQ-21.

And what this rule does not promise, said here and not when it fails. No gate can verify that the run a project declares crosses the port for real: what a machine looks at is the declaration -- that the port is in the inventory of step 11, with its adapter, its double and the run that crosses it naming what is on the other side -- and what decides whether that is true is a person reading the command. That is all the gate there is, and it is more than there was: until 2026-11-03 a project with a doubled port and no run crossing it came out exactly the same as one that had it, and I measured that with the real evaluator over two trees that differed in nothing else. The same holds for CQ-21.3: that the new row covers all the steps is said by whoever executed, not by a program. The figure is mechanical; the truth of the figure belongs to the owner.

And the fourth face arrived later, on 2026-11-07, with D-089. CQ-21.4 was not there the day the rule entered, and it was brought in by a defect found by a gate and not by a person: the mutation-anchor verifier of one arm of the experiment found a mutation declared caught because the test had gone red, and the test had gone red on a type error and not on a failed assertion -- the mutated code blew up before reaching the assertion. The red was real and it did not prove what it said it proved. The same day, in another system, a guard planted a mutant with the line endings of the other operating system, the runner did not parse the file, and the receipt blamed the mutant. It comes in as a face of this rule and not as a rule of its own because the question that detects it is the question of CQ-21 word for word: the run did not get as far as the assertion, so the assertion is a step the run did not reach, and the statement of the rule already says that a step no execution reached neither passed nor failed. The mechanical question, which is asked without tools: is the red a failed assertion, or is it an exception? It is, besides, the exact counterpart of the red routine of step 8: the red first demands that a test can detect, CQ-20 that on detecting it does no harm, and CQ-21.4 that the red put forward as proof got as far as the place where it proves something.

The three rules of the third batch, and why they are three and not ten (D-089). CQ-22, CQ-23 and CQ-24 entered on 2026-11-07 and they come from ten findings: nine that arm C1 of the A/B experiment left behind on closing the twenty-four steps -- twenty-two of twenty-four with no override, which is the correct figure -- and one that did not come from the arm but from an analysis of tooling platforms. The owner brought them with one line, and that line is what turns almost all of them into a single family: do not accept "the measurement exists" as a criterion. And the family splits into three by asking each finding what it is that nobody looks at. What is this figure contrasted against, and who decided? is CQ-22. Is this verdict about this work, and does it still exist? is CQ-23. Can this register answer the question it exists for, over the whole of its stretch? is CQ-24. All three questions can be asked by hand and all three have a falsifiable answer; that is why they are rules and not warnings.

CQ-22, the contrast, and the case that measured it. Step 19 of the arm -- performance tests at volume and breaking point -- closed green with its non-functional requirement unmet: p95 from 557 to 911 milliseconds against a declared ceiling of 300, at two hundred concurrent users, with the connection pool of one module saturated from twenty people on. And the gate that let it through is right about what it measures: it verifies that the receipt exists, that it covers the four flows and that it carries its thresholds or the written impossibility. Words of the arm, which is who found it: "no gate of the ladder asks whether the figure passes". That is what this rule closes, and the consequence has to be said whole because it is a big one: until 2026-11-07 a ladder of twenty-four steps could be closed complete, with every one of its non-functional requirements unmet, and every gate right one by one.

The rule enters with four faces and not one, and each one comes from a different half of the same hole. CQ-22.2 is the structural half and it is the one the owner asked for explicitly: the requirement of the case said "p95 < 300 ms" and did not say at what concurrency, so the same system passes at twenty people and fails at two hundred, and a threshold without its condition cannot be contrasted against anything. CQ-22.3 is the worst case, one step higher up: the infrastructure and recovery manual of the arm, four hundred and nine lines written "for three in the morning", declares in its section 6, verbatim, "There are no backups. None. Of anything.", with the verification command transcribed -- and step 20 closed green, because the gate verifies that the manual exists and declares what it does not know, not that the backup exists. That difference is what this face marks and it is not negotiable: for the p95 the written impossibility is a legitimate exit, because there are systems where load cannot be measured; "there are no backups", written with all the honesty in the world, cannot close a step. A p95 that does not pass is a slow system and it is discovered through angry users; a backup that does not exist is a system there is no coming back from, and it is discovered the day it is needed, which is the worst moment. And CQ-22.4 is the face the tenth finding asked for with its own caveat written: if the demand ends up being "that an inventory exists", it is born with the defect this whole batch came in to fix.

CQ-23, provenance, and the measurement that orders it. Three of the ten findings are the same verdict looked at from three sides. The first: a gate that is not blind and does not run. A commit went in with a rule red because the chain had been run before the document that broke it was written, and the tree was twenty-seven commits ahead, so the hook that looks at the tree that really stays did not run either time. The green was true and old. The second: two runs of the same chain in the same project with opposite results, and the difference was one single thing -- one landed in a record on disk, a hundred and ten lines with thirty-seven gates and four red, and the other travelled inside the response and was lost whole. The third: a step green and not done, whose two checks were satisfied by the same two files of the previous step, with the pending item of the step at zero entries of work; no gate detected it and the arm stopped it, refusing to accept its own green.

Of the three, the wording of CQ-23.3 is the arm's and it has to be credited: "the question that detects it is a single one and it can be asked by hand: which file of this list did not exist before starting? If the answer is none, the green belongs to another one." Mine would have talked about "artefacts of the step itself" and would not have given the reader a question they can ask without tools. It is the second time this happens -- CQ-21.1 is theirs too -- and it is worth writing down.

CQ-24, the register, and the two independent defects that turned it into a family. The headline of the experiment was "twenty-four steps closed, zero forced closes", reported three times on the same day and written into three documents. The arm went to verify it before copying it, and over the hundred and ninety-five items of its register it measured this: zero with the forced-close field written, twenty-eight that name the word in their prose, ten closes the prose declares forced by the owner under nine authorisations, and of those two that are tasks of the ladder -- both with the close reason at "shipped" and no forced-close field at all. The field exists for one exact moment, step 23, and its own schema says it is mandatory "so the retrospective can count forced closes per motive"; in the only project that ever got that far it was never written, and the count had to be made by reading prose, which is precisely what the mechanism came in to avoid. There is a corollary that stands on its own: the list of nine authorisations of the retrospective omitted one, because it was built with a search for the word "override" and the prose of that item does not use it -- a recount by word over free prose is an instrument that cannot guarantee its own population, which is CQ-19.3.

What turned it into a rule rather than a fix was the second occurrence, independent, measured on the same day as the batch and in another system written by another author: the start-up program of a panel wrote into its append-only record a motive field that was constant -- "start of series 2: the old panel had 1.23.5 in memory" -- true of the first start and false of the three that followed, which were archived forever with a motive that was not theirs. The field existed, it was filled on every row, and it lied. Two independent occurrences of the same pattern are a family and not an oversight, so CQ-24.1 is worded for the family -- a mandatory field whose value is not derived from the fact it says it records -- and not for the field that brought it in.

CQ-24.2 is the other finding and it is cheaper to fix than it looks. The arm measured it while writing its retrospective: the twelve first anti-patterns of its register went in in one single commit at step 11 and they describe steps 00 to 11, of which steps 00 to 04 report zero incidents. Their words: "your question -- which step does each incident fall in -- cannot be answered properly for the first fifth of the route, which is where the decisions most expensive to undo are taken." The owner offered two exits -- that the registers be born at step 01, or that retroactive rows be marked as such -- and the face takes both in one, in that order, because the first prevents the problem at the root and the second is the one a legacy system can satisfy when the register was already born late. And it is said here that the first alone is not enough: a register that exists from step 01 and that nobody writes to reports zero just the same.

And what these three rules do not promise, said here and not when they fail. No gate can verify that the figure a project declares is the one it measured, nor that the condition a threshold declares is the one that governs the requirement, nor that a retroactive row was marked honestly. What a machine looks at is the declaration -- the verdict next to the figure, the condition next to the threshold, the denominator of an inventory and the decision of each entry, the date of a verdict against that of the last change, the step at which a register was born -- and the truth of the declaration is decided by a person reading. It is the same border CQ-21 wrote into its own section and it is worth repeating: the figure is mechanical, the truth of the figure belongs to the owner. What changes with respect to not having the rule is that before, silence and health were written the same way.

#### The four faces of CQ-22

idFaceStatement
CQ-22.1The figure against its thresholdEvery figure a declared requirement governs arrives with its threshold next to it and with its verdict -- it passes or it does not -- and the one that does not pass is red and not "measured". The written impossibility still counts as an exit; silence does not.
CQ-22.2The threshold with its conditionA threshold declares the condition under which it holds -- at what volume, at what concurrency, in which environment, over what data -- and a threshold without its condition is reported as undeclared: the same system passes at twenty people and fails at two hundred, so without the condition there is nothing to contrast against. The condition is a decision of the owner and is never assumed.
CQ-22.3A declared absence does not closeA safeguard the method requires and that does not exist does not close its step by being honestly declared. The difference from the written impossibility is mechanical: if what is missing is the figure, honesty is enough and the measurement stays pending; if what is missing is the safeguard, no figure is missing -- the way back is -- and that is a red with its prerequisite project.
CQ-22.4Every entry with its decisionAn inventory the method requires closes with a decision per entry -- accepted, narrowed or rejected, with who and when -- and not with the list existing. A complete inventory whose entries nobody resolved is the same measurement nobody contrasted, in another shape.

#### The three faces of CQ-23

idFaceStatement
CQ-23.1Later than the last changeA verdict is later than the last change that affects what it measures, and it ran over the tree that stays. A green that is true and old reads exactly like a current one; and a gate that looks at the definitive tree and only runs on publishing leaves a green with an expiry date that nobody revalidates.
CQ-23.2The output outlives the runEvery verification that can last longer than the turn that launched it leaves its verdict where the next turn can read it. CQ-23.3The artefact belongs to this workOf the artefacts a step closes with, at least one was born in it, and the question that detects this is asked by hand and without tools: which file of this list did not exist before starting? 5. Part B - Usability

Six families. The first and the second are the ones the owner explicitly asked for; the next three exist because without them the grouping and the profiles degrade just the same, more slowly. The sixth was born later, on 2026-10-27 (D-070), and it is of another kind: the first five are about the screen the system shows a person, and the sixth is about the conversation in which the system gets built.

B1 - Grouping by functional convergence

The mother rule: a functionality is grouped by what the person came to do, not by the module it came out of. The code is organised by entities ("Invoice", "Customer", "Payment"); people are not. The three depth levels, and where they come from. 6. Where it hooks, and what artifacts it produces

The annex does not invent new steps: it hangs from the ones that exist. That avoids duplicating gates and avoids the usual fate of usability lists, which is to be read once.

Where it goes (D-043). It is transversal to the four stretches, with the main weight on the Construction Loop (steps 5 to 12), which is where things are decided and built. It is not a step of its own: a numbered step would force the ladder to be renumbered, would alter the step count and would subject the annex to the mandatory translation of the contracts into the eight languages before the construction could pass green, without adding any gate the loop does not already have.

How it hooks (D-044). One canonical source, five anchors and two previous entries. The text of the rules lives only here and the prompts cite it; they never copy it. Repeating the rules inside the eight prompts of the loop dilutes them (they compete with the work of each step and the agent obeys them differently each time), drifts them (two copies of the same text drift apart on their own, which is the antipattern step 11 pursues) and does not make them more transversal: what makes a rule transversal is that every block passes through the same gate, not that the rule is written everywhere.

The only rule that does enter the prompt, and why (D-070). UX-28 is the written exception to the above, and it is worth saying how it differs from the rest: those are demands on the system being built, and the gate measures them over artifacts the project leaves written; UX-28 is a demand on the conversation in which it gets built, and the only way for the model to obey it is for it to be in what the model reads. It enters narrowly -- it names the four parts and the parameter, and nothing else -- in a single English wording identical across the eight languages, which is the same rule that already governs every prompt; the full statement still lives once, here. What this exception does not authorize is copying any of the others: the argument of D-044 stands whole for everything a gate can look at.

8. The two pieces, in the form the method uses

Code quality.

Set up and maintain the code-quality gate for this project, configured for its frontend family. The core rules are the same in every project; what changes is how each one is measured and at which threshold. Install one formatter and one linter with a single shared configuration; enforce size limits per file and per function, complexity per function, duplication, dead code, declared-and-used dependencies, type checking where the language allows it, coverage with a ratchet, and secret scanning. Where the project has real users, add mutation testing scoped to the changed lines and record the score per block. Then extend the gate to the frontend itself: no business rule inside a screen, one typed client for every server call, one home for shared state, no user-visible string outside the translation catalogue, a stable identifier and role on every interactive element, styling only through tokens, and automatic basic accessibility checks. Every rule has a stable id, a severity (blocks, warns, records) and a named verifier. Thresholds and exceptions are declared by the owner in one configuration file; a rule switched off without a written exception is a gate failure, not a decision. Report the result per rule in docs/CODE_QUALITY.md, not as a single number.

When you need a decision from me, ask it with the four parts of UX-28 (docs/ANEXO_HEURISTICAS.md) and in this order: the data that makes the question necessary, listed; one paragraph of at most five lines that links that data and lets the question be seen; the question itself with its possible answers numbered; and your recommendation. Say it at the technical depth the project declared in communication_depth -- beginner, decision_maker or technical; if the project declared none, use decision_maker.

Usability.

Build the usability criteria for this project before writing the first screen, and enforce them where the method already has hooks. Group functionality by what the person came to do, never by the internal module or the data entity: every menu group is named with a user job in the project glossary, every functionality has exactly one canonical home, no functionality is orphaned, and every group is reachable in no more than three steps from the entry screen. Then declare accessibility in one matrix and derive everything from it: each functionality lists the profiles that have it, each functionality has at least one profile, each profile has at least one functionality and its own entry point, the navigation is generated from the matrix instead of written by hand, and every route checks the permission on the server because hiding an entry is not protecting it. Verify the matrix per profile with an end-to-end check that compares what is reachable with the matrix row: neither less nor more. Require the four states on every list (with data, empty, loading, error), a visible working indicator with the option to cancel long operations, an error message that says what happened and what to do without losing entered data, a primary action per screen, confirmation or undo for destructive actions, and a form that keeps what was typed. Run each manual test case once per affected profile, run the first-click check at the close of every block, and require a real screenshot of the test environment on every item that touches a screen. Record the result per rule, with owner-declared exceptions, in docs/USABILITY.md, and keep the matrix in docs/PROFILE_MATRIX.md as the single source of truth.

When you need a decision from me, ask it with the four parts of UX-28 (docs/ANEXO_HEURISTICAS.md) and in this order: the data that makes the question necessary, listed; one paragraph of at most five lines that links that data and lets the question be seen; the question itself with its possible answers numbered; and your recommendation. Say it at the technical depth the project declared in communication_depth -- beginner, decision_maker or technical; if the project declared none, use decision_maker.

9. What this proposal does not do

  • It does not add a step. They are transversal rules -- of code quality and of usability --, not a new stage. They hang from the steps that already exist. How many there are is not written in this prose: the published data declares it (heuristics.counts), which is the only copy that cannot go stale (D-090).
  • It does not invent a score. A number of "usability 78" does not say what to fix and gets optimised in the wrong direction. It is reported per rule. Section 12 proposes two derived figures, but they are a count of rules with their entry - not a quality or usability grade - and they do not authorise closing anything.
  • It does not replace the owner. The first-click test, the screenshot and the task test remain human: they are the only instrument that distinguishes "it works" from "it serves".
  • It brings no factory thresholds. Those of the adapter are a declared starting point; the project that does not declare its own inherits them and it is recorded as such.
  • It does not promise that the gate detects bad grouping. A machine can verify that there is no duplication and no orphans; if the group is called "Management", a person has to see it.

A violation is planted of a rule that warnsGreen, and a pending item opens that names the rule and the file: warning is not approving.
A violation is planted of a rule that blocksRed, with rule, file and remedy named. And there is no dashboard: the average does not close blocks.
Dashboard: a rule is switched off with a recordCompliance may go up, but the gate coverage goes down and the figure is published with the entry that explains it. With a single figure this scenario would go unnoticed; it is the proof that the two figures exist for a reason (D-047).
Dashboard: two runs with different regulationsBoth figures are shown with their fingerprint (catalogue version, active and switched-off rules, adapter, tool versions) and they are not plotted as a continuous series: they are two different yards and saying so is part of the result.
Dashboard: invalid regulation (rule switched off without a record)It emits no figures. The output is red, not a dashboard with an asterisk.
Dashboard: high compliance with a rule that blocks in redThe block does not close. The dashboard averages; the green is decided by each rule, one by one.
Dashboard: the percentage improves by touching only the automatic partThe percentage goes up and the human verification line does not move; it is reported separately and the report does not add the two things into a single number.

What is looked at is not only the colour: it is looked at that the two switching-off lines exist in the report. A green result without that line is the only failure this test cannot detect on its own by colour, and that is why it is verified by reading the output.

12. The compliance dashboard (DECIDED: two figures, D-047)

Status. Decided on 2026-10-22 in D-047: the dashboard goes with two figures, with the fingerprint of the regulation, with a ratchet, with the human part outside the percentage and with the entry mandatory. It does not authorise closing blocks. What follows is the specification that the implementation (PND-002) has to comply with.

What it is. A deterministic derivative of the per-rule result that the gate already emitted. It measures nothing new and judges nothing new: it counts. If the dashboard could change the verdict of a rule there would be two sources of truth for the same thing, which is exactly what the annex fights in UX-10 (navigation is derived from the matrix) and in CQ-14 (a single place for state).

What it is not. It is not an acceptance criterion. No figure authorises closing a block: the green or the red is decided by each rule that blocks, one by one. The dashboard describes the trend and points at what to look at. If it starts being used as a closing threshold, it stops serving, because the shortest path to raise a number is to switch rules off.

Two figures, never one.

FigureFormulaWhat it measuresHow it moves
Compliancerules that pass / active ruleshow clean what is being looked at isgoes up when it is fixed
Gate coverageactive rules / rules declared in the cataloguehow much of the annex is really watchinggoes down when a rule is switched off

The reason there are two: switching a rule off raises compliance and lowers coverage. With a single figure switching off is free and the dashboard rewards blindness. With two figures that move in opposite directions, the shortcut disappears: to show a better dashboard the code has to be fixed, not the rules switched off. And coverage cannot be raised without measuring.

The fingerprint of the regulation. Every record keeps with which yard it was calculated: catalogue version, active and switched-off rules, frontend adapter and tool versions. Without that, an 82 from yesterday and a 91 from today are not the same measurement even if they are written the same: they are two different regulations and the series is false. Two figures without a fingerprint produce an illusion of progress, which is worse than having no dashboard. If the fingerprint changes, the record is marked as not comparable or the history is recalculated with the current yard; it is never plotted together without saying so.

Ratchet. Compliance does not go down from one block to the next without a recorded decision that explains it. It is the same mechanism that test coverage already has in CQ-08: today's number is tomorrow's floor. Going down is not forbidden; going down without a record is.

What does not enter the count. What only a person can judge - the first-click test (UX-07), the task test (UX-06), the screenshot (UX-27), the form of the question (UX-28) and all the manual cases - is recorded as passed or failed, per block, with name and date, and is reported separately as human verification coverage. It does not enter the compliance percentage. If they are mixed, the result is a number that is sixty per cent measurement and forty per cent opinion, and nobody can say which is which. That is why section 9 still holds: the dashboard does not invent a usability grade, it counts rules.

How it is falsified. It extends the acceptance test of section 11:

ScenarioRequired result
A violation is planted of a rule that blocksCompliance goes down by exactly one rule out of the active ones, and the red appears with the rule named.
A rule is switched off with a recordCompliance may go up; coverage goes down by one rule out of the declared ones; the report names the rule, the reason and the review date.
It is switched off without a recordRed, and no dashboard is emitted: an invalid regulation produces no figures.
Same tree and same configuration, two runsThe same figure, identical.
The configuration changes between two blocksBoth records are marked as not comparable, or the history is recalculated with the current yard.
The dashboard is used as a closing criterionMisuse, not a valid scenario: the green is decided by the rule, not by the average.

Where it appears. In the honest report of step 12 (anchor 5), and in this order: first the binary verdict of the gate, then the two figures, then what is switched off and until when, then the movement against the previous block with its named cause. Always with the entry: it went down four points: CQ-15, three strings outside the catalogue on the charging screen. A figure without an entry is noise; the entry is what makes it actionable for an owner who does not read code.

Declared risk. The dashboard is going to be read by the owner, who does not read code, and that is why they will want it to go up. The number becomes the objective, and the objective displaces the purpose. The defences are the ones above, and there are five: two figures that contradict each other when switching off, the fingerprint that prevents comparing different yards, the ratchet that does not allow going down without a record, the human part outside the percentage and the mandatory entry, with the gate that is not negotiated by average. If any of the five falls, the dashboard is switched off: a dashboard badly used does more damage than not having one.
