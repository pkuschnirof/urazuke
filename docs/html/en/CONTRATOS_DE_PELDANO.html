One file per step. They all have the same skeleton, and they have it on purpose, so that you can compare them and so that, over time, the reflex of reading them becomes automatic: the header with the stretch, the when, the form and the size (mandatory or proportional); what it is for; (1) the required context, named generically; (2) the prompts, in English, each one preceded by a "Before sending the prompt" block (the files and the inputs you have to have made yourself before pasting it, with their content and their minimum state) and followed by the explanation of what I am asking the model for and why each clause is there; (3) the expected result, with its characteristics; (4) the completeness check, which you do without reading code; (5) the warning signs, the symptoms of a badly climbed step.

The English prompts are a single piece, identical in the eight versions of the document; node build/build.mjs extracts them to prompts/ and verifies that no translation has altered them. In the Spanish version each prompt carries its translation alongside, so that the reader can understand it; here the document is already in English, and the explanation under each prompt says what I am asking for and why. What gets pasted to the model is always the English.

I wrote them from what I did in three systems that are in production today; I have not executed them as-is yet. The first project that uses them end to end will correct several of them; that is part of step 23. When a contract cites a real case, it does so with a [Ref. N] mark that links to the references document; the procedure stays clean and the detail is one click away.

Step 0 — Can this project be run this way? (step contract)

Stretch: start-up. When: once only, before opening the repository and before anything else. Form: conversation: the agent evaluates and asks; you decide. Size: mandatory in every project, however small. Status: the exclusionary criteria X1 and X2 are closed; the weighted criteria (D1 to D10) I am still calibrating, and I mark them as hypotheses.

What it is for

Do not get into a project where the method cannot work. If the agent is not going to be able to verify itself end to end, or if there is no domain owner who decides, the method degrades into "programming with AI" and you end up reading code. This step is the entrance door: it is crossed once, and if it cannot be crossed, better to know it before than after.

And a background idea I want you to be clear about: the method does not work for every project, and that is not a defect. A method that promises to work for everything is saying nothing. What this step does is put the boundary in writing, so that the decision to go in or not is a decision and not a hope. The two exclusionary criteria come from projects I turned down, not from the ones I accepted Ref. 46.

1. Required context

What follows is the generic list. The concrete files you have to have made before each prompt, with their content and their minimum state, are in section 2, in the "Before sending the prompt" block that precedes each one.

Named generically; the agent evaluates whether it is enough and asks for what is missing.

  • Survey of the request: what the client wants, for whom, and what happens today without the system.
  • Technology survey: what technology exists or is proposed, whether there is a previous system, whether that system has a repository and how it is managed.
  • Testability survey: whether the screens can be driven by a program and whether every element of the interface is accessible with its different methods (click, type, read its state), so that a test can walk the screen and verify the effect in the backend.
  • Who the domain owner is and how much time they have to decide.
  • What consequence an error has (money, health, operations, nothing).
  • Environments and access: whether there will be a test environment, whether the agent can reach it, whether production can be queried read-only.

2. Prompts

The prompts are in English, which is the language in which models follow instructions with the most precision and the fewest tokens. In the Spanish version each prompt carries its translation; here the document is already in English. Below each prompt is the explanation of what I am asking for and why each clause is there. What you paste to the model is the English.

Opening:

Before sending the prompt, this has to exist, and you (or the business with you) make it, not the agent:

  • docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md: the initial survey. You write it, by hand, after talking to the client (it is the result of the phase 0 intake). Half a page to one page, in prose, not as a form, with six things: (1) the request: what the client wants, in their own words, for whom, and what happens today without the system (the notebook, the spreadsheet, the person who does it by hand); (2) the technology: what exists or what is proposed, whether there is a previous system, whether that system has a repository and how it is managed (branches, who pushes, whether there are tests); (3) testability: whether the screens can be driven by a program, and whether every element of the interface can be found, clicked, typed into and read; if you do not know, say so; (4) the domain owner: who it is, with name and role, and how much time they have per week to decide; (5) the consequence of an error: money, health, operations, nothing; (6) the environments: whether there will be a test one, whether the agent will be able to reach it, whether there is a production that can be queried read-only. What you do not know is written down as "I do not know": do not fill it in yourself so that the agent does not ask, because the point of the step is to see what it asks.
  • docs/ELIGIBILITY_CRITERIA.md: the criteria sheet. You copy it from the main document (the step 0 sheet: the two exclusionary criteria X1 and X2 and the criteria D1 to D10 that weigh), with one empty column for the answer, another for the source and another for green or red. Do not fill it in yourself: the agent fills it in.
  • The project folder, created, with the agent opened there and still nothing in it other than those two files inside docs/.
  • Minimum acceptable state: both files exist, the survey touches the six things even if several of them say "I do not know", and the agent is standing in the folder.

We are going to assess whether this project can be run with the Urazuke method. Read the initial survey in docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md and the criteria sheet in docs/ELIGIBILITY_CRITERIA.md. First, evaluate whether the survey is sufficient to answer every criterion. If any criterion cannot be answered from the survey, ask me for the missing information before going on; do not assume. Then fill in the sheet criterion by criterion: the answer, where it comes from, and for each exclusionary criterion whether it is green or red. Do not decide: present me the completed sheet with your reading and your doubts, and I will decide. Reply in my language.

What I am asking for: that it read the two inputs (the survey and the criteria sheet); that before answering it evaluate whether the survey is enough and, if it is not, ask instead of assuming (this is the central clause of every prompt in the method); that it fill in the sheet with the source of each answer, so that it can be traced later; that it mark the exclusionary criteria green or red; and that it not decide, because eligibility is decided by me. The last line asks it to reply in my language, because the prompt is in English but the conversation does not have to be.

Closing:

Before sending the prompt, you have to have this at hand:

  • Your decision, in one line, taken after reading the complete sheet the agent presented: go, no go, or go under conditions. If there are conditions, write them before pasting the prompt, each one verifiable ("read access to production before step 3" yes; "that the client collaborates" no), and no more than three.
  • The answers you gave to the agent's questions, given in this same chat session: the prompt asks it to include them in the final sheet, and if the session is another one, it does not have them.
  • Minimum acceptable state: every criterion in the sheet has an answer and a source in sight, and you have already decided. If you are still in doubt, do not send the closing: keep asking.

Decision: [go / no-go / go under these conditions: …]. Write the final sheet to docs/ELIGIBILITY.md with the date, who decided, the answer to every criterion with its source, and the conditions if any. Include the questions you asked and my answers.

What I am asking for: that it leave the decision written with date and owner (step 6), with the conditions if there are any, and with the trail of questions and answers, which is the evidence that it assumed nothing.

3. Expected result

A file docs/ELIGIBILITY.md (or whatever name your project uses), one page long, with:

  • Date and who decided.
  • The two exclusionary criteria with their answer and their source:
  • X1: there is an accessible repository managed in a standard way (or the project is new).
  • X2: the interface can be driven by a program and every element is accessible with its different methods, with verification of the effect in the backend.
  • The weighted criteria, each with answer, source and weight (once the weight is calibrated).
  • The decision: go, no-go, or go under conditions, with the conditions written down.
  • The questions the agent asked and the answers, so that the trail remains.

4. Completeness check (you do it, without reading code)

  • Do the two exclusionary criteria have an explicit answer and a source? If either is red, is the decision "no-go", or does it carry a condition that resolves it?
  • Is any criterion answered with "I suppose so" or "probably"? If there is one, the step is not climbed: the agent had to ask.
  • Is the decision written with a date and a name?
  • Are the conditions (if any) verifiable? ("Have read access to production before step 3" is; "the client cooperates" is not.)

5. Warning signs

  • The agent filled in the whole sheet without asking a single question. Either the survey was perfect (it never is) or it assumed.
  • The decision is "go under conditions" and there are more than three conditions. It is probably a "no-go" nobody wants to say out loud.
  • Criterion X2 was answered "yes, with a little work". If the screens cannot be driven by a program today, that "little work" is the whole project.

Step 1 — The working agreement (step contract)

Stretch: start-up. When: once only, immediately after eligibility; revised when a hard lesson demands it. Form: one shot, with one adjustment round from you. Size: mandatory in every project. It is the cheapest step of all and the one that prevents the most frustration.

What it is for

Put in writing, before the first line of code, how you and the agent are going to work. It looks like a formality and it is not: it is what prevents the agent from "helping" by expanding the scope, leaving debt for later, or reporting as done what it believes it did. In my tool this file is called SQ.md (quality standards); the name does not matter, the content does.

Think of it this way: the agent is a new collaborator who arrives every morning with no memory of yesterday. The agreement is what you tell them on the first day, and what they will reread every day. If you do not write it down, you will have to say it every time, and you will say it differently every time. I wrote it for the first time when I had spent two weeks repeating the same things in every session Ref. 1, and nothing I put in it was new in systems engineering; what was new was having to write it for someone without memory.

1. Required context

What follows is the generic list. The concrete files you have to have made before each prompt, with their content and their minimum state, are in section 2, in the "Before sending the prompt" block that precedes each one.

  • None from the project: it is the first file.
  • From you: your working preferences (language, how you want to be asked, what you consider a waste of your time, what level of autonomy you give the agent).

2. Prompts

Opening:

Before sending the prompt, this has to exist, and you (or the business with you) make it, not the agent:

  • Nothing from the project: it is the first file that gets written. If you already did step 0, docs/ELIGIBILITY.md exists and it is fine for the agent to see it, but it is not needed.
  • Your working preferences, thought through before starting and written down in a short list you keep at hand (on a piece of paper, or in docs/MY_PREFERENCES.md if you want it to stay in the repository): in what language you want the conversation; how much you want it to ask you (little and with a recommendation, or a lot); what you consider a waste of your time; how much autonomy you give it (can it commit on its own? can it deploy to test on its own? never to production?); what you will not tolerate. The agent is going to ask you about this; the list is so that you answer in one line and do not improvise.
  • The project folder created and the agent opened there.
  • Minimum acceptable state: you know how to answer each one of those five questions. The agreement that comes out is going to be as good as your answers.

Before we write any code, we are going to set the working agreement between you and me. Write docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md with at least these rules, each with one line explaining what it prevents: (1) My time is sacred: do not ask me what you can resolve on your own, and when you do ask, ask in prose, with your recommendation. (2) Zero technical debt: whatever breaks is fixed at the root, with its regression test, in the same session. (3) Exact scope: no more and no less than what was asked; if you see something else, log it as a pending item, do not do it. (4) Documentation is updated in the same commit as the change. (5) Never report as done something you have not verified; always separate what you verified from what you believe. (6) Speak my language. Add any rules you think this project needs, marked as proposals. If you need to know something about how I work in order to write this, ask me.

What I am asking for: the six minimum rules, each with the failure it prevents (that is what makes them rules and not advice); that it propose rules of its own for the project but mark them as proposals, so that you accept or reject them; and that it ask whatever it needs to know about you. An agreement drafted without a single question is suspicious: the agent does not know the person it is going to work with.

Closing:

Before sending the prompt, you have to have this at hand:

  • The draft of docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md the agent wrote, read in full by you. For every rule it proposed, your verdict: you accept it or you reject it, and if you reject it, a one-line reason. That goes into the [X, Y] and [Z] gaps of the prompt, and you are the one who fills them in.
  • The answers to what it asked you about how you work, already given in the chat.
  • Minimum acceptable state: no proposed rule is left without a verdict. A rule you neither accepted nor rejected stays in the agreement as if you had accepted it.

I accept rules 1 to 6 and your proposals [X, Y]; I reject [Z] because [reason]. Finalize the agreement with today's date and a version number. Then add to the project file the instruction to read this agreement at the start of every session.

What I am asking for: that it close the agreement with a date and a version, and that it hook it into the project file so that it is read in every session. Without that hook the agreement exists but does not act.

3. Expected result

A file docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md (or SQ.md, or CONTRATO.md) with:

  • Date and version.
  • Each rule numbered, in one or two sentences, with the failure it prevents.
  • The project's own rules, marked as such.
  • A line at the foot saying that the agent proposes changes but does not edit the agreement on its own.

And a line in the project file (PROJECT.md or CLAUDE.md) ordering it to be read at the start of every session.

4. Completeness check

  • Are the six minimum rules there, each with the failure it prevents?
  • Is any rule written as an aspiration ("try to…", "whenever possible…")? If there is one, it is useless: rules are either followed or not.
  • Does the project file order it to be read in every session?
  • Did the agent ask you at least one question about how you work? If it drafted everything without asking anything, it is suspicious: it does not know you.

5. Warning signs

  • The agreement has more than twenty rules. Nobody, neither human nor model, follows twenty rules; six or eight get followed.
  • A rule says "the agent may modify this agreement when it considers it necessary". No.
  • Two weeks later, the agent does something the agreement forbids and nobody notices. The agreement is not being read: check the hook in the project file.

Step 2 — The project definition, the semantic tree and mandatory reading (step contract)

Stretch: start-up. When: written once, after the agreement; the semantic tree and the living state are kept up to date throughout the project, with every block and every new decision. Form: conversation: the agent drafts, asks for what is missing, you correct. Size: mandatory in every project. In a small one the project file can be fifty lines and the semantic tree twenty nodes; what they cannot do is not exist.

What it is for

Every session of the agent starts from zero: it does not remember yesterday. Give it a map, a short one, that tells it what the project is, what state it is in and what decisions have already been made, and force it to read the map before touching anything. The failure it prevents is twofold: throwaway work from not reading the map Ref. 9, and the map that grows until it becomes unreadable Ref. 41. The practical rule: the project file is the programme, not the history. What happened goes somewhere else.

The map has two pieces, and the second one does the most work. The first is the project file: what the system is, for whom, what it is NOT, the decisions made. The second is the semantic tree: the system broken down as a tree, with the domains, and under each one its actors, its processes (the use cases), its entities with their states, its screens and its integrations; and hanging from each node, the pending items and the decisions that touch it. The tree comes out of the survey you did with the classic methods of requirements engineering (interviews, use cases, domain model), the agent builds it, you correct it, and it is what gives the model the exact context of the node it is going to touch without having to read the whole project. And it is what lets you verify, without reading code, that the system the agent understands is the one the business asked for.

1. Required context

What follows is the generic list. The concrete files you have to have made before each prompt, with their content and their minimum state, are in section 2, in the "Before sending the prompt" block that precedes each one.

  • Business survey: what the client does, who uses the system, what problem it solves, what it must not do. With the interviews, the use cases or user stories you can name, and the business entities with their states, even if they are incomplete.
  • The working agreement (step 1).
  • If there is a previous system: read access to its code or its documentation.
  • The decisions already made, if any, even if only spoken.

2. Prompts

Opening:

Before sending the prompt, this has to exist, and you (or the business with you) make it, not the agent:

  • docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md (step 0), completed with the business survey. You write it from the intake interviews; if you used a method (interviews, use cases, user stories, screen prototypes), say which one and paste the result. It has to have: what the client does; who uses the system (the actors, with the name of their role); what problem it solves; what it must NOT do, in a section of its own, even if it has two items; the use cases or user stories the owner names, in their own words, even if they are ten lines; the business entities with their states ("an appointment is pending, confirmed, cancelled"), even if they are incomplete; the drawings or the photos of the screens, if there are any; the external integrations (messaging, payments, another system); and the decisions already taken, even if they were verbal, with the date and who took them.
  • docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md (step 1), closed with a date and a version number.
  • If a previous system exists: read access to its code or to its documentation, with the path or the link written down in the survey.
  • Minimum acceptable state: the survey has the "what it is not" section with at least two items, at least one use case in the owner's words, and at least one entity with its states. Without that, the tree that comes out is going to be invented, and you are going to have nothing to compare it against.

We are going to define the project in writing. Read the business survey in docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md and the working agreement in docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md. Evaluate whether the survey is sufficient to describe the system, its users, its domain and its boundaries; if it is not, ask me for what is missing before writing anything. Then write two things. First, PROJECT.md, three hundred lines at most, with: what the system is and for whom; what it is NOT; the main modules as a list; the decisions already made, each with its date; and the instruction to read this file and the semantic tree at the start of every session. Second, docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md: the system as a tree, with the domains at the top level and, under each one, its actors, its processes (the use cases, each with actor, trigger, steps, outcome and exceptions), its entities with their attributes, states and transitions, its screens, and its external integrations. Mark every node you inferred rather than read in the survey, so that I can confirm or correct it. Separately, create docs/STATUS.md for the living state (what is being worked on, what is open): that file changes every day; the other two change only when the system changes. Reply in my language.

What I am asking for: that it evaluate the survey and ask before drafting; that the project file be short (the three-hundred-line cap is arbitrary but necessary: without a cap, it grows); that it say explicitly what the system is not, because what is left unsaid is what the model is going to invent; that the decisions carry a date; that it build the semantic tree with the full structure (domains, actors, use cases with their parts, entities with states and transitions, screens, integrations), which is the domain model of requirements engineering put in the shape of a tree; that it mark what it inferred, because that is where the inventions hide; and that it separate the living state into another file, so that the map does not turn into history.

Closing:

Before sending the prompt, you have to have this at hand:

  • PROJECT.md, read in full by you (it has to fit in five minutes), with your corrections noted down: what is not true, what is not yours, what is superfluous.
  • docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md, walked node by node as if you were the client: every node marked as inferred has your verdict (confirmed or corrected), and you noted the actors that are missing, the states the business does not use, the screens nobody asked for.
  • The list of corrections, written down, which goes into the [list] gap of the prompt. If the list is empty the first time, distrust your reading, not the agent.
  • Minimum acceptable state: no node marked "inferred" is left without a verdict of yours.

Apply these corrections: [list]. Finalize PROJECT.md and docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md with today's date. Add to the working agreement two rules: the change history never goes into PROJECT.md (it goes to docs/STATUS.md or to the pending-items register); and every pending item must hang from a node of the semantic tree, and the tree is updated in the same commit whenever a block adds or changes a node.

What I am asking for: your corrections (above all to the nodes it marked as inferred), the date, and two new rules in the agreement: the one that protects the project file from becoming history, and the one that ties every pending item to a node of the tree, which is what keeps the tree alive and what lets you spot an out-of-scope pending item in ten seconds.

3. Expected result

  • PROJECT.md (or CLAUDE.md): short, mandatory reading, with what it is, for whom, what it is not, modules, decisions with dates, and the order to read it together with the tree. Three hundred lines at most.
  • docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md: the semantic tree, with the inferred nodes marked until you confirm them, and, from the first block onwards, the pending items and decisions hanging from their nodes.
  • docs/STATUS.md: living state, separate from the previous two.
  • In all three, a date of last update.
  • In the agreement, the two new rules.

4. Completeness check

  • Does PROJECT.md fit in a five-minute read? If not, it is wrong: it is the programme, not the history.
  • Does it say explicitly what the system does NOT do? (What is missing is what the agent is going to invent.)
  • Walk the semantic tree as if you were the client: are all the actors you know there? Does each entity have the states the business uses, no more and no less? Is there any node you do not recognise? That one is an invention: correct it now.
  • Are the inferred nodes marked? Did you confirm or correct all of them?
  • Does each decision have a date and who made it?
  • Did the agent ask anything about the business before drafting? If it asked nothing, check what it assumed.
  • Is the living state in another file?
  • The reading test: close the session, open a new one, and ask it what the system does NOT do and which node of the tree a given screen hangs from. If it answers with the map, the hook works.

5. Warning signs

  • The project file grew more than 20 % in a month. Something that is not programme is getting in.
  • The agent starts a session proposing something that was already decided and written down. It did not read the map: the mandatory-reading hook failed.
  • The "what it is not" section is empty or says "nothing in particular". Every system has boundaries; if they are not written down, the model is going to cross them.
  • There are pending items that do not hang from any node of the tree. Either the tree went stale or those items are out of scope; both things have to be looked at.
  • An entity in the tree has a state the business never named. The model invented it because it seemed logical, and it is going to build on top of it.

Step 3 — Size before designing the architecture (step contract)

Stretch: start-up. When: once, after the definition and before building the skeleton; redone if the business changes scale. Form: conversation: the agent computes and presents options; you choose. Size: always mandatory, but proportional: in a small project the sizing can be half a page and a single decision ("it fits in SQLite for years"). What it cannot be is skipped, because the question "how much is it going to grow?" costs five minutes and the wrong answer costs weeks.

What it is for

Make the structural decisions —which database engine, whether the interface is synchronous or asynchronous, how much concurrency— with numbers and not with taste. It is the step that costs the most to skip, and I say so from experience: in one of my systems I started with JSON files and when I measured it turned out it was going to generate 3.4 million rows per year; changing the interface afterwards cost a whole stage of re-engineering Ref. 15.

There is a key point worth keeping in mind: there is data that only the business has (how many entities, how many events per entity, how many operators at the peak, how much has to be kept) and the model cannot infer it. What it can infer is what is derived. That is why the prompt requires it to evaluate whether the primary data is complete and to ask for what is missing, instead of estimating it with a straight face. The one who knows what it lacks in order to compute is the model, not a form.

And a second idea I want you to take away: asymmetric decisions. There are decisions that cost very little if you make them and they turn out unnecessary, and a great deal if you do not make them and they turn out necessary. The asynchronous persistence interface from line one is the canonical example: if the engine ends up synchronous, one await was superfluous; if it ends up asynchronous and you did not plan for it, everything has to be rewritten. Make those decisions whenever they are cheap, even if they are not needed today.

Step 4 — The skeleton with its gates (step contract)

Stretch: start-up. When: once, at the close of the start-up stretch, after the sizing and before any functionality. This is where all the initial infrastructure and the integration and deployment pipeline get built. Form: one long shot, with your visual approval at the end. Size: mandatory in every project that is going to have users. In a throwaway prototype it can be skipped; but if the prototype survives (they always survive), this step is the first thing that has to be paid for.

What it is for

Make the system start, test and deploy EMPTY before it has any functionality. Discovering deployment at the end is hugely expensive: in one of my systems it arrived a month after the start and cost five pending items in a row Ref. 32. If the skeleton is deployed during start-up, those blows land with zero functionality at stake.

The general idea behind it: the path to production is part of the product, not a formality at the end. A system that works on the programmer's machine and cannot be deployed is not a system; it is a demo. And the only way to know the path works is to walk it while there is still nothing to lose. That is why this step sets up everything the loop is going to use later: the repository with its structure, the test environment, the continuous integration that runs the test suite on every push, and the automatic deployment to the test environment.

Step 5 — The plan is the pending items: the detailed specification lives in each one (step contract)

Stretch: loop. When: the register is created when the first functional block opens, as soon as the start-up ends; every new block starts by turning the client's request into specified pending items; the routine is used in every session throughout the project; the documentation of the finished item is written when it is closed, in the same session. Form: one shot when opening each block; afterwards, a routine of every session. Size: mandatory in every project. It is the step that makes all the others traceable: without numbered, specified pending items there is nowhere to hang either the specification or the evidence.

What it is for

Have a single work queue, ordered and gated, that is the truth of the project. There is no "plan" on one side and "tasks" on the other: the list of pending items IS the plan. It prevents the long specification that gets overtaken in two weeks and becomes obsolete Ref. 17, and the work nobody knows why it was done Ref. 7.

But there is more, and it is what distinguishes this step from any old task list. In the classic method there was a requirements specification document, a long one, that described everything the system had to do and that aged before it was finished. Here that document is decomposed: the detailed specification lives in each pending item. A well-written pending item carries: the node of the semantic tree it hangs from; the user story or use case that originates it, in the client's words; the detailed description; the applicable business rules, with their decision and date; the data in and out; the screens affected, with the prototype if there is one; the edge cases and exceptions; the acceptance criteria in the form "given, when, then"; and how it is verified. The sum of the pending items is the specification of the system, and traceability (requirement → pending item → test → commit) is not a separate matrix: it is the pending item itself.

And the third thing, which closes the circle: closed pending items are documentation. When a pending item is closed, it is neither deleted nor filed empty: it stays with its specification, the decision that originated it, the evidence it was closed with (the red and green counts, the screenshot, the measured number), the commit and the date. The queue of closed pending items is the functional history of the system: what was built, why, when and how it was tested. From there come the changelog, the new sections of the manual, and the answer to "why does the system do this?" six months later.

Step 6 — Decisions are written next to the code (step contract)

Stretch: loop. When: in every session of the loop, every time the agent runs into an unwritten business rule or a fork that changes what the user sees. There are usually two or three per block. Also during start-up (steps 0 and 3) and at go-live (14 and 15). Form: short conversation: the agent detects that there is a decision, asks, and leaves it written down. Size: mandatory in every project. In a small one the register can be a ten-row table; what matters is that the business rules have an owner.

What it is for

Make every business rule and every structural decision have an owner, a date and a place. If not, in six weeks nobody knows whether "approved plus balance goes back to the queue" was a decision of the client or an idea of the agent. And avoid the worst: the agent inventing domain rules because they seemed reasonable.

This has a consequence I want to say out loud: the model, when it runs into a gap in the rules, fills it. It does not stop; it completes with what seems most likely to it, and it does so with the same naturalness with which it writes everything else. That is why the routine of this step is not "write down the decisions" but "stop and ask when one is missing". The second is the hard part, and it is what has to be demanded in the agreement. The practice of leaving the rule written in the commit, with my words and the date, I imposed at the Health Fund Ref. 29, and the practice of freezing decisions before building comes from my tool Ref. 5.

Step 7 — Measure before building, with an instrument that is itself verified (step contract)

Stretch: loop. When: every time a pending item depends on a number or on a hypothesis about a cause, BEFORE building. In the start-up stretch, sizing is the first case. In a small project, "measuring" is counting with a command and writing the number down with its date. Form: one shot per measurement; sometimes a conversation, when the hypothesis is yours. Size: proportional. The ratchet and the query budget are for systems with volume. What does not change with size: no number that decides anything comes from an impression.

What it is for

Make every number that feeds a decision come from a program with a test, and not from a text search, from an impression, or from "I think there are about a hundred". And make the instrument itself get audited, because meters lie: one of mine did not see 84 calls with the counter at zero, and another said 27 when there were 44 Ref. 35, Ref. 36. The five rules I wrote after that are: a guarded meter; if the instrument does not exist, it gets built; it is audited in both directions; what is approximate gets marked; and the one who executes verifies the numbers of the one who planned.

This is the step that took me longest to formalise, and for a reason worth telling: I took it for granted that a model would apply on its own something as basic in engineering as "measure before touching". It does not. The model is very good at producing claims with a number in them ("there are about 120 loops of that kind") and those claims sound exactly the same when they have been measured and when they have been estimated. You have no way of telling them apart by the tone. The only way is to demand the instrument: "what did you measure it with? show me the command". If there is no command, it is an impression, however many decimals it has. I imposed the rules myself in session, correcting the agent, before they became doctrine Ref. 45.

Step 8 — Tests as locks, proved red (step contract)

Stretch: loop. When: with every pending item, when closing it: the test red and green, the regression of the neighbours and the end-to-end test of the flow touched. The complete end-to-end suite of every flow, at the close of each block and before every deployment to production. Form: one shot; the agent executes and reports, you read the report. Size: mandatory in every project. It is the heart of the method: if there is one step that cannot be negotiated, it is this one.

What it is for

A test you never saw fail, you do not know whether it proves anything. The rule here is not "write tests": it is "demonstrate that the test turns red against the old code, and then green with the new one". It prevents the tests that pass because they prove nothing, and the green that hides a bug Ref. 44.

Why this matters more with a model than with a human programmer: the model writes the code and the test in the same session, with the same understanding. If it misunderstood the request, the test is going to test what it understood, and it is going to pass. The red against the old code is the only cheap way to check that the test is looking at what matters: if the old code passes it, the test does not tell before from after, and so it is useless. The case that shows it best is the one of the counters that did not match Ref. 26.

There is a serious objection to this step, and I want to leave it written down because people are going to use it against it: the same agent that writes the test and the code can fake the red. A study published in August 2026 measured that, when an agent is asked for the test-first cycle, it sometimes skips the red, reports it without having run it, or implements before the test so that it passes from the start (it is in Part F of the main document, the one on related work). My answer has three pieces, and all three are on the ladder. The first one, here: the red is not a story, it is a receipt: the exact command, the identifier of the version it ran against, the count of failing tests and the exit code, all pasted as they came out, so that you can run it again with a single command without understanding what it does. The second, in step 13: the audit by another session reruns a sample of those receipts. The third, in step 9: mutation testing, which plants bugs on purpose and counts how many of them the tests catch; it is the only measure that, in that same study, told the tests that watch from the tests that decorate.

And the second half: the end-to-end tests. A test that verifies from the screen (clicks, types, reads) and checks the effect in the backend (the row is there, the state changed) is the one that replaces your reading of the code. The acceptance criteria of the pending item ("given, when, then") are its script. And they run three times at three scales: the flow touched with every pending item, every flow at the close of each block, and every flow with the exact version before production.

Step 9 — Code quality verified by machine, with a gate (step contract)

Stretch: loop. When: it is set up once, when the first functional block opens (as soon as the start-up stretch finishes), and from then on it runs on its own on every commit, as a gate of continuous integration; every pending item is closed with quality green, after the red test (8) and before the manual test case (10). At the close of every block and before every deployment, the quality report is read with the owner. In a legacy system, the first run is the baseline and the findings enter the backlog with their speed mark. Form: one shot to set it up; afterwards, a routine of every change and a reading per block. Size: mandatory at its minimum level in every project (a linter and a size limit in the chain); complete in any project with users. Mutation testing is an optional reinforcement, recommended in every project with users.

What it is for

The owner does not read the code, and that is the point of the method. But the code exists, somebody is going to maintain it (the next agent, the next developer), and code that works today and is unreadable tomorrow is debt that gets charged in month three. If nobody reads it, a machine has to look at it: tools that, on every commit, measure what a human reviewer would look at and turn it into a number with a limit. I did this in all my projects, without naming it as a step, and a quality audit handed me back a component of more than two thousand lines and a project file of almost ten thousand Ref. 41: neither of the two would have passed a written size limit. The static ratchet, which prevents a quality number from getting worse Ref. 35, is the shape I give this step.

What gets measured by machine, in order of importance: that the code respects a single style (the linter and the formatter); that no function and no file goes over a size; that the complexity of each function stays under a limit; that there is no duplicated code and no dead code; that the dependencies are declared and used; that the coverage of the tests never drops (the ratchet); and, if the language allows it, that the types close. None of this replaces the red test: impeccable code can be wrong. What it prevents is correct code that nobody can ever touch again.

And an optional reinforcement that is worth it in any project with users: mutation testing. A tool plants bugs on purpose in the code (changes a "greater than" into a "greater than or equal", deletes a line, flips a condition) and runs the tests; every bug the tests do not catch is a surviving mutant, and the proportion caught is the score. It is the measure that complements the demonstrated red of step 8: the red proves that one test watches today's change; mutation measures how much the whole suite watches. And it is, as far as I know, the only one that in the 2026 studies told the tests that prove from the tests that decorate (Part F of the main document).

If you are a beginner. A linter is a program that reads the code and marks what is badly written or dangerous (a variable that is not used, a comparison that always gives the same result), like the spell checker of a word processor. A formatter arranges it with a single style (indentation, spaces) so that all the code looks as if it were written by the same hand. The complexity of a function is how many different paths it can take (every "if" increases it); a function with forty paths is understood by nobody. Duplicated code is the same block copied in two places: the day one of them is fixed, the other one stays wrong. Coverage is what percentage of the code is executed by some test. And a ratchet is a mechanism that only lets things improve: today's number is tomorrow's floor. Mutation testing is a program that breaks the code on purpose, one small change at a time, and looks at whether any test notices; if none does, that piece of code is not being watched.

Step 10 — Manual test cases are written together with the functionality, and people execute them (step contract)

Stretch: loop. When: with every pending item, when closing it, after the red test (8) and quality (9): the agent writes the manual test cases of the flow it touched. They are executed by people at three moments: at the close of every block (the human tests), before the first deployment to production (the acceptance tests with the owner), and after every deployment, in a read-only pass. Every execution that fails reinjects a requirement into the loop as a corrective pending item. Form: a routine of every pending item; afterwards, human execution per cycle. Size: mandatory in every project with users: the manual test case is the only test the model neither writes nor runs.

What it is for

The method has automated tests to spare: the red test, the regression, the end-to-end on three scales. The model writes and runs all of them, and all of them test what the model understood. What is missing is the test a person does with their own eyes and their own hands, following a written script, and which finds what no test finds: the button that is where it should not be, the message that cannot be understood, the flow that technically works and humanly is useless. The human view and the human tests were already in the timeline; what was missing was that they have a script, and that the script be written by the same one who built the functionality, at the same moment, out of the acceptance criteria of the pending item. Without a script, the human test is "play with the system for a while", and what gets found depends on the mood of the day. With a script, it is executed the same way every time, what happened gets recorded, and what fails comes back into the loop as a requirement. It is the closing of the circuit that the process diagram shows: the manual execution of the test cases reinjects requirements into development.

A manual test case always has the same shape: an identifier; the pending item that originates it; the precondition (what has to exist beforehand: a patient, an appointment from yesterday); the steps, numbered, each one with what the person does and what they must see; the expected result at the end, including the backend effect a person can verify (a record that appears, a message in the fake inbox); the test data to use; and the space for the result of each execution: date, who, passed or failed, observation.

If you are a beginner. A manual test case is a recipe for a person to test a piece of functionality: what to prepare, what to do step by step, and what has to happen at each step. It is written so that anyone can follow it the same way, without knowing how the system is built. To execute a case is to follow the recipe and write down whether it passed or failed. A precondition is what has to exist before starting. And to reinject means that whatever the person found wrong comes back to the list of pending items as a new request, in their words.

  • docs/MANUAL_TESTS.md created, even if empty, with the format of the case in the header: identifier, pending item, precondition, steps (action and visible result), expected result with the backend effect verifiable by a person, test data, and the execution table (date, who, passed or failed, observation).
  • The decision about who executes and when, written in PROJECT.md or in the agreement: for a small project, the owner at the close of every block and before the first deployment.
  • docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md, where the routine goes.
  • Minimum acceptable state: the file exists with its format and the pending item has acceptance criteria.

Every pending item that changes what a user sees or does is closed with its manual test cases written in docs/MANUAL_TESTS.md, derived from its acceptance criteria: one case per flow the item touches, with an id, the pending item, the precondition, the numbered steps (what the person does and what they must see), the expected result including a backend effect a person can verify without tools, the test data to use, and an empty execution table (date, who, passed or failed, observation). Write them for a person who does not know how the system is built. Keep the cases of closed items up to date: when a flow changes, its cases change in the same commit. Never mark a case as executed yourself: execution is human.

What I am asking for: that the case come out of the acceptance criteria (so that it tests what was asked for, not what the model understood); that it be written for somebody who does not know how the system works, which is the difference between a test case and a technical note; that it include the backend effect verifiable by a person, so that the manual test also looks at the other side of the screen; that the cases be maintained in the same commit, just like the documentation; and that the model never execute them, because executed by the model they are one more test, and the whole point is that a person executes them.

For an execution (close of block, acceptance, after a deployment):

Before sending the prompt, you have to have this at hand:

  • The execution done: a person followed the cases of docs/MANUAL_TESTS.md in the test environment (or in production, read-only, after a deployment) and wrote in the table of each case the date, who, passed or failed, and the observation. The person does this, by hand, before pasting the prompt; the agent does not do it.
  • The loose observations that do not fit in any case (what was annoying, what was not understood), written at the end of the file with a date.
  • Minimum acceptable state: every case in scope has today's result, even if it is "could not be executed" with the reason.

Read the executions of today in docs/MANUAL_TESTS.md. For every case that failed, and for every loose observation at the end of the file, open a corrective pending item in docs/PENDING.md with the person's words as the way to reproduce it, hanging from the node of the semantic tree it touches; do not fix anything now. For every case that passed, nothing. Then tell me, flow by flow, how many cases passed and how many failed, compared with the previous execution, and which failures repeat. If a case could not be executed, tell me why and whether the case or the system is the problem. Reply in my language.

What I am asking for: the reinjection made operative: every human failure is a corrective pending item, with the person's words (not the model's) as the reproduction, and hanging from the tree; the comparison with the previous execution, which is what tells you whether the system improves or degrades between blocks; and the distinction between "the case is badly written" and "the system is wrong", which are two different pending items.

3. Expected result

  • docs/MANUAL_TESTS.md with one case per flow touched by every closed pending item, in the format, written for a person.
  • The executions recorded, with date and who, in every cycle.
  • One corrective pending item for every failure and for every observation.
  • The routine in the agreement.

4. Completeness check (you do it, without reading code)

  • Take a case at random and follow it yourself in the test environment without asking the agent anything: could you? If you had to ask, the case is not written for a person.
  • Does every closed pending item that touches a screen have its case? Ask for the list of pending items without a case.
  • Does the case say what effect to verify on the other side of the screen, and how a person verifies it?
  • Does the last execution have a date and a name in every case? Is there any "passed" without a date?
  • Does every failure of the last execution have its pending item, with the person's words?

5. Warning signs

  • The agent marked the cases as executed. It executed them, which is the same as not executing them.
  • The cases are copies of the automated test in prose. A manual case has to look at what the test does not look at: what is seen, what is understood, what is annoying.
  • All the cases pass in every execution. People always find things; if they find nothing, they are not following the script or the script does not ask them to look.
  • The failures get "fixed" on the spot, without a pending item. The person's words and the trail of what repeated get lost.

Step 11 — Producer/consumer symmetry, and the anti-pattern register (step contract)

Stretch: loop. When: with every pending item that touches a contract between two parts of the system. The map of contracts is built when the second functional block opens, once there is a screen and a server. The anti-pattern register grows every time something breaks for the second time. Form: automatic inside the loop; plus a file that grows with every blow. Size: symmetry is always mandatory; the producer/consumer map is optional in a single-module project, and mandatory as soon as there is a screen and a server that are separate.

What it is for

When you change one end (the server, the robot, the database schema), make the other end (the screen, the counter, the consumer) change in the same commit. If you do not, sooner or later they diverge, and the user sees one number on one side and another number on the other Ref. 26. And the mistakes you have already paid for once, write them down so you do not pay for them twice Ref. 2.

The general principle behind this, which I imposed at the Health Fund and which holds for every system Ref. 27: every list has a single owner. The columns of a table, the possible states of an order, the reasons why something is waiting: one single part of the system (the producer) defines them and all the others read them from there. As soon as there are two copies of the same list, one of them is out of date; the only thing left to find out is which. The semantic tree of step 2 is the first place where those lists are declared: the states of an entity are there, and the code has to read them from a single source.

1. Required context

What follows is the generic list. The concrete files you have to have made before each prompt, with their content and their minimum state, are in section 2, in the "Before sending the prompt" block that precedes each one.

  • A map of which parts of the system consume what: which screens read which services, which processes write which tables. If it does not exist, the first prompt builds it.
  • The anti-pattern register docs/GOTCHAS.md, even if it is empty.

2. Prompts

To build the map (once):

Before sending the prompt, this has to exist, and you (or the business with you) make it, not the agent:

  • The system with at least one functional block built: with no contracts between parts there is no map to make.
  • docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md up to date: the lists (states, types, causes) are declared there, and the map has to match the tree.
  • A list, written by you, of the parts that live outside the repository and that produce or consume something from the system (a robot, a spreadsheet that reads the database, a process from another team), so that the agent puts them in the map even though it cannot see them. If there are none, say so.
  • Minimum acceptable state: the agent can read the whole repository, and you told it what is outside.

Build docs/PRODUCER_CONSUMER_MAP.md: for every contract in the system (a service, a table, a file format, a list of values), who produces it and who consumes it. If there are parts of the system you cannot access or do not understand, tell me instead of leaving them out of the map. Reply in my language.

What I am asking for: the inventory of contracts with their two sides, and the completeness clause in its form for this step: what cannot be seen is declared, not omitted. A map with hidden holes is worse than no map at all.

Routine (goes in the working agreement):

Before sending the prompt, you have to have this at hand:

  • docs/PRODUCER_CONSUMER_MAP.md, built and reviewed by you: pick one contract and verify that the two sides are the ones you know.
  • docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md, where the routine goes.
  • Minimum acceptable state: the map exists and has at least the contracts of the first block.

Before closing any change that touches a contract, look up every consumer in the map and update them in the same commit. Always prefer that the producer defines the list (columns, causes, states) and the consumer reads it, rather than each keeping its own copy. If the map does not have that relationship, add it.

What I am asking for: symmetry as a reflex (look for the other side before closing), the rule of the list with a single owner, and that the map maintains itself as it gets used.

When something breaks for the second time:

Before sending the prompt, you have to have this at hand:

  • The evidence that it is the second time: the pending item or the commit of the first one, located by you (search among the closed pending items in docs/PENDING.md), to paste it together with the prompt. Without that reference, the agent is going to record a generic anti-pattern that is no use for detecting anything.
  • docs/GOTCHAS.md, created even if it is empty, with the format in the header: the smell, the root cause, the fix, how to detect it earlier.
  • Minimum acceptable state: you can name the first time with a date or with a pending item number.

This has happened to us before. Record it in docs/GOTCHAS.md with: how it smells (the early sign), the root cause, the fix, and how to detect it next time before it hurts. And add, if possible, a test or an automatic check that detects it.

What I am asking for: the fixed format of an anti-pattern (smell, root cause, fix, early detection) and, above all, the automatic check: an anti-pattern that is written down but not automated will repeat itself when the person or the session changes.

3. Expected result

  • docs/PRODUCER_CONSUMER_MAP.md: the list of contracts with producer and consumers.
  • In every commit that touches a contract: the consumers updated, named in the message.
  • docs/GOTCHAS.md: one entry per anti-pattern, with the four parts (smell, root cause, fix, how to detect it beforehand), and the date of the first time.

4. Completeness check

  • Pick a contract from the map and ask the agent to show you where each consumer reads it. If any of them keeps its own copy of the list, there is debt.
  • Is there a number that is shown on two different screens? Compare: do they match?
  • Does every GOTCHAS entry have the four parts?
  • Did a mistake that was already in GOTCHAS repeat itself? If so, the automatic check is missing.

5. Warning signs

  • The same value appears hand-written in more than one file ("PENDING", "APPROVED" on the server and on the screen). Two copies; one will diverge.
  • A change to the server was closed with the server test green and nobody touched the screen.
  • GOTCHAS has entries without the "how to detect it beforehand" part. Those are anecdotes, not anti-patterns.

Step 12 — Honest verification and proportional reporting (step contract)

Stretch: loop. When: in every report, of every pending item, throughout the whole project. It is the shape that everything the agent tells you has to have. Form: routine; it is the shape every report from the agent must have. Size: mandatory in every project, without exception. It costs nothing and it is what lets the owner not read code.

What it is for

If you are not going to read code, the agent's report has to be reliable. Not optimistic, not "complete in appearance": reliable. This is the genuinely new thing about the method: the author of the code also manufactures the evidence, and does so with the same confidence in its voice when it is right and when it is wrong. That is why the evidence has to prove itself, and the report has to separate what was verified from what is believed. I wrote the rule in June Ref. 8, and I needed it again in October, when an authentication error was reported with the label "success" Ref. 43.

Understand why the model tends to over-report. It is not bad faith: it is that it was trained to be useful and complete, and a report that says "I could not verify this" feels less useful than one that says "done". So ask it explicitly for the bad news, the blind spots and what it did not do. If you do not ask, it does not give it to you, not because it hides it but because it does not consider it part of the answer.

1. Required context

What follows is the generic list. The concrete files you have to have made before each prompt, with their content and their minimum state, are in section 2, in the "Before sending the prompt" block that precedes each one.

  • The work done and the pending item that asked for it.
  • The working agreement (step 1), which already forbids announcing what was not done.

2. Prompts

Routine (goes in the working agreement; applies to every report):

Before sending the prompt, this has to exist, and you (or the business with you) make it, not the agent:

  • docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md (step 1), where the routine goes.
  • The list of what you asked for in the session, written by you (the items of the request), to contrast the report against: the prompt asks it to cover every item one by one, and you have to know how many there were.
  • Minimum acceptable state: nothing else. This routine is pasted once and rules forever.

When you report, separate three blocks: VERIFIED (what you tested, with the command and its output), BELIEVED (what you think works but did not test, and why not), and BLIND SPOTS (what you cannot see from where you are: another environment, real data, the user's browser). Cover every item I asked for, one by one, even when the answer is "I did not do it". No claim without its proof next to it. Do not say "done": say what is done and how you know it. If something went wrong, say it first. Reply in my language.

What I am asking for, clause by clause: the three blocks, which are the structure that makes a report honest (what was verified with its proof, what is believed with the reason why it was not tested, what it cannot see); coverage item by item, so that nothing "gets forgotten"; the proof next to every claim; the ban on a bare "done"; and the bad news first, because the model tends to leave it for the end or for never.

When you say "it is still broken":

Before sending the prompt, you have to have this at hand:

  • Your verification, done in the place where you look (the test environment, your browser), with what you saw written down: at what URL, what version the system shows if it shows one, what you did, what you expected, what happened.
  • The version number or the commit the agent said contained the fix, copied from its previous report.
  • Minimum acceptable state: you can tell it "I look at [URL], I see version [N], I do [this], [this] happens". Without that, "it is still broken" is an impression, and the agent is going to diagnose an impression.

Before diagnosing again, compare the version running where I see the problem with the version that contains your fix. Tell me both. If they differ, the problem is publication, not code.

What I am asking for: that faced with an "it is still broken" it does not diagnose again from scratch but first compares versions. Half of the "still broken" cases are fixes that are in the repository but not in the place where you are looking Ref. 45.

3. Expected result

Every report from the agent with:

  • The three blocks (verified / believed / blind spots), even if one of them is empty.
  • One line per item requested, with its status.
  • For every "verified": the command and the output, or the test and its result, or the number and its meter.
  • What went wrong, at the beginning and not at the end.

4. Completeness check

  • Are the three blocks there? If "believed" and "blind spots" are always empty, the agent is not being honest: there is always something it did not see.
  • Pick a claim from the "verified" block at random: does it have its proof next to it? Can you reproduce it?
  • Does it cover every item you asked for, or is there one that "got forgotten"?
  • Is the bad news at the beginning?
  • Did it ever say a bare "done"? That is a breach of the agreement.

5. Warning signs

  • Ten reports in a row without a single blind spot. Nobody sees everything; the block is empty out of habit, not out of truth.
  • The "verified" block has phrases like "it should work". That is "believed" in disguise.
  • The report is longer than the change. A proportional report says what needs to be said; an inflated one is covering something up with volume.

Step 13 — The periodic audit as input to the plan (step contract)

Stretch: going live. When: mandatory before the first deployment to production; it opens the go-live stretch. Afterwards, in operation, every one or two months depending on the pace of change, and at the close of every large stage. Form: one long shot, ideally to an agent different from the one that builds. Size: mandatory before the first deployment to production, in any project; afterwards, proportional to the pace of change (a system that changes every week is audited every month; a stable one, every six).

What it is for

Look at the whole system with fresh eyes every so often, and turn what you see into pending items. The audit is not a report that gets filed: it is plan. It avoids the password written into the code that nobody had seen, the queries that read whole tables Ref. 18, the known vulnerability in a dependency, the component of thousands of lines Ref. 41. And it is the independence of quality control taken one step further in: the one who audits is not the one who built.

On independence: you do not need another company or another tool. Another session, with another instructions file, that did not take part in the construction is enough. What you are after is that the auditor does not share the builder's assumptions. A model that built a module "knows" that the hardcoded password was provisional; a model that sees it for the first time reports it as what it is. That is how two administrator doors that the builder had left in a single day were detected Ref. 40.

1. Required context

What follows is the generic list. The concrete files you have to have made before each prompt, with their content and their minimum state, are in section 2, in the "Before sending the prompt" block that precedes each one.

  • The complete repository, read-only.
  • Production or the test environment, read-only (database, logs).
  • The previous audit, if there was one, to measure what changed.
  • The scope: integrity, security, performance, code quality, or all of them.

2. Prompts

Opening (to an agent different from the builder, if possible):

Before sending the prompt, this has to exist, and you (or the business with you) make it, not the agent:

  • A new session of the agent, different from the builder's: a clean clone of the repository in another folder and, if your tool allows it, a different instructions file that only says "you are an auditor; you did not build this system". Without this, the auditor shares the builder's assumptions and confirms them.
  • Read-only access for that session to the test environment or to production: database, logs. Read-only credentials, outside the repository, with the name of the variable said in the chat.
  • The scope, decided by you, for the [scope] gap: integrity, security, performance, code quality, or everything.
  • The previous audit, if there was one (docs/AUDIT_[date] from last time), so that it measures what changed.
  • Minimum acceptable state: the auditor's session is not the builder's, and it has read-only access.

Audit this system for [scope]. Read-only: change nothing except writing your report to docs/AUDIT_[date].md. If auditing some part requires access you lack (the database, the log, an environment), tell me and mark that part as not audited; do not pass it as fine. Go through all of the code, not a sample. For every finding: where it is (file and line), what the risk is, what evidence proves it (the command, the query, the number), and its priority: P0 must be fixed now, P1 soon, P2 when possible. Give a grade to every dimension you audited. End with the list of P0 findings turned into proposed pending items. Reply in my language.

What I am asking for, clause by clause: read-only (the auditor does not fix; it proposes); the completeness clause in its form for auditing: what could not be verified is declared, not approved by omission; all of the code and not a sample, because the serious findings are usually where nobody looks; every finding with location, risk, evidence and priority, so that it is actionable without re-reading the report; grades per dimension, to compare with the previous audit; and the P0 findings already turned into proposed pending items.

Closing (to the builder):

Before sending the prompt, you have to have this at hand:

  • docs/AUDIT_[date].md, read by you, and two findings at random verified with the auditor (ask it for the command, for the line; it has to be able to show them).
  • The builder's session open: this prompt goes to the builder, not to the auditor, with the date of the file put into the gap.
  • Minimum acceptable state: you read the report, you know how many P0s and P1s there are, and you are going to count the pending items it opens.

Read docs/AUDIT_[date].md. Open one pending item for every P0 and P1, citing the finding. If you disagree with any of them, tell me with the evidence against; do not ignore it.

What I am asking for: that the audit turns into pending items (otherwise it is a report that gets filed), and that disagreement be explicit and backed by evidence, not silent.

3. Expected result

  • docs/AUDIT_[date].md with: the scope, what could be verified and what could not (and why), the findings with location, risk, evidence and priority, a grade per dimension, and the list of proposed pending items.
  • In docs/PENDING.md: one pending item for every P0 and P1, citing the finding.

4. Completeness check

  • Does the report say what it could NOT verify? If it says it verified everything, be suspicious.
  • Pick two findings: do they have file and line, and evidence you can reproduce?
  • Does every P0 have its pending item open before the audit is considered closed?
  • Will the grades per dimension be comparable with the previous audit?
  • Did the same agent that built also audit? If so, ask for a second look at the P0 findings.

5. Warning signs

  • The audit found no P0 at all. In a system more than three months old, that is almost never true; more likely the scope was small or the auditor shared the assumptions.
  • The findings have no file line. They are opinions, not findings.
  • Two weeks went by and the P0 findings still have no pending item. The audit got filed.

Step 14 — Fail-closed, locks and zero silent harm (step contract)

Stretch: going live. When: in the go-live stretch, BEFORE building any functionality that charges, sends, approves or deletes; revisited with every new consequential action. New automations start in dry-run mode and are enabled after watching their log for a few days. Form: conversation: the agent lists the consequential actions, you confirm which ones get a lock and of what kind. Size: mandatory as soon as the system does anything irreversible (charging, sending, deleting, approving). A read-only system can skip it; the moment it gains its first button that acts on the world, this step becomes the most important one.

What it is for

Make the system, when in doubt or on failure, NOT act, and say so. It sounds obvious and it is the opposite of what software does by default, which when in doubt carries on. It avoids the double charge, the automatic approval that never went past a human, the git add -A that uploads a credential, and the deletion of the client's data. The locks I put in at the Health Fund are the model: the panic button with a double safety, the auto-approver in dry-run mode, the watchman with guards Ref. 28; and the reflex that a failure leaves a row without its button instead of breaking the whole listing Ref. 25.

The principle has a name, "fail-closed", and it comes from physical security: a security door that stays shut when the power goes out. The opposite, "fail-open", is the door that opens. In software most things are fail-open by default: if the validation fails, the code carries on; if the service does not respond, everything is assumed to be fine. The method inverts the default: when in doubt, no. And it applies both to the system and to the agent that operates it.

1. Required context

What follows is the generic list. The concrete files you have to have made before each prompt, with their content and their minimum state, are in section 2, in the "Before sending the prompt" block that precedes each one.

  • Survey of consequential actions, named generically: what the system does that charges, sends, approves, deletes, or notifies someone outside; what happens if it is done twice; what happens if it is done wrong; how it is undone. The business knows it; the agent evaluates whether the list is complete and asks. The semantic tree helps: every consequential process is there.
  • Which actions the agent itself can take on the system (if it is embedded).

2. Prompts

Opening:

Before sending the prompt, this has to exist, and you (or the business with you) make it, not the agent:

  • docs/CONSEQUENTIAL_ACTIONS_SURVEY.md: the survey of consequential actions, written with the domain owner. What the system does or is going to do that charges, sends, approves, deletes, or notifies somebody outside; and for each action: what happens if it is done twice, what happens if it is done wrong, how it is undone, and who should be able to do it. The processes of the semantic tree give you the starting list: walk them one by one. The prompt names it "the survey of consequential actions", with no path: save it under this name or tell it in the chat where it is.
  • If the agent is going to be embedded or resident: which actions it can perform itself on the system, in a separate section of the same survey.
  • docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md, up to date.
  • Minimum acceptable state: every process of the tree that acts on the world is in the list with its "what happens if it is done twice".

We are going to decide which actions of the system need a lock. Read the survey of consequential actions, and go through the code looking for everything that charges, sends, approves, deletes or notifies outwards. Compare the two lists: if the code has actions the survey does not mention, or the other way round, ask me before going on. For every action propose: whether it is reversible, which lock it gets (explicit confirmation, double safety, dry-run mode, daily limit, a human in the loop), and what the way back is if it goes wrong. General rule: on any doubt or failure along the way, the action is NOT executed and the reason is logged. Present me the table; I confirm lock by lock. Reply in my language.

What I am asking for: the cross-check between what the business said and what the code does (that is where the actions nobody remembered show up); the completeness clause in its form for this step: the differences between the two lists are asked about, not resolved; for every action, reversibility, lock and way back; and the general fail-closed rule written into the prompt, so that it ends up in the table and in the code.

Closing:

Before sending the prompt, you have to have this at hand:

  • The table the agent presented, read lock by lock with the owner; for every row, your confirmation or your change, written out for the [list], [X] and [Y] gaps of the prompt.
  • Minimum acceptable state: there is no row without a verdict. An unconfirmed lock is a lock that is not going to be implemented.

I confirm locks [list]; change [X] to [Y]. Implement them with one test per lock that proves that, under the doubt condition, the action does NOT happen (proved red against the version without the lock). Record the final table in docs/LOCKS.md. And for any new automation: dry-run mode first, logging what it would have done, until I enable it.

What I am asking for: the test per lock proved red (without the lock, the action happened; with the lock, it does not), which is step 8 applied to safety; the final table; and the dry-run rule for every new automation, which is what lets you see what it would have done before letting it do it.

3. Expected result

  • docs/LOCKS.md: one row per consequential action: what it does, whether it is reversible, the lock, the way back, who approved it and when.
  • One test per lock, proved red, which verifies that when in doubt the action does not happen.
  • New automations starting in dry-run mode, with the log of "would have done".
  • If the agent is embedded: its permissions written down (what it can read, what it can change, what requires approval).

4. Completeness check

  • Is every action that charges, sends, approves or deletes in the table? Think of one that is not there and ask.
  • Does every lock have its red test? ("Without the lock, the action happened; with the lock, it does not.")
  • Does every irreversible action have a written way back? If it does not, it needs a double safety.
  • Did the new automations start in dry-run mode? Did you see the "would have done" log before enabling them?
  • What happens if the embedded agent tries something outside its permissions? Ask it to try and show you the rejection in the log.

5. Warning signs

  • An automation went from "does not exist" to "runs on its own" without a dry-run period.
  • The log has no entry at all of "action not executed due to doubt". Either the system never doubts (impossible) or when it doubts it carries on.
  • A lock can be skipped with a parameter "for testing". That parameter will end up set in production.

Step 15 — Continuous integration, environments, secrets and deployment as part of the product (step contract)

Stretch: going live. When: the integration chain and the deployment to the test environment are set up in step 4. The closing towards production — the real environment, secrets on the server, promotion, tested rollback, lock against secrets — is done in the go-live stretch, after the audit and the locks. The routine of every deployment applies throughout operation. Form: one shot to set it up; routine for every deployment. Size: mandatory in every project with users. In a small one, "continuous integration" can be a single GitHub action and "environments" can be two folders on the same server; what is not negotiable is that the tests run on their own, that the configuration is not in the repository, and that there is a rollback.

What it is for

Make reaching production a reproducible package: the tests run ONCE, the configuration lives outside the repository, and the secrets are not floating around. It avoids the tests run three times per commit and the burned quota Ref. 39, the deployment hardened one pending item at a time because it was discovered late Ref. 32, and the secrets pasted into the chat Ref. 42.

On secrets there is a rule to adopt as a reflex, with no exceptions: a secret that was seen in a chat, in a log or in a commit is no longer a secret. It does not matter whether "nobody saw it" or whether "it was deleted afterwards". It gets rotated. Rotation costs ten minutes; finding out whether somebody used it is impossible.

1. Required context

What follows is the generic list. The concrete files you have to have made before each prompt, with their content and their minimum state, are in section 2, in the "Before sending the prompt" block that precedes each one.

Step 16 — Total observability and traceability, with a configurable level (step contract)

Stretch: going live. When: in the go-live phase, after step 15 (there is already a deployment chain and configuration per environment) and before the performance tests (19), because without observability the performance tests do not say what broke. In a legacy system, the minimum level (structured log records with a configurable level and a correlation id) goes into the safety belt (N4); the complete level, here. The rule that "every new module logs and propagates" applies throughout operation. Form: one shot; the agent builds it and demonstrates it; you read one real trace. Size: mandatory at its minimum level in every project that is going to have users; complete in any project with consequences.

What it is for

When something fails in production, and it will, I want to be able to follow an action from the screen to the database and to every external call, with a single identifier, without opening the code and without asking the agent to "have a look". And I want to be able to raise the level of detail of the log records in production, in a minute, without redeploying, and lower it afterwards. That is observability: seeing what the system does. And the second thing is traceability: that every consequential action (charged, sent, deleted, approved) leaves a trace of who, when, what, from where and with which outcome, in a record the application cannot edit. Without this, the resident agent of step 23 operates blind, the monitoring of steps 21 and 22 has nothing to feed on, and every incident is diagnosed with hypotheses instead of with evidence. The orphan turn that stayed alive for an hour and forty-three minutes was found through the log, not through the code Ref. 16; and the resident agent started, precisely, by reading logs Ref. 13.

"Configurable level" is the clause that distinguishes this from "putting in logs": the level (error, warning, info, debug, trace) is changed by configuration on the server, per environment, without touching code and without deploying, and production runs at info except when something is being investigated.

If you are a beginner. A log record is a line the system writes every time it does something: "10:42:13, scheduling module, user reception1, appointment 17 cancelled". Structured means that every line has the same parts in the same order, so that they can be searched by machine. The level is how much detail is written: at "error", only what failed; at "trace", every step. The correlation id is a unique number given to each action when it starts (you pressed "cancel") and that travels attached to everything that action provokes (the query to the database, the message to the patient), so that afterwards you search for that number and the complete story appears. And the audit trail is a list, which cannot be edited, of the important actions: who did what and when.

Step 17 — Optimization of the persistence layer, measured before and after (step contract)

Form: two shots: measure and propose; apply with before and after. Size: proportional: in a small project, the indexes of the real queries and the end of the N+1 loops; in one with consequences, complete, with budgets per path.

What it is for

The persistence layer is where systems die slowly: they work well with a hundred records, acceptably with ten thousand, and at a hundred thousand every screen takes twenty seconds, and nobody knows when it started. This step exists so that this gets decided with numbers and before production, not with complaints and afterwards. And so that it gets decided with the method of step 7: every query the system makes is counted and timed with a meter that has a test, what the numbers say gets changed, and it is measured again on the same data. What it is not: it is not "rewriting the data access layer", nor choosing another engine. Step 3 decided that. It is adjusting what is there (indexes, pagination, batching, retention, pool size) on the basis of what the real queries do.

If you are a beginner. The persistence layer is the part of the system that stores and reads the data, almost always a database. An index is like the index of a book: without it, to find an appointment the database reads the whole table (that is a full scan); with it, it goes straight there. An N+1 loop is a very common mistake: to show a hundred appointments, the system makes one query for the list and then a hundred more queries, one per appointment, instead of just one. Pagination is showing twenty at a time instead of everything at once. Retention is how long each piece of data is kept before archiving or deleting it. And the connection pool is the number of "open lines" the system has with the database at the same time. None of this requires you to read code: it requires you to look at numbers before and after.

I want to know three things before the first user comes in: how the system behaves at the volume the business stated (does the most used screen respond within what the owner would tolerate?), how it behaves at "times ten" (does it hold, or what stops holding first?), and where it breaks: the exact load at which something gives way, and what that something is (the database connections, the memory, a queue, a lock). The breaking point is the most useful number of the whole stretch, because the alert thresholds of step 21 come out of it: an alert set "by eye" warns too late or warns always; an alert set below the measured breaking point, with a margin, warns while there is still time. The scale patterns I took from a system in production Ref. 12 and the test suite that was dying by memory and looked like a failed test Ref. 44 are the two antecedents: the first, about measuring the real load; the second, about a breaking point not found in time disguising itself as something else.

If you are a beginner. A load test is a program that simulates many users using the system at the same time, to see what happens. Throughput is how many actions per second it holds. Latency is how long an action takes to respond; you look at the median (half of the actions took less than this) and the 95th percentile (95 % took less than this; the remaining 5 % are the users who complain). The breaking point is the amount of load at which something stops working: not "it gets slow" but "the database connections ran out" or "the memory filled up". And a threshold is a limit number above which the monitoring warns.

What it is for

Step 22 — Error monitoring and cyclic report: no error without a pending item (step contract)

Stretch: operation. When: it is set up in the operation phase, with the first deployment, together with step 21, on the log records of step 16; from then on it runs on its own, with alerts at the moment and a cyclic report that is read with the owner. Form: one shot to set it up; afterwards, a cyclic routine. Size: mandatory in every project with users: the centralized capture and the report are not negotiable; the alerts at the moment, proportional.

What it is for

A system in production produces errors no user reports: the one who saw a blank screen and tried again, the one from the nightly job that failed and nobody looks at, the one from the external call that returned an error and the code swallowed. If I do not capture them, they do not exist until they exist too much. This step is the counterpart of step 21: that one watches performance, this one watches errors, and both end up in the same place, the loop, as corrective pending items. The underlying rules are two. No error without a pending item: a new error opens one, and it is closed with the test that reproduces it in red, as the corrective mode of the evolutive path demands. The authentication error reported as a success Ref. 43 and the four rounds to the root cause Ref. 22 are the two antecedents: the first, about errors hiding if they are not captured by machine; the second, about an error without a reproduction being fixed several times.

If you are a beginner. An error is anything the system tried to do and could not: a screen that did not load, a nightly job that was cut short, a call to another service that failed. An unhandled exception is an error the code did not foresee and that cuts short what it was doing. To capture an error is to store it with all its data (when, in which version, with which identifier, the stack, which is the list of the program's steps up to the failure) in a single central place, instead of letting it get lost on a user's screen. To group is to join the thousand times the same error happened into a single entry with a counter. And the error rate per flow is, out of every hundred times somebody booked an appointment, how many failed.

It avoids the agent that operates without a map of the system, and the method that repeats itself identically project after project without learning. The retrospective with numbers that led me to eliminate a whole team of agents is the model for the second part Ref. 6.

On the resident agent, the rule that orders everything: it acts in someone's name. Its credential is mapped to a responsible person, and everything it does is logged under that person's name. It is not an anonymous system account: it is an employee with a boss. That defines its scope (what that person authorised it to do) and its traceability (who answers for what it did).

1. Required context

What follows is the generic list. The concrete files you have to have made before each prompt, with their content and their minimum state, are in section 2, in the "Before sending the prompt" block that precedes each one.

For the resident agent:

  • What the agent can and cannot do in production: read, diagnose, change configuration, execute actions. You decide.
  • How the agent identifies itself to the system (a credential of its own, mapped to a responsible person).
  • Whom it notifies when it finds something it cannot resolve (ticket, email).

For the retrospective:

  • The project's incidents, the costs (tokens, quota, your time), the pending items that took the most rounds, and the dates.

2. Prompts

For the resident agent (opening):

Before sending the prompt, this has to exist, and you (or the business with you) make it, not the agent:

  • The decision to leave a resident agent, taken with the owner and written down: what for (support, diagnosis) and with what initial limits.
  • A credential of its own for the agent in production, created by you, mapped to a responsible person with a name, with the minimum scope (read-only, to begin with); the value, outside the chat and outside the repository.
  • docs/LOCKS.md (step 14) and docs/DEPLOYMENT.md (step 15), up to date: the prompt asks it to read the first one.
  • Whom the agent notifies when it finds something it cannot resolve: a channel (a ticket, an email) decided by you.
  • Minimum acceptable state: the credential exists, it has a human owner, and it is read-only.

We are going to leave you resident in production. First, build the map of the system you will need in order to operate it: modules, environments, where each configuration lives, which actions are consequential (read docs/LOCKS.md), whom to notify. If there are parts of the system you cannot see from where you are, tell me; the map must not have hidden gaps. Then propose your scope in three levels: read-only and diagnosis; configuration changes with my approval; consequential actions behind locks. I choose the level. Your credential acts as the person who created it: everything you do is logged under that person's name. Reply in my language.

What I am asking for: the map of the system before operating (operating without a map is the failure this step avoids); the completeness clause in its form: the gaps in the map are declared, not hidden; the three levels of scope so that you choose one and not an "it depends"; and the rule of the credential in a person's name.

For the resident agent (closing):

Before sending the prompt, you have to have this at hand:

  • The map and the three levels the agent proposed, read by you, and your choice of level (to begin with, always the first one).
  • A concrete action outside the chosen scope, thought up by you, so that the agent attempts it and you see the rejection in the log (with level 1, for example: changing a configuration).
  • Minimum acceptable state: you chose the level and you know which attempt has to fail.

Level [X]. Write in docs/RESIDENT_AGENT.md the map, the scope, the credential (never its value), and the procedure to request my approval. Then prove, with a real attempt recorded in the log, that something outside your scope is rejected.

What I am asking for: the resident agent's document (without the value of the credential, ever), and the red test of the scope: a real attempt, logged, to do something outside the level, rejected. It is step 8 applied to permissions.

For the retrospective:

Before sending the prompt, this has to exist, and you (or the business with you) make it, not the agent:

  • The numbers only you have, gathered beforehand: how many hours you put in (from your calendar, not from memory), how much you spent on the AI tool (the invoice or the usage panel), how much continuous integration quota was used, and the start and end dates of the stage. Write them in docs/RETROSPECTIVE_INPUTS.md, or give them in the chat when it asks for them.
  • The list of the incidents of the stage, as you remember them, with an approximate date, to contrast with what the agent pulls out of the pending items and the commits. What you remember and the agent does not find is a documentation gap, and it is worth noting down.
  • docs/PENDING.md with the closed items documented (step 5) and docs/DECISIONS.md (step 6), which are the source of the numbers.
  • Minimum acceptable state: the hours and the money are written down with their source. Without that, the retrospective is done with adjectives.

[The stage / the project] is over. Do the retrospective with numbers, not impressions: what it cost (tokens, quota, my time), how many pending items, which ones took the most rounds and why, which incidents happened and which step of the ladder each falls into, and which step failed or was missing. If you lack the data for any figure (my time, for instance), ask me for it or mark it as an estimate; do not invent it. End with a concrete proposal to change the ladder: which step is modified, how, and which incident justifies it. Write it to docs/RETROSPECTIVE_[date].md. Reply in my language.

What I am asking for: numbers with their source (costs, pending items, rounds, incidents); every incident mapped to a step, because an incident that falls into none is a step that is missing; the completeness clause for the numbers it does not have (ask or mark as an estimate); and at least one concrete proposal to change the ladder, because a retrospective that changes nothing was not a retrospective.

3. Expected result

  • docs/RESIDENT_AGENT.md: map of the system, chosen scope, credential mapped to a person, approval procedure, and the rejection test recorded in the log.
  • docs/RETROSPECTIVE_[date].md: the numbers with their source, the incidents mapped to steps, and at least one proposal to change the ladder with its justification.

4. Completeness check

  • Does the map of the system say what the agent can NOT see? If it does not say so, it has hidden gaps.
  • Did you test (or see in the log) that an action outside the scope is rejected?
  • Does the agent's credential act in the name of an identifiable person?
  • Does the retrospective have numbers with their source, or adjectives? ("It cost a lot" is not a number.)
  • Does every incident fall into a step? If one falls into none, a step is missing: that is the change proposal.
  • Is there at least one concrete proposal to change the ladder? If the retrospective changes nothing, it was not a retrospective.

5. Warning signs

  • The resident agent "needs" a higher level of permissions every week. Check whether what it asks for is to solve the system's problems or its own limits.
  • The retrospective says everything went well. Everything never goes well; it was written for you, not for the method.
  • Two projects in a row had the same incident and the ladder did not change between one and the other. Step 23 is not being closed: the retrospective gets written and not applied.
