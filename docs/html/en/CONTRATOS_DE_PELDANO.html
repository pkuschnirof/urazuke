One file per step. Each has the same skeleton: the header with phase, form and size (mandatory or proportional); what it is for; (1) required context, named generically; (2) the prompts, in English, each followed by the explanation in the reader's language of what we are asking for and why each clause is there; (3) the expected result, with its characteristics; (4) the completeness check, done by the owner without reading code; (5) the warning signs, the symptoms that the step was climbed badly.

The English prompts are a single piece, identical in every version of the document in the eight languages; node build/build.mjs extracts them to prompts/ and verifies that no translation has altered them.

They are written from what we did in the three repositories, not executed as-is yet. The first project that uses them end to end will correct several: that is part of step 15.

Step 0 — Can this project be run this way? (step contract)

Phase: start (once per project, before opening the repository). Form: conversation (the agent assesses and asks; the owner decides). Size: mandatory in every project, however small. It takes an hour; it saves months. Status: being elaborated with Pablo. The exclusionary criteria X1 and X2 are confirmed; the weighted criteria (D1 to D10) are still hypotheses without weights.

What it is for

To avoid getting into a project where the method cannot work. If the agent will not be able to verify itself end to end, or if there is no domain owner who decides, the method degrades into "programming with AI" and the human ends up reading code. This step is the front door: you cross it once, and if you cannot cross it, better to know before than after.

There is an underlying idea worth saying out loud: the method does not work for every project, and that is not a defect. A method that promises to work for everything is saying nothing. What this step does is put the boundary in writing, so that the decision to go in or not is a decision and not a hope.

1. Required context

Named generically; the agent evaluates whether it is enough and asks for what is missing.

  • Survey of the request: what the client wants, for whom, and what happens today without the system.
  • Technology survey: what technology exists or is proposed, whether there is a previous system, whether that system has a repository and how it is managed.
  • Testability survey: whether the screens can be driven programmatically and whether every element of the interface is accessible with its various methods (click, type, read state), so that a test can walk through the screen and verify the effect on the backend.
  • Who the domain owner is and how much time they have to decide.
  • What the consequence of an error is (money, health, operations, nothing).
  • Environments and access: whether there will be a test environment, whether the agent can reach it, whether production can be queried read-only.

2. Prompts

The prompts are in English, the language in which models follow instructions most precisely and with the fewest tokens. Under each one, the explanation of what we are asking for and why each clause is there.

Opening:

We are going to assess whether this project can be run with the Urazuke method. Read the initial survey in docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md and the criteria sheet in docs/ELIGIBILITY_CRITERIA.md. First, evaluate whether the survey is sufficient to answer every criterion. If any criterion cannot be answered from the survey, ask me for the missing information before going on; do not assume. Then fill in the sheet criterion by criterion: the answer, where it comes from, and for each exclusionary criterion whether it is green or red. Do not decide: present me the completed sheet with your reading and your doubts, and I will decide. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for: to read the two inputs (the survey and the criteria sheet); to evaluate whether the survey is sufficient before answering and, if it is not, to ask rather than assume (this is the central clause of every prompt in the method); to fill in the sheet with the source of every answer, so that it can be traced later; to mark the exclusionary criteria green or red; and not to decide, because eligibility is decided by the owner. The last line asks it to answer in the owner's language, because the prompt is in English but the conversation need not be.

Closing:

Decision: [go / no-go / go under these conditions: …]. Write the final sheet to docs/ELIGIBILITY.md with the date, who decided, the answer to every criterion with its source, and the conditions if any. Include the questions you asked and my answers.

What we are asking for: to leave the decision written with date and owner (step 6), with the conditions if any, and with the trail of questions and answers, which is the evidence that nothing was assumed.

3. Expected result

A file docs/ELIGIBILITY.md (or whatever name the project uses), one page, with:

  • Date and who decided.
  • The two exclusionary criteria with their answer and source:
  • X1: there is an accessible repository managed in a standard way (or the project is new).
  • X2: the interface can be driven programmatically and every element is accessible with its various methods, with verification of the effect on the backend.
  • The weighted criteria (D1 to D10 of the ladder, once they are settled), each with answer, source and weight.
  • The decision: go, no-go, or go under conditions, with the conditions written down.
  • The questions the agent asked and the answers, so that the trail remains.

4. Completeness check (done by the owner, without reading code)

  • Do the two exclusionary criteria have an explicit answer and a source? If either is red, is the decision "no-go", or is there a condition that resolves it?
  • Is any criterion answered with "I suppose so" or "probably"? If so, the step is not climbed: the agent had to ask.
  • Is the decision written with a date and a name?
  • Are the conditions (if any) verifiable? ("Have read access to production before step 3" is; "the client cooperates" is not.)

5. Warning signs

  • The agent filled in the whole sheet without asking a single question. Either the survey was perfect (it never is) or it assumed.
  • The decision is "go under conditions" and there are more than three conditions. It is probably a "no-go" nobody wants to say.
  • Criterion X2 was answered "yes, with a bit of work". If the screens cannot be driven programmatically today, that "bit of work" is the whole project.

Step 1 — The working agreement (step contract)

Phase: start (once per project; revised when a hard lesson demands it). Form: one shot, with one adjustment round from the owner. Size: mandatory in every project. It is the cheapest step of all and the one that prevents the most frustration.

What it is for

So that human and agent agree on how they will work before the first line of code. It looks like a formality and it is not: it is what prevents the agent from "helping" by expanding the scope, leaving debt for later, or reporting as done what it believes it did. In Forge this file is called SQ.md (standards of quality); the name does not matter, the content does.

One way to understand it: the agent is a new colleague who arrives every morning with no memory of yesterday. The agreement is what you tell them on the first day, and what they will re-read every day. If you do not write it, you will have to say it every time, and you will say it differently every time.

1. Required context

  • Nothing from the project: it is the first file.
  • From the owner: their working preferences (language, how they want to be asked, what they consider a waste of their time, how much autonomy they give the agent).

2. Prompts

Opening:

Before we write any code, we are going to set the working agreement between you and me. Write docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md with at least these rules, each with one line explaining what it prevents: (1) My time is sacred: do not ask me what you can resolve on your own, and when you do ask, ask in prose, with your recommendation. (2) Zero technical debt: whatever breaks is fixed at the root, with its regression test, in the same session. (3) Exact scope: no more and no less than what was asked; if you see something else, log it as a pending item, do not do it. (4) Documentation is updated in the same commit as the change. (5) Never report as done something you have not verified; always separate what you verified from what you believe. (6) Speak my language. Add any rules you think this project needs, marked as proposals. If you need to know something about how I work in order to write this, ask me.

What we are asking for: the six minimum rules, each with the failure it prevents (that is what makes them rules rather than advice); project-specific rules proposed but marked as proposals, so that the owner accepts or rejects them; and that it asks whatever it needs to know about the owner. An agreement drafted without a single question is suspicious: the agent does not know the person it is going to work with.

Closing:

I accept rules 1 to 6 and your proposals [X, Y]; I reject [Z] because [reason]. Finalize the agreement with today's date and a version number. Then add to the project file the instruction to read this agreement at the start of every session.

What we are asking for: to close the agreement with a date and a version, and to hook it into the project file so that it is read every session. Without that hook the agreement exists but does not act.

3. Expected result

A file docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md (or SQ.md, or CONTRATO.md) with:

  • Date and version.
  • Every rule numbered, in one or two sentences, with the failure it prevents.
  • The project-specific rules, marked as such.
  • A line at the bottom saying that the agent proposes changes but does not edit the agreement on its own.

And a line in the project file (PROJECT.md or CLAUDE.md) ordering it to be read at the start of every session.

4. Completeness check

  • Are the six minimum rules there, each with the failure it prevents?
  • Is any rule written as an aspiration ("try to…", "where possible…")? If so, it is useless: rules are either followed or not.
  • Does the project file order it to be read every session?
  • Did the agent ask you at least one question about how you work? If it drafted everything without asking, be suspicious: it does not know you.

5. Warning signs

  • The agreement has more than twenty rules. Nobody, human or model, follows twenty rules; six to eight get followed.
  • A rule says "the agent may modify this agreement whenever it deems necessary". No.
  • Two weeks later the agent does something the agreement forbids and nobody notices. The agreement is not being read: check the hook in the project file.

Step 2 — The project definition and mandatory reading (step contract)

Phase: start (once; kept up to date throughout the project). Form: conversation (the agent drafts, asks what is missing, the owner corrects). Size: mandatory in every project. In a small one the project file can be fifty lines; what it cannot be is absent.

What it is for

Every agent session starts from zero: it does not remember yesterday. So it needs a map, a short one, telling it what the project is, what state it is in and which decisions have already been made. And it needs the obligation to read it before touching anything. The failure it prevents is twofold: throwaway work from not reading the map, and the map that grows until it is unreadable (in one of the source systems the project file reached 9,661 lines, and in that state nobody reads it, not even the model).

The practical rule that comes out of that lesson: the project file is the programme, not the history. What happened goes elsewhere (the pending-items register, the living status, the journal); the project file says what the system is, for whom, what it is not, and what was decided.

1. Required context

  • Business survey: what the client does, who uses the system, what problem it solves, what it must not do.
  • The working agreement (step 1).
  • If a previous system exists: read access to its code or its documentation.
  • The decisions already made, if any, even if only spoken.

2. Prompts

Opening:

We are going to define the project in writing. Read the business survey in docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md and the working agreement in docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md. Evaluate whether the survey is sufficient to describe the system, its users and its boundaries; if it is not, ask me for what is missing before writing anything. Then write PROJECT.md, three hundred lines at most, with: what the system is and for whom; what it is NOT; the main modules as a list; the decisions already made, each with its date; and the instruction to read this file and the working agreement at the start of every session. Separately, create docs/STATUS.md for the living state (what is being worked on, what is open): that file changes every day; PROJECT.md almost never does. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for: to evaluate the survey and ask before drafting; a project file that is short (the three-hundred-line cap is arbitrary but necessary: without a cap, it grows); to say explicitly what the system is not, because what is left unsaid is what the model will invent; decisions with dates; and the living state in a separate file, so that the project file does not turn into a history.

Closing:

Apply these corrections: [list]. Finalize PROJECT.md with today's date. Add to the working agreement that the change history never goes into PROJECT.md: it goes to docs/STATUS.md or to the pending-items register.

What we are asking for: the owner's corrections, the date, and a new rule in the agreement that protects the project file from becoming a changelog. That rule is the one that was missing in the 9,661-line case.

3. Expected result

  • PROJECT.md (or CLAUDE.md): short, mandatory reading, with what it is, for whom, what it is not, modules, decisions with dates, and the order to read it. Three hundred lines at most.
  • docs/STATUS.md: living state, separate from the above.
  • In both, a last-updated date.
  • In the agreement, the rule that the history does not go in PROJECT.md.

4. Completeness check

  • Can PROJECT.md be read in five minutes? If not, it is wrong: it is the programme, not the history.
  • Does it say explicitly what the system does NOT do? (What is missing is what the agent will invent.)
  • Does every decision have a date and who made it?
  • Did the agent ask something about the business before drafting? If it asked nothing, check what it assumed.
  • Is the living state in another file?

5. Warning signs

  • The project file grew more than 20 % in a month. Something that is not programme is getting in.
  • The agent opens a session proposing something that was already decided and written. It did not read the map: the mandatory-reading hook failed.
  • The "what it is not" section is empty or says "nothing in particular". Every system has boundaries; if they are not written, the model will cross them.

Step 3 — Size before designing the architecture (step contract)

Phase: start (once; redone if the business changes scale). Form: conversation (the agent computes and presents options; the owner chooses). Size: always mandatory, but proportional: in a small project the sizing can be half a page and a single decision ("it fits in SQLite for years"). What it cannot be is skipped, because the question "how much will it grow?" costs five minutes and the wrong answer costs weeks.

What it is for

So that structural decisions — which database engine, whether the interface is synchronous or asynchronous, how much concurrency — are made with numbers and not with taste. It is the most expensive step to skip: in Kikan the system started on JSON files, and when it was measured it turned out to generate 3.4 million rows per year; changing the interface afterwards cost a whole stage of re-engineering.

A key point, which surfaced while writing this contract: there is data only the business has (how many entities, how many events per entity, how many operators at peak, how long to retain) and the model cannot infer it. What it can infer is the derived data. That is why the prompt demands that it evaluate whether the primary data is complete and ask for what is missing, instead of estimating it with a confident face. The one who knows what is missing in order to compute is the model, not a form.

And a second idea worth naming: asymmetric decisions. There are decisions that cost very little if you make them and they turn out unnecessary, and a great deal if you do not make them and they turn out necessary. The asynchronous persistence interface from line one is the canonical example: if the engine ends up synchronous, you wasted one await; if it ends up asynchronous and you did not plan for it, you rewrite everything. Those decisions are always made when they are cheap, even if they are not needed today.

1. Required context

  • Volume data survey from the business, named generically: main entities and their growth, events per entity, simultaneous operators and peak time, data retention period, exceptional loads (campaigns, month-end), expected growth. The survey is incomplete by nature; the agent evaluates and asks.
  • If a previous system exists: read access to its database to get real numbers instead of estimates.
  • PROJECT.md (step 2).

2. Prompts

Opening:

Before proposing any architecture we are going to size the system. Read PROJECT.md and the volume survey in docs/VOLUME_SURVEY.md. Distinguish primary data (facts only the business knows) from derived data (what can be computed from the primary data). Evaluate whether the primary data is complete enough to compute what follows; if any primary figure is missing, or is marked as an assumption, ask me for all the missing figures before going on. Never estimate a primary figure. If a previous system can be queried, use its real numbers and state where each came from. With the primary data complete, compute, showing the arithmetic: rows per year for the three largest tables, peak concurrency, data size at five years, and what breaks first if everything is multiplied by ten. Then present three options for persistence and concurrency, each with the cost of migrating to the others later. Do not choose: I choose. Do not write code. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for, clause by clause: to distinguish primary from derived (the distinction that prevents invention); to ask for the missing primary figures and never estimate them; to use real numbers from the previous system when there is one, with their source; to compute the derived figures showing the arithmetic, so that the owner can redo it; the "times ten" scenario, which is what reveals what breaks first; three options with the cost of migrating between them, because the decision is not only "which" but "how much does being wrong cost"; and not to choose or write code, because this step is about deciding, not building.

Closing:

I choose option [B]. Record in docs/SIZING.md: the primary data with its source, the derived data with its arithmetic, the three options, the chosen one and why, and the decisions we are taking "just in case" even though they are not needed today (for example: the persistence interface is asynchronous from line one, even if the first engine is synchronous). Include the questions you asked and my answers.

What we are asking for: the full record, with sources and arithmetic; the chosen option and the reason; and the asymmetric decisions written down, because if they stay implicit they are not honoured.

3. Expected result

A file docs/SIZING.md with:

  • Date and who decided.
  • Table of primary data: value, source (reported by the business / measured in the previous system), date. None with source "assumption".
  • Table of derived data: value, which primary figures it comes from, the arithmetic.
  • The "times ten" scenario: what breaks first.
  • The three options, numbered, with the cost of migrating between them.
  • The chosen one and the reason, in one or two sentences.
  • The asymmetric decisions (cheap if you are wrong, very expensive if you do not make them), listed.
  • The questions the agent asked and the answers.

4. Completeness check

  • Is any primary figure sourced as "assumption" or unsourced? If so, the step is not climbed: the agent had to ask for it.
  • Does every derived figure show which primary figures it comes from and the arithmetic? Can you redo one calculation by hand and get the same result?
  • Are there three options and one chosen with a written reason?
  • Does it say what happens if the volume is multiplied by ten?
  • Are the asymmetric decisions written down, or did they stay implicit?
  • Did the agent ask? If it presented numbers without a single question, be suspicious.

5. Warning signs

  • All three options lead to the same conclusion. The agent had already chosen and built the other two to comply.
  • A derived figure has more precision than the primary figures it comes from ("1,234,567 rows" from "about 5,000 families"). The precision is false.
  • The "times ten" scenario says "nothing happens". Something always happens; if it did not find it, it did not look.

Step 4 — The skeleton with its gates (step contract)

Phase: start (once). Form: one long shot, with the owner's visual approval at the end. Size: mandatory in every project that will have users. In a throwaway prototype it can be skipped; but if the prototype survives (they always survive), this step is the first thing to pay for.

What it is for

So that the system starts, is tested and is deployed EMPTY before it has any functionality. Discovering deployment at the end is very expensive: in Kikan it arrived a month after the start and cost five pending items in a row. If the skeleton is deployed in the first week, those blows land with zero functionality at stake.

The general idea behind it: the path to production is part of the product, not a chore at the end. A system that works on the programmer's machine and cannot be deployed is not a system; it is a demo. And the only way to know the path works is to walk it when there is still nothing to lose.

1. Required context

  • docs/SIZING.md (step 3): the chosen option defines the skeleton.
  • Environments survey: where it will run (cloud, own server), whether there is a test environment and a production one, who has access, how deployment is done today if anything exists.
  • The kind of project: new (greenfield), corrective, or evolution of something existing.

2. Prompts

Opening:

We are going to build the skeleton of the system, with no functionality at all. Read PROJECT.md, docs/SIZING.md and the environments survey. Evaluate whether you have what is needed for the skeleton to run in the test environment (access, URL, deployment method); if anything is missing, ask me before starting. Then build: the folder structure; the server and the UI starting up; the test structure with one smoke test that passes; continuous integration running that test suite on every push; and the deployment to the test environment. Declare the gates of the workflow (what must be green to move from one stage to the next) in docs/GATES.md. When done, take a screenshot of the empty UI running in the test environment and show it to me. Do not add any functionality "while you are at it". Reply in my language.

What we are asking for: to verify it has the access before starting (without that the skeleton stays on the agent's machine); the exact list of what a skeleton is (structure, startup, one test, continuous integration, deployment to test); the gates written down; a real screenshot of the test environment, which is the only valid evidence that it was deployed; and the explicit prohibition on adding functionality, because the model tends to "while I'm at it" and that breaks exact scope.

Closing:

Skeleton approved. Record in docs/STATUS.md the test environment URL, the command to run the tests and the command to deploy, and mark this step as done. Then run the deployment a second time and show me that the second run changed nothing.

What we are asking for: to leave the three commands where the next session will find them, and to prove that the deployment is repeatable (run it twice without breaking anything). That second run is the proof that the procedure is a procedure and not luck.

3. Expected result

  • The repository with the structure, the server and the screen starting up.
  • A smoke test in green and the suite running in continuous integration (with the link to the green run).
  • The deployment to the test environment working, with the URL, and run twice.
  • docs/GATES.md: the list of gates, each with the condition to pass.
  • A screenshot of the empty system running in the test environment.
  • docs/STATUS.md updated with the three commands (run, test, deploy).

4. Completeness check

  • Can you open the test environment URL and see the empty screen? (You do it, not the agent.)
  • Is there a link to a green continuous-integration run?
  • Was the deployment run twice, and did the second run break nothing?
  • Are the gates written with one verifiable condition each?
  • Is there any functionality you did not ask for? If so, the agent violated exact scope.

5. Warning signs

  • The skeleton "works locally" but the deployment "we'll see later". No: the deployment is the skeleton.
  • The screenshot is from the agent's machine, not the test environment. Ask for the URL.
  • The gates say things like "the code has good quality". A gate is a verifiable condition: "the suite is green", "the deployment to test worked".

Step 5 — The plan is the pending items (step contract)

Phase: loop (used with every change, throughout the project). Form: one shot to create it; then a routine every session. Size: mandatory in every project. It is the step that makes all the others traceable: without numbered pending items there is nowhere to hang the evidence.

What it is for

So that there is a single work queue, ordered and gated, which is the truth of the project. There is no "plan" on one side and "tasks" on the other: the pending-items list IS the plan. It prevents the specification that is overtaken in two weeks and goes stale, and the work nobody knows the reason for.

A well-written pending item has three things that an item on a to-do list usually lacks: where it comes from (the decision or request that originated it), how it is verified as done, and the evidence it was closed with. The first keeps the work from losing its reason; the second keeps it from being closed with "done"; the third is what the owner looks at instead of looking at the code.

1. Required context

  • The specification or the client's initial request, however incomplete.
  • The audits, if there have been any.
  • PROJECT.md and docs/STATUS.md.

2. Prompts

To create the register:

We are going to turn everything that must be done into numbered pending items. Read PROJECT.md, the client's request and whatever is in docs/STATUS.md. If the request is ambiguous on any point such that you cannot write a verifiable pending item for it, ask me; do not resolve it by assuming. Create docs/PENDING.md with one item per thing to do: number (PND-001 onwards), one-line title, what is requested, which decision originates it (or "client request, date"), how it will be verified as done, and status. Order them by the priority I gave you; if I gave none, propose an order and tell me why. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for: to turn the request into verifiable items and to ask when the request is not enough to write a verifiable one (ambiguity is resolved by asking, not by choosing); the fields of each item, in particular "how it is verified", which is what later allows closing it with evidence; and a proposed order with its reason, for the owner to confirm or change.

Routine for every session (goes in the working agreement or in PROJECT.md):

Before touching any code, open docs/PENDING.md, tell me which item you are taking and why. Every commit cites its pending item. A pending item is closed with written evidence (the test, the screenshot, the number), never with "done". If along the way you see something that is not on the list, add it as a new pending item; do not do it.

What we are asking for: the discipline of every session: announce what is taken, cite the item in every commit, close with evidence, and write down rather than do whatever shows up outside the list. That last clause is what protects exact scope when the model "sees something else".

3. Expected result

docs/PENDING.md (or whatever tool the project uses) with, per item:

  • Number, title, status (open / in progress / closed), opening and closing dates.
  • What is requested, in two or three sentences.
  • Origin: the decision or the request, with date.
  • How it is verified as done: the test, the screen, the number.
  • On closing: the evidence and the commit.

And the routine in the agreement or in PROJECT.md.

4. Completeness check

  • Does every item say how it is verified? An item without verification cannot be closed.
  • Are there "closed" items without evidence or commit? If so, reopen them.
  • Take three commits at random: do they cite their item?
  • Did something appear in the code that has no item? That is where the agent left the scope.
  • Did the agent ask you about the ambiguous points of the request, or resolve them alone?

5. Warning signs

  • An item has been "in progress" for more than three sessions. Either it is three items, or it is blocked by a decision nobody made (step 6).
  • More items were closed in a day than commits were made that day. They are being closed without evidence.
  • The list has more than fifty open items. It is not a plan, it is an inventory of anxieties: prioritise and archive.

Step 6 — Decisions are written next to the code (step contract)

Phase: loop (whenever a business rule or a structural decision appears). Form: short conversation: the agent detects that there is a decision, asks, and writes it down. Size: mandatory in every project. In a small one the register can be a ten-row table; what matters is that business rules have an owner.

What it is for

So that every business rule and every structural decision has an owner, a date and a place. If not, in six weeks nobody knows whether "approved plus balance goes back to the queue" was a client decision or an agent's idea. And it prevents the worst: the agent inventing domain rules because they seemed reasonable.

This has a consequence worth saying out loud: when the model runs into a gap in the rules, it fills it. It does not stop; it completes with what seems most likely, and it does so with the same naturalness with which it writes everything else. That is why the routine of this step is not "write down the decisions" but "stop and ask when one is missing". The second is the hard part, and the one that must be demanded in the agreement.

1. Required context

  • The conversation with the owner: it is the only context. The agent cannot decide business rules.
  • An agreed place where decisions are written: the commit message, the pending item's document, or a register docs/DECISIONS.md.

2. Prompts

Routine (goes in the working agreement):

Whenever implementing something requires a business rule that is not written down, or you must choose between two approaches that change what the user sees, stop and ask me, in prose, with your recommendation and the alternative. Do not implement the one you prefer. When I decide, write the decision with my name and the date in the commit message and in docs/DECISIONS.md, with the exact words I used. If I do not answer and you must move on, implement the most reversible option and mark it "decision by omission, pending confirmation", so that I can review it.

What we are asking for: to stop at an unwritten rule or a visible fork; to ask with a recommendation and an alternative (so that the owner can decide in ten seconds, not ten minutes); to record the decision in the owner's exact words, not an "improved" paraphrase; and, for when the owner does not answer, the most reversible option marked "by omission", which is what allows moving on without the decision disappearing.

Closing (each decision):

Rule: [exact wording]. Decided by me, [date]. Write it down and continue.

What we are asking for: the minimum format of a decision: the rule verbatim, who, when. Three things; without any of the three it is not a recorded decision.

3. Expected result

  • docs/DECISIONS.md: one line or paragraph per decision, with date, who decided, the verbatim sentence, the item or commit where it was applied, and whether it was "by omission".
  • In every commit that implements a business rule: the originating sentence ("rule from the owner: approved + balance = back to the queue", "owner decision 2026-08-10").

4. Completeness check

  • Open three commits that touch business rules: does each cite who decided and when?
  • Are there unconfirmed "by omission" decisions? Confirm or revert them; do not leave them.
  • Is there any rule in the system you do not recognise as yours? If so, the agent invented it: it is a failure of the step, written up as a pending item and reviewed.
  • Are the sentences verbatim or "improved" by the agent? They have to be yours.

5. Warning signs

  • A week of work went by and the decision register did not grow. Either the work was purely mechanical (rare) or the agent decided alone.
  • All recorded decisions are written in the same polished style. They are the agent's paraphrases, not your words.
  • A user of the system asks "why does it do this?" and nobody can find the decision.

Step 7 — Tests as locks, proved red (step contract)

Phase: loop (every change). Form: one shot; the agent executes and reports, the owner reads the report. Size: mandatory in every project. It is the heart of the method: if there is one step that cannot be negotiated, it is this one.

What it is for

A test you never saw fail, you do not know whether it tests anything. The rule here is not "write tests": it is "prove that the test turns red against the old code, and then green with the new one". It prevents tests that pass because they test nothing, and the green that hides a bug.

Why this matters more with a model than with a human programmer: the model writes the code and the test in the same session, with the same understanding. If it misunderstood the request, the test will test what it understood, and it will pass. The red against the old code is the only cheap way to check that the test is looking at what matters: if the old code passes it, the test does not tell before from after, and so it is useless.

1. Required context

  • The change already implemented (by default tests come after implementation; "test first" stays as an option for small, well-defined scopes).
  • The pending item that originates the change, with its "how it is verified".
  • The existing suite and the command to run it (step 4).

2. Prompts

Routine (goes in the working agreement; applied to every change):

To close this pending item I need evidence, not your word. (1) Write the test that proves what the item asks for. (2) Demonstrate that this test fails against the previous code: you may temporarily revert the change, or run the test against the old version, but show me the red output. (3) Show me the green output with the change. (4) Run the regression suite for the neighbouring items and show me it is still green. (5) Do not replace with mocks the very part you are trying to test. If testing end to end requires something you lack (data, access, an environment), ask me for it; do not simulate what you do not have. Finally, the commit message says "proved red against [what]" and the test counts.

What we are asking for, clause by clause: (1) the test; (2) the demonstrated red, output in view, which is the clause that distinguishes this method from "write tests"; (3) the green; (4) the neighbours' regression in the same session, because a change breaks things next to it; (5) no mocking of precisely what is being tested; and the completeness clause adapted to this step: if something is missing to test end to end, ask for it, do not simulate it. The commit message with the counts leaves the evidence where the code lives.

When the agent says it cannot be tested:

Explain why it cannot be tested, what would have to be added so that it can, and open a pending item for that. The change is not closed until it can be tested.

What we are asking for: that "it cannot be tested" is not the end of the conversation but the start of a pending item. Non-testability is debt, and debt is recorded.

3. Expected result

  • The new test in the repository, named after the item it closes.
  • In the agent's report and in the commit message: the red output (how many fail, against which version), the green output, and the regression in green with its counts.
  • If it was tested end to end (screen → backend), the evidence that the backend changed (the row, the record, the state).

4. Completeness check

  • Does the report show the red? With numbers: "4 of 8 fail against the old criterion". If it only shows green, the step is not climbed.
  • Did the regression run in the same session? How many tests, how many green?
  • Is any part "simulated" (mocked) right where the change is? Ask the agent what it mocked and why.
  • Does the commit cite the item and say "proved red"?
  • If the test is a screen test, does it also verify the effect on the backend?

5. Warning signs

  • All of the week's new tests passed green on the first run, and no red was ever shown. Either the agent is skipping the step or the tests test nothing.
  • The suite takes longer and longer and nobody knows why. Usually the end-to-end tests have been replaced by simulations "so it runs fast".
  • The agent reports "the tests pass" without saying how many. Always with numbers.

Step 8 — Producer/consumer symmetry, and the anti-pattern register (step contract)

Phase: loop (every change that touches a contract between two parts). Form: automatic within the loop; plus a file that grows with every hard lesson. Size: the symmetry is always mandatory; the producer/consumer map is optional in a single-module project, and mandatory as soon as there is a screen and a server apart.

What it is for

When you change one end (the server, the robot, the database schema), the other end (the screen, the counter, the consumer) has to change in the same commit. If not, sooner or later they diverge, and the user sees one number on one side and another number on the other (the 1,355 versus 1,234 case). And the mistakes we already paid for once are written down so we do not pay for them twice.

The general principle behind it: every list has a single owner. The columns of a table, the possible states of an order, the reasons something is waiting: one part of the system (the producer) defines them and every other part reads them from there. As soon as there are two copies of the same list, one of them is out of date; the only question is which.

1. Required context

  • A map of which parts of the system consume what: which screens read which services, which processes write which tables. If it does not exist, the first prompt builds it.
  • The anti-pattern register docs/GOTCHAS.md, even if empty.

2. Prompts

To build the map (once):

Build docs/PRODUCER_CONSUMER_MAP.md: for every contract in the system (a service, a table, a file format, a list of values), who produces it and who consumes it. If there are parts of the system you cannot access or do not understand, tell me instead of leaving them out of the map. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for: the inventory of contracts with both sides, and the completeness clause in its form for this step: what cannot be seen is declared, not omitted. A map with hidden gaps is worse than no map.

Routine (goes in the working agreement):

Before closing any change that touches a contract, look up every consumer in the map and update them in the same commit. Always prefer that the producer defines the list (columns, causes, states) and the consumer reads it, rather than each keeping its own copy. If the map does not have that relationship, add it.

What we are asking for: symmetry as a reflex (look up the other side before closing), the single-owner-per-list rule, and the map maintaining itself as it is used.

When something breaks for the second time:

This has happened to us before. Record it in docs/GOTCHAS.md with: how it smells (the early sign), the root cause, the fix, and how to detect it next time before it hurts. And add, if possible, a test or an automatic check that detects it.

What we are asking for: the fixed format of an anti-pattern (smell, root cause, fix, early detection) and, most importantly, the automatic check: an anti-pattern written down but not automated will repeat when the person or the session changes.

3. Expected result

  • docs/PRODUCER_CONSUMER_MAP.md: the list of contracts with producer and consumers.
  • In every commit that touches a contract: the consumers updated, named in the message.
  • docs/GOTCHAS.md: one entry per anti-pattern, with the four parts (smell, root cause, fix, how to detect it earlier), and the date of the first occurrence.

4. Completeness check

  • Pick a contract from the map and ask the agent to show you where each consumer reads it. If any has its own copy of the list, there is debt.
  • Is there a number shown on two different screens? Compare: are they equal?
  • Does every GOTCHAS entry have the four parts?
  • Did any mistake already in GOTCHAS repeat? If so, the automatic check is missing.

5. Warning signs

  • The same value is typed by hand in more than one file ("PENDING", "APPROVED" in the server and in the screen). Two copies; one will diverge.
  • A server change was closed with the server test green and nobody touched the screen.
  • GOTCHAS has entries without the "how to detect it earlier" part. They are anecdotes, not anti-patterns.

Step 9 — Measure before building, with an instrument that is itself verified (step contract)

Phase: loop (whenever a number feeds a decision). Form: one shot per measurement; sometimes a conversation when the hypothesis is the owner's. Size: proportional. In a small project, "measuring" can be counting with one command and writing the number down with its date; the ratchet and the query budget are for systems with volume. What does not change with size: no number that decides anything comes from an impression.

What it is for

So that every number that feeds a decision comes from a program with a test, and not from a text search, an impression, or "I think there are about a hundred". And so that the instrument itself is audited, because meters lie: in Kikan one did not see 84 calls with the counter at zero, and another said 27 when there were 44. The five rules are: a guarded meter; if the instrument does not exist, build it; audit in both directions; mark what is approximate; and the one who executes verifies the numbers of the one who planned.

There is a deep reason for this step to exist: the model is very good at producing claims with a number ("there are about 120 loops of this kind") and those claims sound exactly the same whether they were measured or estimated. The owner has no way to tell them apart by tone. The only way is to demand the instrument: "what did you measure it with? show me the command". If there is no command, it is an impression, however many decimals it has.

1. Required context

  • The hypothesis, from the owner or the agent: "I think the problem is X", "there must be about a hundred".
  • Access to what is to be measured: the code, the database read-only, the log.
  • The meters that already exist in the repository, if any.

2. Prompts

Opening:

Before building anything, we are going to measure. The hypothesis is: [X]. Write a meter (a script that lives in the repository) that measures [the quantity], and a test for the meter that checks it against a known case where the answer is known in advance. If measuring requires access or data you lack, ask me; do not approximate silently. If some part can only be approximated, mark it as approximate and tell me the margin. Run the meter, show me the number together with the command that produces it, and audit it in both directions: from the code to the measurement (is everything that should be counted, counted?) and from the measurement to the code (does everything counted actually exist?). Only then propose what to build. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for, clause by clause: the meter in the repository (not a loose command that gets lost); the meter's test against a known case (the guard); the completeness clause in its measuring form (ask for access, do not approximate silently); mark what is approximate with its margin; the number with the command that produces it, so that the owner can run it; the audit in both directions, which is what uncovered the two lying meters; and the order: first measure, then propose.

When there is a budget:

Set the budget for [queries / loops / time] on this path at the measured value plus a small margin, and protect it with a test that fails if it is exceeded. Tell me the number and the margin.

What we are asking for: that the budget be the measured value plus a little, not a round number, and that it has a test that fails when exceeded. That way a performance regression shows up in the suite and not in production.

When the one who planned gave numbers (rule five):

Before executing the plan, verify every number the plan asserts with the meter. If any does not match, stop and tell me; do not execute on a wrong number.

What we are asking for: that the one who executes does not trust the planner's numbers, even if it is the same model in another session. In Kikan, three of four claims in the plan were wrong; where there was an instrument, the number was exact.

3. Expected result

  • The meter in the repository (tools/measure_*.py, scripts/meter-*.mjs or similar), with its test.
  • The number, with the command that produces it and the date.
  • The two-direction audit, written in two or three lines.
  • What is approximate marked as such, with its margin.
  • If there is a budget: the value, the margin, and the test that protects it.
  • In the commit: "measured: [number] with [meter]".

4. Completeness check

  • Can you run the meter's command yourself and get the same number? (You run it; you do not need to understand it, only to execute it.)
  • Does the meter have a test? Against which known case?
  • Is there any number in the report without a meter behind it? Ask where it came from.
  • Is what is approximate marked?
  • If the plan carried numbers, did the agent verify them before executing and tell you which did not match?

5. Warning signs

  • A number changed between two reports without the code changing. One of the two was not measured.
  • The meter reads zero. It is almost never zero; it is almost always that the meter does not see something (in Kikan, twice).
  • The agent proposes to build "while measuring", to save time. The order is the method: first the number, then the plan.

Step 10 — Honest verification and proportional reporting (step contract)

Phase: loop (on closing every change). Form: routine; it is the shape every report from the agent must have. Size: mandatory in every project, without exception. It costs nothing and it is what lets the owner not read code.

What it is for

If the owner is not going to read code, the agent's report has to be trustworthy. Not optimistic, not "complete in appearance": trustworthy. This is the genuinely new thing about the method: the author of the code also manufactures the evidence, and does so with the same confidence in its voice when it is right and when it is wrong. That is why the evidence has to prove itself, and the report has to separate what was verified from what is believed.

One has to understand why the model tends to over-report. It is not bad faith: it was trained to be helpful and complete, and a report that says "I could not verify this" feels less helpful than one that says "done". So you have to explicitly ask for the bad news, the blind spots and what it did not do. If you do not ask, it will not give them to you, not because it hides them but because it does not consider them part of the answer.

1. Required context

  • The work done and the pending item that asked for it.
  • The working agreement (step 1), which already forbids announcing what was not done.

2. Prompts

Routine (goes in the working agreement; applies to every report):

When you report, separate three blocks: VERIFIED (what you tested, with the command and its output), BELIEVED (what you think works but did not test, and why not), and BLIND SPOTS (what you cannot see from where you are: another environment, real data, the user's browser). Cover every item I asked for, one by one, even when the answer is "I did not do it". No claim without its proof next to it. Do not say "done": say what is done and how you know it. If something went wrong, say it first. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for, clause by clause: the three blocks, which are the structure that makes a report honest (what was verified with its proof, what is believed with the reason it was not tested, what cannot be seen); item-by-item coverage, so that nothing gets "forgotten"; the proof next to every claim; the ban on a bare "done"; and the bad news first, because the model tends to leave it for the end or for never.

When the owner says "it is still broken":

Before diagnosing again, compare the version running where I see the problem with the version that contains your fix. Tell me both. If they differ, the problem is publication, not code.

What we are asking for: that on "still broken" it does not diagnose from scratch again but first compares versions. Half of the "still broken" reports are fixes that are in the repository but not in the place where the owner is looking.

3. Expected result

Every report from the agent with:

  • The three blocks (verified / believed / blind spots), even if one is empty.
  • One line per item requested, with its status.
  • For every "verified": the command and its output, or the test and its result, or the number and its meter.
  • What went wrong, at the beginning and not at the end.

4. Completeness check

  • Are the three blocks there? If "believed" and "blind spots" are always empty, the agent is not being honest: there is always something it did not see.
  • Pick one claim from the "verified" block at random: is its proof next to it? Can you reproduce it?
  • Does it cover every item you asked for, or did one "get forgotten"?
  • Is the bad news at the beginning?
  • Did it ever say a bare "done"? That is a violation of the agreement.

5. Warning signs

  • Ten reports in a row without a single blind spot. Nobody sees everything; the block is empty out of habit, not truth.
  • The "verified" block contains phrases like "it should work". That is "believed" in disguise.
  • The report is longer than the change. A proportional report says what needs saying; an inflated one is covering something with volume.

Step 11 — The periodic audit as input to the plan (step contract)

Phase: operation (every so often: on closing a large stage, before an important deployment, or every one or two months). Form: one long shot, ideally by an agent different from the one that builds. Size: mandatory before the first deployment to production, in any project; afterwards, proportional to the pace of change (a system that changes every week is audited every month; a stable one, every six).

What it is for

To look at the whole system with fresh eyes every so often, and to turn what is seen into pending items. The audit is not a report to be filed: it is plan. It prevents the hard-coded password nobody saw, the 264 queries reading whole tables, the known vulnerability in a dependency, the 2,679-line component. And it is QA independence taken one step further in: the one who audits is not the one who built.

On independence: no other company or other tool is needed. Another session, with another instructions file, that did not take part in the construction is enough. What is sought is that the auditor does not share the builder's assumptions. A model that built a module "knows" the hard-coded password was temporary; a model seeing it for the first time reports it as what it is.

1. Required context

  • The complete repository, read-only.
  • Production or the test environment, read-only (database, logs).
  • The previous audit, if there was one, to measure what changed.
  • The scope: integrity, security, performance, code quality, or all of them.

2. Prompts

Opening (to an agent different from the builder, if possible):

Audit this system for [scope]. Read-only: change nothing except writing your report to docs/AUDIT_[date].md. If auditing some part requires access you lack (the database, the log, an environment), tell me and mark that part as not audited; do not pass it as fine. Go through all of the code, not a sample. For every finding: where it is (file and line), what the risk is, what evidence proves it (the command, the query, the number), and its priority: P0 must be fixed now, P1 soon, P2 when possible. Give a grade to every dimension you audited. End with the list of P0 findings turned into proposed pending items. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for, clause by clause: read-only (the auditor does not fix; it proposes); the completeness clause in its auditing form: what could not be audited is declared, not approved by omission; all of the code and not a sample, because the serious findings tend to be where nobody looks; every finding with location, risk, evidence and priority, so that it is actionable without rereading the report; grades per dimension, to compare with the previous audit; and the P0 findings already turned into proposed pending items.

Closing (to the builder):

Read docs/AUDIT_[date].md. Open one pending item for every P0 and P1, citing the finding. If you disagree with any of them, tell me with the evidence against; do not ignore it.

What we are asking for: that the audit becomes pending items (otherwise it is a report that gets filed), and that disagreement is explicit and evidenced, not silent.

3. Expected result

  • docs/AUDIT_[date].md with: scope, what could and could not be audited (and why), the findings with location, risk, evidence and priority, a grade per dimension, and the list of proposed pending items.
  • In docs/PENDING.md: one item for every P0 and P1, citing the finding.

4. Completeness check

  • Does the report say what it could NOT audit? If it says it audited everything, be suspicious.
  • Pick two findings: do they have file and line, and evidence you can reproduce?
  • Does every P0 have its pending item opened before the audit is considered closed?
  • Can the grades per dimension be compared with the previous audit?
  • Did the same agent that built also audit? If so, ask for a second look at the P0 findings.

5. Warning signs

  • The audit found no P0. In a system older than three months that is almost never true; more likely the scope was small or the auditor shared assumptions.
  • The findings have no file line. They are opinions, not findings.
  • Two weeks went by and the P0 findings still have no pending item. The audit got filed.

Step 12 — Fail-closed, locks and zero silent harm (step contract)

Phase: operation (designed before the system acts on the world; revised with every new consequential action). Form: conversation: the agent lists the consequential actions, the owner confirms which get a lock and of what kind. Size: mandatory as soon as the system does anything irreversible (charge, send, delete, approve). A read-only system can skip it; the moment it gains its first button that acts on the world, this step becomes the most important one.

What it is for

So that the system, on doubt or failure, does NOT act, and says so. It sounds obvious and it is the opposite of what software does by default, which on doubt carries on. It prevents the double charge, the automatic approval without a human in the loop, the git add -A that uploads a credential, and deleting the client's data.

The principle has a name, "fail-closed", and comes from physical security: a security door that stays shut when the power is cut. The opposite, "fail-open", is the door that opens. In software most things are fail-open by default: if validation fails, the code continues; if the service does not answer, everything is assumed fine. The method flips the default: on doubt, no. And it applies both to the system and to the agent that operates it.

1. Required context

  • Survey of consequential actions, named generically: what the system does that charges, sends, approves, deletes, or notifies someone outside; what happens if it is done twice; what happens if it is done wrong; how it is undone. The business knows this; the agent evaluates whether the list is complete and asks.
  • Which actions the agent itself may take on the system (if it is embedded).

2. Prompts

Opening:

We are going to decide which actions of the system need a lock. Read the survey of consequential actions, and go through the code looking for everything that charges, sends, approves, deletes or notifies outwards. Compare the two lists: if the code has actions the survey does not mention, or the other way round, ask me before going on. For every action propose: whether it is reversible, which lock it gets (explicit confirmation, double safety, dry-run mode, daily limit, a human in the loop), and what the way back is if it goes wrong. General rule: on any doubt or failure along the way, the action is NOT executed and the reason is logged. Present me the table; I confirm lock by lock. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for: the comparison between what the business said and what the code does (that is where the actions nobody remembered appear); the completeness clause in its form for this step: differences between the two lists are asked about, not resolved; for every action, reversibility, lock and way back; and the general fail-closed rule written in the prompt, so that it ends up in the table and in the code.

Closing:

I confirm locks [list]; change [X] to [Y]. Implement them with one test per lock that proves that, under the doubt condition, the action does NOT happen (proved red against the version without the lock). Record the final table in docs/LOCKS.md. And for any new automation: dry-run mode first, logging what it would have done, until I enable it.

What we are asking for: the test per lock proved red (without the lock, the action happened; with the lock, it does not), which is step 7 applied to safety; the final table; and the dry-run rule for every new automation, which is what lets you see what it would have done before letting it do it.

3. Expected result

  • docs/LOCKS.md: one row per consequential action: what it does, whether it is reversible, the lock, the way back, who approved it and when.
  • One test per lock, proved red, verifying that on doubt the action does not happen.
  • New automations starting in dry-run mode, with the "would have done" log.
  • If the agent is embedded: its permissions written down (what it can read, what it can change, what needs approval).

4. Completeness check

  • Is every action that charges, sends, approves or deletes in the table? Think of one that is not and ask.
  • Does every lock have its red test? ("Without the lock, the action happened; with the lock, it does not.")
  • Does every irreversible action have a written way back? If it does not, it needs double safety.
  • Did the new automations start in dry-run mode? Did you see the "would have done" log before enabling them?
  • What happens if the embedded agent tries something outside its permissions? Ask it to try and to show you the rejection in the log.

5. Warning signs

  • An automation went from "does not exist" to "runs on its own" without a dry-run period.
  • The log has no "action not executed due to doubt" entry at all. Either the system never doubts (impossible) or when it doubts it carries on.
  • A lock can be bypassed with a "for testing" parameter. That parameter will end up set in production.

Step 13 — Continuous integration, environments, secrets and deployment as part of the product (step contract)

Phase: operation (built on the skeleton of step 4; revised with every infrastructure change). Form: one shot to set it up; routine for every deployment. Size: mandatory in every project with users. In a small one, "continuous integration" can be a single GitHub action and "environments" can be two folders on the same server; what is not negotiable is that the tests run by themselves, that the configuration is not in the repository, and that there is a way back.

What it is for

So that reaching production is a reproducible package: the tests run ONCE, the configuration lives outside the repository, and secrets do not wander around. It prevents the tests running three times per commit (2,000 minutes of quota in 16 days), the database key printed in a transcript, the CORS that only knew the first domain, and the configuration overwritten by the test suite.

On secrets there is a rule worth adopting as a reflex, with no exceptions: a secret that was seen in a chat, a log or a commit is no longer a secret. It does not matter whether "nobody saw it" or "it was deleted afterwards". It is rotated. Rotation costs ten minutes; finding out whether someone used it is impossible.

1. Required context

  • Environments and access survey, generically: which environments exist (test, production), where they run, who has access, how something is promoted from one to the other, which secrets exist and where they live today, how much continuous-integration quota there is. The agent evaluates whether it is enough and asks.
  • The skeleton with its continuous integration and its deployment to the test environment (step 4).

2. Prompts

Opening:

We are going to close the path to production. Read the environments and access survey. Evaluate whether you have what is needed to deploy to production safely (access, domain, where the secrets live, how to roll back); if anything is missing, ask me. Then set up: (1) continuous integration runs the full test suite exactly once per commit; (2) the deployment waits for that run's result and only packages and uploads, without running tests again; (3) each environment's configuration lives on the server (environment file or vault), never in the repository; (4) a written procedure to promote from the test environment to production, which can be run twice without breaking anything; (5) the rollback, tested; (6) an automatic check that prevents committing a secret. Document all of it in docs/DEPLOYMENT.md so that a person can do it by hand without you. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for, clause by clause: the access before starting; the tests exactly once per commit (running them three times is what burned the quota); the deployment that only packages; the configuration outside the repository; promotion as a repeatable procedure; the rollback tested (a rollback that was never tested does not exist); the lock against secrets in commits; and the documentation for a person, which is step 14 applied to infrastructure.

Routine for every deployment:

Before touching production: the test environment is green with the same version; the rollback is ready; and tell me what changes in the configuration. After deploying: show me the version running in production and an end-to-end check on production, read-only.

What we are asking for: the three preconditions (test green with the same version, rollback ready, configuration changes announced) and the two pieces of evidence afterwards (the version running and an end-to-end check that writes nothing).

If a secret showed up in the chat or in a commit:

That secret is compromised: rotate it now, tell me everywhere else it was used, and add the check that would have caught it.

What we are asking for: immediate rotation, an inventory of where else it was, and the check that would have detected it, so that next time it does not reach the chat.

3. Expected result

  • Continuous integration running exactly once per commit, with a link to a run.
  • The deployment that waits for integration and only packages; measured: how long it takes before and after.
  • The configuration outside the repository; in the repo, only an example without values.
  • docs/DEPLOYMENT.md: how to deploy, promote and roll back, step by step, for a person.
  • The rollback tested at least once, with its evidence.
  • The anti-secrets check active (an attempt to commit one, rejected).
  • A record of continuous-integration quota consumed per month.

4. Completeness check

  • How many times does the suite run per commit? It has to be one. Ask for the link.
  • Is there any real configuration value (a key, a password, an internal URL) in the repository? Ask the agent to search and show you the empty search.
  • Could you follow docs/DEPLOYMENT.md yourself, without the agent? Read it with that question.
  • Was the rollback tested? When? What evidence is there?
  • Was any secret pasted in the chat during the project? If so, was it rotated?
  • Do you know how much continuous-integration quota is left this month?

5. Warning signs

  • The production deployment is done "by hand, quickly, because it is a small change". Small changes are the ones that break production, because nobody tests them.
  • The example configuration file has values that look real. They probably are.
  • Nobody can say when the rollback was last tested.

Step 14 — Living documentation and manuals a person can follow without AI (step contract)

Phase: operation (born with the system; maintained with every change). Form: routine; plus one long shot when the manuals are generated. Size: proportional. A small system needs, at minimum, the infrastructure and recovery manual (how to bring it up, how to restore); the user manual can be one page. What does not change: if the person who built it disappears, another must be able to carry on.

What it is for

So that the system can be used, maintained and recovered without the agent and without the person who built it. If, on the day the agent is not there, it cannot be deployed, it is not finished. In the classical method documentation was expensive and aged; here regenerating it is free, so the rule becomes "in the same commit" and "reproducible by hand". It prevents the stale document that caused a commit in the wrong repository, and the project file turned into a history.

A quality criterion for the recovery manual, usable to judge it without being technical: a trusted person who does not program must be able to follow it, at three in the morning, with the system down and the agent offline. If any step says "consult the developer" or "as appropriate", that step is incomplete.

1. Required context

  • Who the readers of each manual are: the end user, the maintainer, the infrastructure operator. The business knows; the agent asks if it is unclear.
  • The system running in the test environment, to take real screenshots.
  • docs/DEPLOYMENT.md (step 13) and docs/LOCKS.md (step 12), which are input.

2. Prompts

Routine (goes in the working agreement):

Every change that modifies what the user sees, what the operator does, or how the system is deployed, updates the corresponding manual in the same commit. If you do not know which manual, ask.

What we are asking for: the same-commit rule, which is the only one that keeps a manual up to date, and the question when it is unclear which manual it goes to.

To generate the manuals:

Generate three manuals from the system as it is today in the test environment: the user manual (screen by screen, with real screenshots, in the user's language, no jargon); the maintenance manual (how it is built, where everything is, how to run the test suite, how to add a module); and the infrastructure and recovery manual (how to deploy, how to roll back, how to restore a backup, what to do if it goes down; written for a person who has to do it by hand, without AI, at three in the morning). If for any section you need something only the business knows (who is responsible for what, which phone number to call), ask me; do not leave a gap and do not invent. Each manual carries a date and the system version. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for: the three manuals with their readers; real screenshots from the test environment (not drawings or descriptions); the three-in-the-morning criterion for the recovery manual; the completeness clause in its documenting form: what only the business knows is asked for, and no gap is left and nothing is invented; and date and version, so that it is known which system each manual corresponds to.

3. Expected result

  • docs/USER_MANUAL.md (or HTML), with real, embedded screenshots.
  • docs/MAINTENANCE_MANUAL.md.
  • docs/INFRASTRUCTURE_AND_RECOVERY_MANUAL.md: step by step, for a person.
  • Each with a date and the version of the system it documents.
  • In the commits that change screens, operation or deployment: the manual updated.

4. Completeness check

  • Open the user manual at a random screen: does the screenshot match what is in the test environment today?
  • Could you, or a trusted person who does not program, follow the recovery manual? Read the "it went down" section: does it say what to do, in order, with the commands?
  • Pick a recent commit that changed a screen: did it touch the manual?
  • Is there a gap like "[to be completed]" or "consult the person in charge"? That is where the agent had to ask.
  • Does every manual say which system version it is valid for?

5. Warning signs

  • The user manual has screenshots of a version that no longer exists. It was generated once and not maintained: the same-commit rule is not in the agreement or is not being applied.
  • The recovery manual begins with "open a session with the agent". If the agent is a prerequisite, it is not a manual for a person.
  • Nobody outside the builder ever read the recovery manual. Ask someone to follow it in the test environment; whatever stops them is what needs fixing.

Step 15 — The agent inside the system, and the retrospective with numbers (step contract)

Phase: operation (once the system is in production) and closure (returns to step 0 for the next project). Form: two parts: a conversation to define the resident agent's scope, and one long shot for the retrospective. Size: the resident agent is optional (many systems do not need one; those whose users ask for support do). The retrospective is mandatory in every project, even if it is one page: without it the method does not learn.

What it is for

Two things that close the same cycle. First: the agent stays resident in production, with a bounded scope, to diagnose, operate and support without the builder having to be there. Second: the method itself is reviewed with numbers, and what is learned changes the ladder. It prevents the agent operating without a map of the system, and the method repeating itself unchanged project after project without learning (the multi-agent crew was killed by a retrospective: 60 dollars and 73 minutes versus 90 minutes).

On the resident agent, the rule that orders everything: it acts on someone's behalf. Its credential is mapped to a responsible person, and everything it does is logged under that person's name. It is not an anonymous system account: it is an employee with a boss. That defines its scope (what that person authorised) and its traceability (who answers for what it did).

1. Required context

For the resident agent:

  • What the agent may do in production and what not: read, diagnose, change configuration, execute actions. The owner decides.
  • How the agent identifies itself to the system (its own credential, mapped to a responsible person).
  • Whom it notifies when it finds something it cannot resolve (tickets, email).

For the retrospective:

  • The project's incidents, the costs (tokens, quota, the owner's time), the pending items that took the most rounds, and the dates.

2. Prompts

For the resident agent (opening):

We are going to leave you resident in production. First, build the map of the system you will need in order to operate it: modules, environments, where each configuration lives, which actions are consequential (read docs/LOCKS.md), whom to notify. If there are parts of the system you cannot see from where you are, tell me; the map must not have hidden gaps. Then propose your scope in three levels: read-only and diagnosis; configuration changes with my approval; consequential actions behind locks. I choose the level. Your credential acts as the person who created it: everything you do is logged under that person's name. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for: the map of the system before operating (operating without a map is the failure this step prevents); the completeness clause in its form: gaps in the map are declared, not covered up; the three scope levels so that the owner chooses one and not a "depends"; and the credential-on-someone's-behalf rule.

For the resident agent (closing):

Level [X]. Write in docs/RESIDENT_AGENT.md the map, the scope, the credential (never its value), and the procedure to request my approval. Then prove, with a real attempt recorded in the log, that something outside your scope is rejected.

What we are asking for: the resident agent's document (without the credential's value, ever), and the red proof of the scope: a real, logged attempt to do something beyond the level, rejected. It is step 7 applied to permissions.

For the retrospective:

[The stage / the project] is over. Do the retrospective with numbers, not impressions: what it cost (tokens, quota, my time), how many pending items, which ones took the most rounds and why, which incidents happened and which step of the ladder each falls into, and which step failed or was missing. If you lack the data for any figure (my time, for instance), ask me for it or mark it as an estimate; do not invent it. End with a concrete proposal to change the ladder: which step is modified, how, and which incident justifies it. Write it to docs/RETROSPECTIVE_[date].md. Reply in my language.

What we are asking for: sourced numbers (costs, items, rounds, incidents); every incident mapped to a step, because an incident that falls into none is a missing step; the completeness clause for the figures it lacks (ask, or mark as estimate); and at least one concrete proposal to change the ladder, because a retrospective that changes nothing was not a retrospective.

3. Expected result

  • docs/RESIDENT_AGENT.md: map of the system, chosen scope, credential mapped to a person, approval procedure, and the rejection proof recorded in the log.
  • docs/RETROSPECTIVE_[date].md: the numbers with their source, the incidents mapped to steps, and at least one proposal to change the ladder with its justification.

4. Completeness check

  • Does the map of the system say what the agent can NOT see? If it does not, it has hidden gaps.
  • Did you test (or see in the log) that an action outside the scope is rejected?
  • Does the agent's credential act on behalf of an identifiable person?
  • Does the retrospective have sourced numbers, or adjectives? ("It cost a lot" is not a number.)
  • Does every incident fall into a step? If one falls into none, a step is missing: that is the change proposal.
  • Is there at least one concrete proposal to change the ladder? If the retrospective changes nothing, it was not a retrospective.

5. Warning signs

  • The resident agent "needs" a higher permission level every week. Check whether what it asks for is to solve the system's problems or its own limitations.
  • The retrospective says everything went well. Nothing ever goes entirely well; it is written for the owner, not for the method.
  • Two consecutive projects had the same incident and the ladder did not change between them. Step 15 is not being closed: the retrospective is written and not applied.
