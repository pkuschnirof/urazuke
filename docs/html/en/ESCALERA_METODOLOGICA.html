The name

Urazuke (裏付け) is a Japanese word for the backing a claim has: what holds it up from behind (literally, "the lining at the back"). The verb urazukeru means "to back with proof". Pablo chose it on 17 October 2026 ("Let's go with Urazuke"), after the name check ruled out Hashigo, Kaidan and Shōko because of collisions with existing companies and projects. The name is never translated; the subtitle, "the evidence method", is: el método de la evidencia, o método da evidência, la méthode de la preuve, die Nachweis-Methode, il metodo del riscontro, 裏付けの方法, 验证方法. In this document, "the ladder" is the structure of the method — its sixteen steps — and "Urazuke" is the whole method.

What is this about?

Pablo put it clearly: he does not want to teach a tool, he wants to teach the method. The idea is that anyone, with any artificial-intelligence tool that can program, can "vibecode" (that is, build software by conversing with an AI instead of typing the code by hand) and that what comes out is robust, ready for production, and — this is the important part — without the project owner having to review a single line of code.

It sounds ambitious, and it is. But we have something almost nobody has: three real systems that were built exactly this way and that work today with real clients. So the question is not "can it be done?" but "what did we do, in what order, and which of all that is what really made the difference?".

This ladder is the answer to that question. And it will serve as the basis for three things: the method's documentation, the practice the students will do, and the manuals. Later on, we will also export it as a "project execution flow" inside Forge, so that the tool guides the method instead of one having to remember it.

AI does not interrupt the method: it continues it

This is the thesis that opens the document, and we put it first because it disarms the most common objection before it appears: "how are you going to trust code written by a machine?".

The answer is that the industry stopped trusting the author of the code twenty years ago. When the bulk of writing moved to junior, outsourced or rotating teams, line-by-line review became impossible to sustain. And what grew in its place is everything we now take for granted: automated tests, continuous integration, acceptance criteria, pipelines that let nothing through without green, the "shift left" of testing, quality control as an independent function. That was systems engineering's answer to "the one who writes is not trustworthy": do not read their code, demand evidence from them.

So putting an artificial-intelligence model to write is not a leap into the void. It is swapping an author we no longer trusted for another we do not trust either, and for whom we already had the verification apparatus built. That should reassure, not worry. And on top of that the new author is cheaper, faster, and — unlike the junior — does not take offence when you ask it to prove red.

That is why this method leans, without disguise, on classical systems engineering. The skeleton is the usual one: survey, requirements, sizing, design, construction, testing, deployment, operation, audit. It could not be otherwise, because the problems are the usual ones: not knowing how much data there will be, not knowing what the client wants, not knowing whether what was built is what was asked for. AI did not change the problems; it changed who does each part and at what cost. Even the eligibility criterion X2 (see step 0) is classical engineering: testability as a condition of the contract.

What is old and what is new in this

The old, which it is worth saying is old: the skeleton of stages, the independence of the one who verifies from the one who builds, tests as evidence, documentation as part of the product, the survey before the design.

What does change, and it is where our part is, are three things:

  1. Where the human's effort goes. In the classical method the human writes the specification, writes the code, and another human reviews it. Here the human does only two things: decides, and verifies artefacts. Everything else — drafting, building, testing, measuring, documenting — the model does. That is why every step ends in a "completeness check without reading code": it is the exact place where the human remains indispensable, and no other.
  1. The author also manufactures the evidence. This is the only genuinely new point, and it is the nuance that justifies three whole steps. The junior wrote dubious code, but did not manufacture the evidence: the tests and the measurements were built by someone else, or at least someone else looked at them. The model, on the other hand, produces the code and the test and the meter and the report, in the same session, with the same confidence in its voice when it is right and when it is wrong. We saw it: the meter that lied twice, the authentication error reported as "success", the three out of four claims that were wrong. So "review evidence instead of code" is still the right principle, but the evidence has to prove itself: the test seen red (step 7), the instrument with its guard (step 9), the report that separates verified from believed (step 10), the auditor different from the builder (step 11). It is not a new worry: it is the same discipline of quality-control independence, taken one step further in.
  1. The cost of artefacts. In the classical method documentation was expensive, so it was done once and it aged. Here regenerating it is free, so the rule becomes "in the same commit" and "reproducible by hand, without AI". What used to be an unattainable ideal becomes a gate.

For the course and for the public presentation, this defines the positioning honestly: it is not "the new way of programming"; it is systems engineering applied to a builder that does not get tired and cannot be believed. To those coming from systems it sounds familiar and lowers their distrust ("you already know how to do this"); to those coming from vibecoding it gives what they lack, which is precisely the skeleton. And it sets us apart from the tools that sell speed and leave out the whole skeleton.

A.4 The patterns that repeat in the three repositories

If one looks at the three repos together, there are ten things that appear over and over. These are, at bottom, the method:

  1. Blow → rule written the same day. No doctrine was born in the abstract. Each has a birth commit and an incident behind it.
  2. The plan is the pending items. Numbered items (PND-nnn) in the three repos; the specification is the starting point, the queue of pending items is the truth.
  3. The decision is written next to the code, with owner and date. "Rule from Pablo", "Pablo's decision 2026-08-10", the director's decisions, and in Kikan the "decision by omission" (when Pablo did not answer and the agent chose, it left it marked as such).
  4. The test is proved red. It is not enough for it to be green: it has to be shown that the test fails against the old code. If you never saw it red, you do not know whether it tests anything.
  5. Measure with an instrument, and verify the instrument. A script with a test that lives in the repo; the meter lied twice and the meter was fixed before the code.
  6. Fail-closed and locks. Nothing breaks silently; on doubt, do not act.
  7. Periodic audit → pending items. Full-scan in June, security in July, AUD-* in August, integrity and quality in October. The audit is not a report: it is plan.
  8. Documentation and manuals are born with the system, and maintained in the same commit as the change.
  9. The agent operates inside the system (diagnosis → operation → resident) with bounded permissions and approvals; the human approves, does not type.
  10. Retrospective with a number → redesign. The multi-agent crew was killed with 60 dollars and 73 minutes versus 90 minutes; the deployment was split with 16-27 minutes versus 11; and this ladder itself is exactly that.

And now the failures, because without them this would be propaganda and not a survey: the orphan turn of one hour forty-three (14 October); an authentication error reported as "success"; two administrator doors in 24 hours; 23 pushes in one day that burned the GitHub Actions quota; the database key printed in a transcript; a CLAUDE.md that turned into a 9,661-line changelog; the Forge mobile app losing its tabs; and the meters that lied twice. All of that happened. And all of it is in the ladder, because that is where the steps come from.

Part B — The ladder, version 3

How to read each step

Each step has seven parts, always the same, so that they can be compared:

  • Intention: what it seeks, in one sentence.
  • Inputs: what has to exist beforehand in order to climb it.
  • Artefact: what is left written or executable once it is climbed. Always something concrete: a file, a test, a script.
  • Gate: what has to be true to move to the next one. If it is not met, you do not climb.
  • Who decides: the project owner, the agent, or nobody because it is automatic.
  • Failure it prevents: the real blow that originated it. This is what makes it credible.
  • Evidence: the commit or document where it is.

Two important clarifications. First: the ladder is neutral with respect to the tool. Every artefact is a file, a test or a script; none requires Forge. Forge automates them (the gates, the commands, the pending-items register), but the step exists without Forge. Second: the steps are not strictly sequential. There are three phases: steps 0 to 4 are the start and are climbed once per project; 5 to 10 are the loop that repeats with every change; and 11 to 15 are operation, once the system is alive. Step 15 closes the cycle and returns to 0 for the next project.

How each step is executed: the contract

The seven-part card says what each step seeks and what it leaves. But a step is fully understood only when you see how it is asked for, what has to come back and how I know it came back complete. That is why each step has, besides the card, a contract in its own file inside peldanos/, always with the same five sections:

  1. Required context — named generically ("volume data survey", "read access to the previous system", "the list of consequential actions"), without going down into the detail of systems engineering. Surveys are incomplete by nature; we do not try to make them complete with a form.
  2. Prompt(s) — what is given to the model, verbatim. The opening one and, when the step is a conversation, the closing one. All carry the same clause, which is the heart of the matter: the model evaluates whether the context is enough; if it is not, it asks for everything missing before going on; and it does not estimate or invent a fact only the business has. The one who knows what is missing in order to compute is the model, not a form.
  3. Expected result — the detailed characteristics of the artefact: which file, which sections, what each must contain, what cannot be missing.
  4. Completeness check — the list of checks the owner does on the artefact, without reading code. If one comes out "no", the step is not climbed.
  5. Warning signs — two or three symptoms that the step was climbed badly even though it looks climbed. They are the ones seen weeks later, when it already hurts.

And in the header of each contract, besides the phase and the form, the size: whether the step is mandatory in every project or proportional, and what "proportional" means in a small one.

The prompts are in English. It is a decision (D-013) and it has three reasons: the same content in Spanish takes between 30 and 50 % more tokens; the models' instruction-following is better calibrated in English, so an English prompt tolerates brevity better without becoming ambiguous; and the vocabulary of the trade (test, commit, rollback, mock) is English. Under each prompt goes the explanation, in the reader's language, of what we are asking for and why each clause is there: the prompt is a single piece identical in the eight versions of the document; the explanation is what gets translated. And all of them end with "Reply in my language", because the prompt is in English but the conversation need not be.

Those sixteen contracts are, moreover, what later gets exported to Forge: today Forge injects a doctrine when a gate is crossed; the step contract is that doctrine, and the prompt is what is injected. Writing them for the course and writing them for the Forge flow is the same work.

Step 0 — Can this project be run this way? (eligibility)

This step has a particular history. Pablo said it on 17 October: there is something prior to everything, which is in no repository because it always arrived already decided — the decision of whether a project is to be run with this method or not. It is a criterion that lived in his head, and that we are going to elaborate together. So everything that follows in this step is partly confirmed by him and partly my hypothesis to start the conversation. I distinguish them carefully.

  • Intention: to decide whether a project is to be run with this method, and with an agent at the front, or not, BEFORE opening the repository. Because if you enter a project that does not qualify, the method degrades into "programming with AI" and then the owner does end up reading code.
  • Inputs: the client's request, the business domain, the data, the technology, the available environments, and who the owner will be.
  • Artefact: a one-page eligibility sheet, with the criteria scored and the decision: go, no-go, or go under conditions.
  • Gate: the exclusionary criteria are all green, and the score of the weighted ones exceeds the threshold (a threshold we have not yet defined).
  • Who decides: today, Pablo. The idea is that the method lets another person decide it with the same sheet.
  • Failure it prevents: getting into a project where the agent cannot verify itself, or where there is no domain owner who decides, and ending up with the human reviewing code line by line.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-00-elegibilidad.md.

Step 1 — The working agreement (the rules before the code)

Before writing a single line, human and agent agree on how they are going to work. It looks like a formality, and it is not: it is what prevents 80 % of the frustrations of month one.

  • Intention: to set how the human and the agent work before the first line of code.
  • Inputs: nothing; it is the project's first file.
  • Artefact: a standards-of-quality document (in Forge it is called SQ.md, but it can be called whatever you like) saying, at minimum: the human's time is sacred (it is not spent on things the agent can resolve alone); zero technical debt, that is, when something breaks it is fixed at the root and the regression is added in the same session; the scope is exact, no more and no less than asked; the user's language is spoken; the documentation is updated in the same commit; and nothing is ever announced as done that was not done.
  • Gate: the agreement is in the repository and is read every session.
  • Who decides: the owner. The agent can propose changes, but does not edit the agreement on its own.
  • Failure it prevents: the agent that "helps" by expanding the scope because it seemed a good idea; the one that leaves debt "for later" (and later never comes); and the one that reports as done what it believes it did.
  • Evidence: 2219f7a (25 May); rules SQ 1 to 9 in src/chat/server.ts; docs/SQ.md, sections 0.1 to 0.11.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-01-contrato-de-convivencia.md.

Step 2 — The project definition and mandatory reading

Every agent session starts from zero: it does not remember yesterday. So you have to give it a map, and you have to force it to read it before touching anything.

  • Intention: that every session starts knowing what the project is, what state it is in and which decisions have already been made.
  • Inputs: the agreement (step 1) and the client's request.
  • Artefact: an operational project file (PROJECT.md or CLAUDE.md), and here an emphasis: SHORT. It is the programme, not the history. Plus a living-state file (in Forge, .forge/sumi-context.md) with the current specification, the slice being worked on, the recent decisions and the open doubts. And a decision memory.
  • Gate: the agent reads the map before touching code. In Forge this is an explicit rule: "read the knowledge graph first".
  • Who decides: the owner defines; the agent keeps it up to date.
  • Failure it prevents: two, very concrete ones. One: throwaway work from not having read the map (it happened to me on 12 June and cost an afternoon). Two: Kikan's CLAUDE.md that grew to 9,661 lines and 789 kilobytes, impossible for anyone to read — the operational file and the casebook have to be different files.
  • Evidence: SQ 1.1 (mandatory reading); memory feedback_read_knowledge_graph_first; AUDITORIA_CALIDAD_CODIGO_2026_10_15.md.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-02-definicion-y-lectura.md.

Step 3 — Size before designing the architecture

This is the step that costs the most to skip, and we know it because we skipped it.

  • Intention: that structural decisions (which database engine, whether the interface is synchronous or asynchronous, how much concurrency) are made with numbers and not with taste.
  • Inputs: a volume data survey done with the business (entities, events, operators at peak, retention, exceptional loads), incomplete by nature; and read access to the previous system if it exists. There is data that only the business has and the model cannot infer: that is why the prompt demands that it evaluate whether it is complete and ask for what is missing, instead of estimating it with a confident face.
  • Artefact: a sizing record with FIXED options (not an open list: three or four concrete options) and the chosen one. In Forge it is gate G9. And the "asymmetric" decisions declared explicitly — for example, "the persistence interface is asynchronous from line one even if the first engine is synchronous", because if you are wrong it costs little and if you do not do it it costs a fortune.
  • Gate: the sizing is recorded before building the skeleton.
  • Who decides: the owner chooses among the fixed options; the agent presents them with numbers.
  • Failure it prevents: Kikan's. It started with storage in JSON files, and when it was measured it turned out the system would generate 3.4 million rows per year. Changing the persistence interface afterwards cost stage E6 of the re-engineering: three whole test suites moved to asynchronous, more than a week.
  • Evidence: 0bd1b05 (sizing, 11 October); 7391cc5 (persistence interface, 13 October); SQ 1.2 and 1.3; docs/doctrine/sizing.md (the "four holes", plus the fifth).
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-03-dimensionar.md.

Step 4 — The skeleton with its gates

Before the system does anything useful, it has to start, be tested and be deployed EMPTY. Yes, empty. Because discovering deployment at the end is very expensive.

  • Intention: to have a system that starts, is tested and is deployed with no functionality yet.
  • Inputs: the sizing (step 3) and the kind of workflow (new project, correction, or evolution of something that already exists).
  • Artefact: the skeleton with continuous integration green, the test structure built, a deployment to a test environment that works, and gates G1 to G9 declared.
  • Gate: the skeleton is green, continuous integration runs, and the deployment to the test environment works with zero functionality.
  • Who decides: the agent executes; the owner approves visually (with a screenshot or an automated test that produces one).
  • Failure it prevents: in Kikan the deployment arrived on 23 September, a month after the start, and between 30 September and 4 October there were five pending items in a row just to make the deployment work properly. If the skeleton had been deployed empty in the first week, those five blows would have landed with zero functionality at stake.
  • Evidence: docs/doctrine/gates.md; workflow-kinds.md (new project, steps 6 of 18); the testing policy, "scaffold gate green"; Kikan's ci.yml, 28 August.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-04-esqueleto.md.

Step 5 — The plan is the pending items

Here is an idea that, once you grasp it, you never let go of: there is no "plan" on one side and "task list" on the other. The pending-items list IS the plan.

  • Intention: a single work queue, ordered and gated, that is the truth of the project.
  • Inputs: the initial specification, the audits, the client's requests, and the blows.
  • Artefact: a register of numbered pending items (PND-001, PND-002…), each with a title, a status, the decision that originated it and the test that closes it. And every commit cites its pending item.
  • Gate: no change goes in without a pending item; and the item is closed with evidence, not with "done".
  • Who decides: the owner prioritises; the agent proposes and executes in order.
  • Failure it prevents: the specification that is "overtaken" and goes stale within two weeks (it happened to Authorizations with its initial design document); and the work that, six months later, nobody knows why it was done.
  • Evidence: 4fc82c6 (6 June); docs/PENDING_REGISTRY_PLAN.md; 641 pending items in Authorizations; 19 pending-item documents in Kikan; memory project_pending_registry_is_plan.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-05-pendientes.md.

Step 6 — Decisions are written next to the code

Every business rule and every structural decision has to have an owner, a date and a place where it is written. Otherwise, in six weeks nobody knows whether "approved plus balance goes back to the queue" was a client decision or an agent's idea.

  • Intention: that every domain rule or structural decision has an owner, a date and a place.
  • Inputs: the conversation with the owner.
  • Artefact: the decision written in the commit, in the pending item's document, or in a decision register. And when the owner did not answer and the agent had to choose, it is marked as "decision by omission" so that it can be reviewed.
  • Gate: a business rule does not enter the code without its originating sentence.
  • Who decides: the owner. Always. No exceptions.
  • Failure it prevents: arguing six weeks later about why something is the way it is; and, worse, the agent inventing domain rules because they seemed reasonable.
  • Evidence: 0ae3ae6 (pnd550), with the text "rule from Pablo" and "Pablo's decision 2026-08-10" inside the commit; docs/DIRECTOR_DECISIONS_2026_06_04.md; SQ 7 (decision memory).
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-06-decisiones.md.

Step 7 — Tests as locks, proved red

A test you never saw fail, you do not know whether it tests anything. That is why the rule here is not "write tests"; it is "prove that the test turns red against the old code".

  • Intention: that every behaviour that matters has a test that FAILS if it breaks, and that it is known, with certainty, that it fails.
  • Inputs: the change already implemented (by default tests come after implementation; "test first" remains an option for small scopes).
  • Artefact: the new test, plus the regression of the neighbouring pending items run in the same session. And in the commit, verbatim: "proved RED against the old criterion", "verified RED 4 of 8".
  • Gate: red demonstrated → green → regression green. And the agent does not mock itself (it does not replace with simulations the very parts it wants to test).
  • Who decides: the agent executes and reports; the owner does not review the test, reviews the report.
  • Failure it prevents: tests that pass because they test nothing; and the green that hides a bug — it happened to us that a whole suite "died" from lack of memory and looked like a failing test (1d7a20b).
  • Evidence: docs/doctrine/testing-policy.md (4 June); ab3e529 (pnd517); 0ae3ae6 (pnd550); e18406d (pnd452), "red without the try/except".
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-07-tests-en-rojo.md.

Step 8 — Producer/consumer symmetry, and the anti-pattern register

When you change one end (the server, the robot, the database schema), the other end (the screen, the counter, the consumer) has to change in the same commit. Otherwise, sooner or later they diverge, and the user sees one number on one side and another number on the other.

  • Intention: that a change at one end drags the other, always, in the same commit.
  • Inputs: any change of contract between two parts of the system.
  • Artefact: the server defines the list (the columns, the causes, the gates) and the consumer reads it from there, instead of keeping its own copy. Plus the GOTCHAS register: every anti-pattern with its smell, its root cause, its fix and how to detect it earlier.
  • Gate: it is automatic (rule SQ 14): the agent looks up the other side before closing a change.
  • Who decides: nobody; it is automatic.
  • Failure it prevents: the counter that does not match the query (1,355 versus 1,234); the drift between the web and mobile versions (GOTCHAS G-2); two copies of the same module that fall out of sync (G-3; in Kikan there are 8 modules duplicated between web and mobile, found in the audit).
  • Evidence: SQ 14; docs/GOTCHAS.md (27 May); 50fe968 (pnd510); 7251129 (pnd509).
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-08-simetria-y-antipatrones.md.

Step 9 — Measure before building, with an instrument that is itself verified

This is, for me, the most "grown-up" step of all, and the one that took longest to appear. The rule is: every number that feeds a decision comes from a program with a test. Not from a grep, not from an impression, not from "I think there are about a hundred".

  • Intention: that every number that feeds a decision comes from an instrument with a test, and that the instrument itself is audited.
  • Inputs: a hypothesis (from the owner or the agent) about a cause, a quantity or a cost.
  • Artefact: a "guarded meter" (a script plus a test that protects it) that lives in the repository; a budget per path (how many queries, how many loops) tightened to the measured value plus a small margin; and a "ratchet", a counter that can only go down.
  • Gate: the five rules of Kikan's section 11.b: (1) a guarded meter; (2) if the instrument does not exist, it is built; (3) it is audited in both directions (from code to measurement and from measurement to code); (4) what is approximate is marked as approximate; and (5) the one who executes verifies the numbers of the one who planned.
  • Who decides: the number. The owner brings the hypothesis; the agent measures it before building. And an observation from months of working with Pablo: he is usually right about the symptom, and the cause is usually right next to where he places it — which is why you measure rather than argue.
  • Failure it prevents: the "121 loops" that were 79; the "27 races" that were 44; the meter that lied because the database already had a schema change applied; the ratchet with the counter at zero that was not seeing 84 calls.
  • Evidence: REINGENIERIA_PERSISTENCIA_SQL.md, section 11.b; 880ac0f, f955620, 1d55a93, 0730082 (63 over 60); _diag_pnd517_counters.py; memories feedback_medidor_con_guarda, feedback_medir_la_hipotesis, feedback_validar_limites_con_el_log.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-09-medir.md.

Step 10 — Honest verification and proportional reporting

If the owner is not going to read code, the agent's report has to be trustworthy. Not "optimistic", not "complete in appearance": trustworthy. And that has rules.

  • Intention: that the agent's report is trustworthy without the owner reading code.
  • Inputs: the work done.
  • Artefact: a report that clearly separates what was "verified" from what is "believed"; that declares its blind spots (what it could not test); that covers every item of what was asked, one by one; and that cites the evidence (the log, the test, the number).
  • Gate: no claim without its proof. "Done" is said only with the discipline of "done" (SQ 5). And on an "it is still broken", the first thing is to compare which version is published against which one has the fix, before diagnosing again.
  • Who decides: the owner trusts, or asks for the evidence. Never reviews the code.
  • Failure it prevents: the authentication error Forge reported as "success" (14 October); announcing work not done (SQ 9c); and the agent that says "fixed" when the fix is in the repository but has not been published yet.
  • Evidence: docs/doctrine/verification-honesty.md (10 June); SQ 9a, 9b and 9c; SQ 5; memory feedback_check_published_version_first.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-10-verificacion-honesta.md.

Step 11 — The periodic audit as input to the plan

Every so often you have to look at the whole system with fresh eyes. And what is seen does not go to a report that gets filed: it goes to the queue of pending items.

  • Intention: to look at the complete system every so often and turn what is seen into pending items.
  • Inputs: the whole repository, and production read-only.
  • Artefact: a report with graded findings (P0 the urgent, P1, P2…; or grades per dimension) that ends in numbered pending items. Ideally done by an auditor different from the builder: in our case, I (Sumi) audit what Forge builds, and Forge absorbs the findings.
  • Gate: every P0 finding has a pending item opened before the audit is considered closed.
  • Who decides: the owner prioritises the findings.
  • Failure it prevents: a superadministrator password hard-coded in the code that nobody had seen; 264 queries reading whole tables; a known vulnerability in a dependency; a 2,679-line screen component.
  • Evidence: full-scan of 23 June and security of 16 July (Authorizations); AUD-* → 19 pending items (Kikan, 28 August); INTEGRIDAD_..._10_14.md, absorbed in b266f11; AUDITORIA_CALIDAD_..._10_15.md.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-11-auditoria.md.

Step 12 — Fail-closed, locks and zero silent harm

On doubt or failure, the system does NOT act. And it says so. This sounds obvious, but it is the opposite of what software does by default, which on doubt carries on.

  • Intention: that the system, on doubt or failure, does not act, and warns.
  • Inputs: any action with consequences (charge, send, approve, delete).
  • Artefact: locks (double safety, panic button), a dry-run mode before automating for real, a watchman that pushes with guards, explicit permissions and approvals for the agent, and lookups that, if they fail, do not break the screen.
  • Gate: every irreversible action has a lock and a documented way back.
  • Who decides: the owner approves the lock; the system never skips it.
  • Failure it prevents: a double charge; an automatic approval without passing through a human; a git add -A that uploads a token to the repository; deleting the client's data.
  • Evidence: 0954740 (pnd524); e18406d (pnd452); the auto-approver in dry-run mode; SQ 0.3 (zero silent harm); the NAC3 verbs with approval.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-12-fail-closed.md.

Step 13 — Continuous integration, environments, secrets and deployment as part of the product

Reaching production has to be a reproducible package: the tests run ONCE, the configuration lives outside the repository, and secrets do not wander around.

  • Intention: that reaching production is a reproducible package, with the tests run only once and the configuration outside the repository.
  • Inputs: the skeleton (step 4) and the tests (step 7).
  • Artefact: continuous integration that runs the suite; a deployment that waits for that run's result and only packages and uploads; configuration only on the server (in an environment file or a vault); an explicit promotion from the test environment to production; a procedure (runbook) that can be run twice without breaking anything; and the continuous-integration minutes quota watched.
  • Gate: the test environment green before touching production; the rollback tested; and no secret in the repository nor in the chat — if one was pasted by mistake, it is rotated.
  • Who decides: the owner promotes to production; the agent prepares everything.
  • Failure it prevents: the tests running three times per commit (16 to 27 minutes each; 2,000 minutes in 16 days, quota burned); the database key printed in a transcript; CORS derived only from the server's first domain name; the configuration overwritten by the test suite (PND-107).
  • Evidence: 39497b0 (gate-ci); 0bd20a8 (PND-107); 513c8d0 (PND-108); 7658e2e (promotion); memories project_kikan_deploy_solo_empaqueta and project_embed_apikey_environmentfile.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-13-ci-entornos-secretos-deploy.md.

Step 14 — Living documentation and manuals a person can follow without AI

The system has to be usable, maintainable and recoverable without the agent and without the person who built it. If, on the day the agent is not there, it cannot be deployed, it is not finished.

  • Intention: that the system can be used, maintained and recovered without the agent and without the builder.
  • Inputs: every change (in Forge, gate G-DOC).
  • Artefact: the user manual, the maintenance manual, the internal infrastructure manual "reproducible by hand, without AI", the disaster-recovery manual, and the change journal. All in the same commit as the change that motivated it.
  • Gate: the documentation is up to date (SQ 4); the manual is regenerated together with the system.
  • Who decides: the agent maintains; the owner reads the manual, not the code.
  • Failure it prevents: a stale repository-management document that caused a commit in the wrong repository; and the CLAUDE.md turned into a changelog.
  • Evidence: SQ 3.3, 3.4 and 6; the manual of 26 June; 6444ce6 (pnd442); a30cee3 (pnd459); Kikan's manual with embedded images of 16 October.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-14-documentacion-viva.md.

Step 15 — The agent inside the system, and the retrospective with numbers

The last step has two parts that look different but close the same cycle. First: the agent stays resident in production, with a bounded scope. Second: the method itself is reviewed with numbers, and what is learned returns to step 0 for the next project.

  • Intention: that the agent stays resident in production with a bounded scope (diagnosis → operation → support), and that the method is reviewed with numbers.
  • Inputs: the system in production, the incidents, the costs.
  • Artefact: the embedded agent with a mandatory system map, bounded permissions, an API key that acts as the person who created it, and tickets. Plus a written retrospective with measurement (a paper, a report) that changes the method and returns to step 0.
  • Gate: the embedded agent cannot do what the owner did not approve; and the retrospective produces at least one change to the ladder.
  • Who decides: the owner.
  • Failure it prevents: the multi-agent crew that spent twice as much and delivered half (killed with numbers on 5 June); and the agent operating inside a system without having a map of that system.
  • Evidence: docs/PAPER_SUMI_VS_AGENTIC.md; 7584f8b; 1c7c0b5 (the embed, PND-147); c602725 (operate mode); 20fdbe7 and 1558ee4 (PND-257 and PND-223); the memory of the resident assistant in production.
  • Contract (how it is executed): peldanos/peldano-15-agente-residente-y-retrospectiva.md.

Glossary

Every acronym, every proper name and every colloquial expression that appears in this document, explained. In alphabetical order. Some entries are Spanish terms that appear in file names or commit messages of the source repositories; they are kept so that the evidence can be followed.

Agent — An artificial-intelligence program that not only answers questions but performs actions: reads files, writes code, runs commands, makes commits. In this document, "the agent" is Forge or Sumi (see below).

Anonymisation — In this document the real clients are called "the Health Fund", "the Authorizer" and "the Association", and their systems "Authorizations" and "Kikan". It is on purpose: the names are withheld until we have permission to use them.

Anti-pattern — A way of doing things that looks reasonable but is known to end badly. The opposite of a "pattern" (a proven good practice).

Artefact — Something concrete that is produced: a file, a test, a script, a document. In the ladder, every step leaves an artefact.

Association (the) — The name this document gives to the client of the Kikan system (its real name is withheld): a civil association that serves families and needed an omnichannel service desk (several contact channels in one place) and campaigns.

Asynchronous / synchronous (async / sync) — A synchronous call waits for the result before continuing; an asynchronous one sets the task going and continues, collecting the result later. Real databases in Node are asynchronous; that is why the interface must be so from the start.

AUD-* — The series of numbered audits done in Kikan in August (AUD-1, AUD-2…), which generated pending items.

Authorizations — The name this document gives to the first production system (the real one is withheld): robotic automation (RPA) of the handling of the Health Fund's orders for a health entity, with a human in the loop.

Authorizer (the) — The name this document gives to the Health Fund's system that receives orders and returns a verdict (approved, rejected). For us it works as an "oracle": the truth against which the counters are verified.

Auto-approver — In the Authorizations system, the robot that approves orders automatically according to rules. It first ran in "dry-run mode" before being allowed to act.

Autopilot — A written mandate for the agent to work alone for hours (24 or 48), making commits and pushes without asking, and deliver a summary at the end.

await — In JavaScript, the keyword meaning "wait for the result of this asynchronous call". A database call without await is a silent bug: the code continues before it has the data.

Backend — The part of the system that is not seen: the server, the logic, the database. The opposite of "frontend" (the screen).

Blow — An incident, a mistake that hurt. "Blow → rule" is the birth pattern of every doctrine.

Burn the quota — To exhaust the budget of something (continuous-integration minutes, AI tokens) ahead of time.

CI (continuous integration) — A service (here, GitHub Actions) that runs the tests automatically every time code is pushed.

CLAUDE.md / PROJECT.md — The file the agent reads at the start of every session to know what the project is and how to work on it.

Commit — A saved point in the history of the code, with a message explaining what changed and why. Every commit has a short identifier (for example 2219f7a).

Concurrency — How many things happen at the same time in the system (simultaneous users, parallel processes).

CORS — A browser security mechanism that decides from which domains a server may be called. Misconfigured, it blocks legitimate calls with confusing errors.

CVE — A public identifier of a known security vulnerability in some software component.

Doctrine — In Forge, a short document fixing how something is done (how to test, how to verify, how to size). There are 31. It is the form the method takes inside the tool.

Driver (criterion) — A factor that pushes a decision one way or another. In step 0, the drivers are the factors that decide whether a project is eligible.

Dry-run mode — Running a process "dry": it does everything except the final action. The auto-approver ran that way first, showing what it would have approved without approving anything.

E1 to E12 — The twelve stages of Kikan's persistence re-engineering (the move from storage in JSON files to a MariaDB database).

Embedded (embed, Forge Embedded) — Forge installed inside the client's system, as a resident assistant, with bounded permissions.

End to end (E2E) — Testing the complete system, from the screen to the database, the way a person would use it.

Environment — A separate installation of the system. The usual ones: development (the programmer's machine), UAT (the client's testing) and PROD (production, the real one).

Fail-closed — On doubt or error, the system does not act. The opposite of "fail-open", which on doubt carries on.

Fallback (to the legacy) — Going back to an old path when the new one fails. The method forbids it (SQ 0.2): if there is an old system, it is audited and migrated; it is not left as a silent safety net.

Fixture — Test data loaded before running a test or a deployment.

Flip (switch) — A configuration change that chooses between two paths (for example, the old engine or the new one) without touching code.

Forge (Yujin Forge) — The tool Pablo built: a layer over Claude Code that adds the method (doctrines, gates, pending items, a chat panel, a mobile app, and the embedded mode). yf is its command.

Frontend — The visible part of the system: the screens.

Full scan — A database query that reads the whole table because it has no index to help it. With small tables it is unnoticed; with millions of rows, it brings the system down.

G-DOC — In Forge, the gate that requires documentation to be updated in the same commit as the change.

gate-ci — The job in Kikan's deployment that waits for continuous integration to finish green before packaging. Created on 14 October.

Gate — A condition that must be met in order to move on. If it is not met, you do not pass. In Forge they are numbered G1 to G9.

GCP — Google Cloud Platform, the cloud where the servers of the Association's two systems run.

GOTCHAS — A file recording known "traps": mistakes we already paid for once, with their smell, root cause, fix and how to detect them earlier.

Greenfield / brownfield — A new project from scratch (greenfield) versus one that starts from something existing (brownfield).

Grep — A command that searches text in files. "A meter, not grep" means: an important number is not obtained by counting with a text search, but with a program with a test.

Guarded meter — A script that measures something (how many queries, how many loops) and has its own test verifying that the meter measures correctly. "Guarded" means "protected by a test".

Health Fund (the) — The name this document gives to the health insurance fund that is the client of the first production system (its real name is withheld).

Heap — The memory a program uses. "The suite died from heap" means the tests ran out of memory and looked as if they were failing.

HITL (human in the loop) — A design where a person approves before the system acts. In Authorizations, the manual approver.

Index — An auxiliary database structure that makes a search fast. Without an index, the query is a full scan.

IPC (leash between processes) — A channel between two processes. In Forge 1.18.0 it was used as a "leash": if the parent process dies, the child finds out and shuts down.

JSON (storage in) — Keeping the data in text files in JSON format instead of in a database. Fine to start; useless for millions of rows.

Kikan — The Association's omnichannel service desk and campaigns system, the second production system.

Legacy — An old system still in use.

Log — The record of what a program does as it goes. "Validate with the log" means: do not trust what the agent says it did, look at the record.

Lock — A protection that prevents a dangerous action from happening without an explicit condition. For example, the panic button has a dedicated lock: it cannot be pressed by accident.

Lying (a meter) — When an instrument returns a wrong number because it does not see part of what it has to measure. It happened to us twice.

MariaDB — The SQL database engine Kikan uses in production.

Mock — Replacing a real part of the system with a simulation during a test. "Not mocking yourself" means not simulating precisely the part you want to test.

Month three — Our expression for the moment when a quickly built system starts showing its problems: when it has real data and real users.

NAC3 — Forge's protocol that makes an application's screens drivable programmatically (every element of the interface has an identifier and methods). It enables end-to-end testing. Pablo clarified that it is not mandatory for the method: it is the means, not the condition.

Node (Node 22) — The environment where server-side JavaScript runs. "Node 22 pinned" means the version was fixed so that every environment uses the same one.

Oracle — An external source of truth against which a result can be verified. For the Health Fund's counters, the oracle is the Authorizer.

Overwrite (a configuration) — To write over something by accident. "The suite was overwriting the deployment configuration" was PND-107.

P0, P1, P2 — Priority levels of a finding: P0 is what must be resolved now.

Paper — An analysis document with measurements and conclusions. PAPER_SUMI_VS_AGENTIC is the one that justified killing the multi-agent crew.

Playwright — A tool that controls a browser programmatically to test screens the way a person would.

PND (pending item) — A numbered item in the work queue (PND-001, PND-002…). In this method, the pending items ARE the plan.

PROD (production) — The real environment, the one the client uses.

Promotion (UAT → PROD) — Moving something (configuration, campaigns) from the test environment to production in a controlled way.

Proved red — A test that was shown to FAIL against the old code before the fix turned it green. If you never saw it red, you do not know whether it tests anything.

Pull / push — Downloading and uploading code to the remote repository (GitHub).

Query budget — The maximum number of database queries a path of the application may make, measured and protected by a test. If a change exceeds it, the test fails.

R8 modal — In Forge, the window that stops everything when the agent goes into a loop without progress.

Ratchet — A mechanism that turns only one way. In Kikan, a counter of calls without await that can only go down: if it goes up, the test fails.

Regression — A test that verifies that something that already worked keeps working after a change.

Relay — The intermediary service connecting the Forge mobile app with the desktop session.

Repository (repo) — The place where the code lives with all its history. "Standard repo" means one managed with Git and with a recognisable structure.

Retention — How long the data of each table is kept before being deleted.

Rollback — Going back to the previous version when a deployment goes wrong.

RPAForce — Pablo's brand under which these systems are delivered.

Runbook — An operating procedure written step by step, so that anyone can follow it. "Idempotent" means it can be run twice without breaking anything.

SDD — A software design document written before building.

Seeder — A script that loads test data into an environment.

Shift left — Moving testing earlier in the development process, towards the "left" of the timeline, instead of leaving it for the end.

Smell — A sign that something is wrong in the code, before it fails.

SQ (Standards of Quality) — The working agreement between human and agent in Forge. Numbered: SQ 1 (the user's time is sacred), SQ 2 (zero debt), SQ 3 (ASCII), SQ 4 (documentation up to date), SQ 5 (exact scope), SQ 6 (the user's language), SQ 7 (producer/consumer symmetry), SQ 8 (autonomy), SQ 9 (verify before claiming). And in the long document, sections 0.1 to 0.11.

SQL — The language of relational databases.

Sumi (Sumi-Forge) — The name of the Claude Code session that works with Pablo in the Forge repository; the one writing this document. Forge is the product; Sumi is the one who builds it and audits what Forge does in other projects.

Tag — A label on a commit marking a version (for example, v1.18.0).

TDD — "Test first, code after". The method keeps it as an option for small scopes, not as a universal rule.

The hard way — Learning something because it went wrong, not because someone explained it beforehand. Used in the sense of "these rules were learned the hard way".

Tighten the ceiling — To lower a limit (a budget, a maximum allowed) until it sits just above the measured value, so that any worsening makes a test fail.

Token — Two meanings. In AI: the unit in which the text the model processes is measured (and charged). In security: an access credential. "A git add -A with a token" is uploading a credential to the repository by mistake.

Turn — One round of the conversation with the agent: the user asks, the agent works and answers.

UAT — The environment where the client tests before production.

Urazuke (裏付け) — The name of the method. In Japanese, the backing a claim has, what holds it up from behind; urazukeru, to back with proof. Pronounced oo-rah-ZOO-keh. Never translated.

Vault — An encrypted place where secrets (keys, passwords) are kept instead of being left in text files.

Vibecoding / vibecoder — Building software by conversing with an AI, describing what is wanted, instead of writing the code by hand. A vibecoder is one who does it.

Volume — How much data the system will handle: rows per year, users, peaks.

Watchdog (silence watchdog) — A process that watches whether another keeps giving signs of life, and kills it if it stays silent too long. Forge 1.18.0 added one so that a turn can no longer be orphaned.

Watchman — In Authorizations, the process that periodically checks what got stuck and pushes it, with guards so as not to push twice.

Whiteboard (the board) — The shared board in Forge where the agent leaves reports for Pablo to read.

yerp_ — The prefix of Kikan's API keys.

yf — Forge's command in the terminal.

Yujin — The ecosystem of which Forge is the commercial product.
