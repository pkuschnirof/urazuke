Before you start

Who this course is for

For someone who wants to build software with artificial intelligence and wants that software to be used for real, by real people, for months. It is not a programming course: you are not going to learn a language or a framework. Nor is it a "prompts" course: you are not going to learn tricks to make the model write better. It is a course of method: of what to do in what order, what to ask the model for at each moment, what it has to deliver to you, and how to know —without reading code— that what it delivered is complete.

It is useful to you if you are one of these people:

  • You have a business, a practice, a small consultancy, and you want your own system without hiring a development team.
  • You are an automation consultant or freelancer and your clients ask you for "something more" than a robot.
  • You know how to program, but you want the model to do the heavy lifting and keep the decisions for yourself.
  • You already "vibecoded" something, it worked for two weeks, and in month three it fell over.

It is not useful to you if what you want is a prototype for Friday's demo. There are faster tools for that, and this method is going to feel slow to you. The method is for what has to keep working after the demo.

What you will have at the end

An application of your own, running in production, with: tests that were seen failing before they passed; end-to-end tests of every flow; reproducible deployment with rollback; configuration and secrets outside the repository; the business decisions written down with their date; a semantic tree of the system and the detailed specification of every thing that was built, in its pending items; locks on everything that charges, sends or deletes; three manuals that a person can follow without AI; an audit done by an agent different from the one that built; and a retrospective with numbers. That is what I call "production grade" here: it is not that it works, it is that it can be sustained.

And you will have something more, which is what you really take with you: the reflex of not believing the model without evidence, and of knowing exactly what evidence to ask it for at every step.

What you need

  1. An AI tool for programming. Any one that can read and write files in your repository and run commands: Claude Code, Cursor, Codex CLI, Gemini CLI, Forge. The method does not depend on which one; the main document has a table of where each artefact goes in each tool. In this manual I say "the agent" to refer to whichever one you use.
  2. A GitHub account (or equivalent) and minimal notions of what a repository, a commit and a push are. If you do not have them, spend an afternoon on it before starting; any introductory tutorial is enough. Nothing more is needed.
  3. A project. Ideally it is your own: a system you need or that a client asked you for, with users who are going to use it. If you do not have one, use the guide project I describe below; it is designed to go through the twenty-two steps without skipping any. If what you have is a system that already exists and has to be corrected or made to grow, this first part teaches you the method on a new project, and part 2 of the course teaches you to normalize it first, with a second, inherited guide project.
  4. Time. The course has twenty-two lessons, one per step, and a calendar that tells you in which week each thing falls (see "The course calendar"). A small project, end to end, is between six and eight weeks of part-time work.
  5. A domain owner with time to decide. If the project is yours, that is you. If it is a client's, it is the client, and they have to know that they are going to have to answer questions throughout the whole project. This point is not negotiable: without an owner who decides, the model decides, and there the method breaks.

How the course works

Every lesson has the same structure:

  • What you need to understand: the concept, explained the way you explain it to a person.
  • The task: step by step, with the exact prompt you are going to give the agent (in English, because models follow instructions more precisely in English; below each one it is explained what you are asking for) and what to do with what it gives you back. Before each prompt there is a block that says "Before sending the prompt": the files and the inputs you have to have made yourself, with your own hands, before pasting it. Do not skip it: the prompt names those files, and if they do not exist or are empty, the agent is going to invent them or is going to ask you for them.
  • The completeness check: the list of questions you ask yourself about what the agent delivered, without opening the code. If any of them comes out "no", the lesson is not finished.
  • The deliverable: the file or the evidence that has to exist at the end.
  • Typical mistakes: what happens to almost everybody the first time.
  • In the guide project: what this lesson looks like applied to the example project.

One rule for the whole course: do not move on to the next step with a check at "no". The temptation of "I'll fix it later" is exactly what the method exists to avoid. If a step is not climbed completely, the next one is built on sand.

The guide project: "Turnera"

If you do not have a project of your own, use this one. It is small, but it has everything needed so that none of the twenty-two steps is a fake: real users, personal data, messages that go out into the world, and money.

The business. A practice (it can be a studio, a hairdresser, a workshop: any place that gives appointments). Today the appointments are written in a notebook and confirmed by WhatsApp by hand. Appointments are lost because people forget, there are double bookings because two people wrote in different notebooks, and nobody knows how many appointments were cancelled last month.

What they want. A web schedule where the two receptionists and the three practitioners see the appointments; automatic reminders 24 hours before, by WhatsApp or email; the possibility of asking for a deposit through a payment link for certain appointments; and a couple of numbers at the end of the month (how many appointments, how many no-shows, how many deposits).

The owner. The practice manager. She has judgement about the business (she knows which appointments carry a deposit, how far in advance one can cancel, what to say to a patient who has missed three times) and she has fifteen minutes a day to answer questions. She does not program, and she is not going to read code.

The numbers only she knows. About forty appointments a day, two hundred and fifty days a year. About three messages per appointment (confirmation, reminder, sometimes rescheduling). Patient data is kept for five years. The peak is Monday morning, with the two receptionists entering at the same time. And a question she cannot answer and that matters: if this works, are they going to sell it to other practices? (The answer changes the sizing by ten.)

What has consequences. Sending a reminder (if it is sent twice, it annoys; if it is sent to the wrong number, it is a privacy problem). Charging a deposit (money). Cancelling an appointment (irreversible for the patient who loses it). Deleting a patient (personal data).

The use cases the practice manager names, just as she says them: "I book an appointment", "I look up a patient", "I cancel an appointment", "the patient gets the reminder", "I ask for the deposit", "I see how many appointments there were this month". There are six; they will be the end-to-end flows.

This is enough to start. Everything else the agent is going to ask, and it is part of the exercise that it asks.

The start-up stretch: lessons 0 to 4

These five lessons are done once per project, in week 1, before writing functionality. They are the ones that tempt you most to skip ("I want to see something working"), and they are the ones that cost the most to skip. Do them in one go, and only then open the loop.

Lesson 0 — Can this project be run this way?

What you need to understand. The method does not work for every project, and knowing that before starting is the first quality decision. There are two conditions without which it does not work: that there is a standard repository where the evidence can be left (X1), and that the screens can be driven by a program in order to test end to end, from the screen to the backend (X2). If either of the two fails, the agent is not going to be able to verify itself, and then you are going to end up reading code yourself, which is exactly what the method promises to avoid. I took both from projects I turned down Ref. 46. Then there are weighted criteria (whether there is an owner with time, whether an error has consequences, whether it can be tested in a separate environment, etc.), which I am still calibrating.

For the guide project the two conditions are met: it is a new project (there is no legacy) and we are going to build it with a web interface that can be driven by a program. For a project of your own, this lesson may end in "no-go", and that is a valid outcome of the course.

The task.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands (the agent cannot do this for you):

  • docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md: you write it by hand, from the week 0 intake: what the client wants, for whom, what happens today without the system, what technology there is or is proposed, whether there is a previous system and whether it has a repository, whether the screens are going to be operable by a program, who the owner is and how much time they have, what consequence an error has, what environments there are going to be. Half a page. It does not matter that it is incomplete; in fact, it is part of the exercise that it is incomplete, and what you do not know you write down as "I do not know".
  • docs/ELIGIBILITY_CRITERIA.md: copy the criteria sheet from the main document (the exclusionary criteria X1 and X2 and the candidates), with the answer, source and green-or-red columns left empty. Do not fill it in yourself: the agent fills it in.
  • The project folder, still empty except for those two files, with the agent opened there.
  • The complete content of each file is in the step 0 contract, in the same block.
  1. Paste it this prompt:

We are going to assess whether this project can be run with the Urazuke method. Read the initial survey in docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md and the criteria sheet in docs/ELIGIBILITY_CRITERIA.md. First, evaluate whether the survey is sufficient to answer every criterion. If any criterion cannot be answered from the survey, ask me for the missing information before going on; do not assume. Then fill in the sheet criterion by criterion: the answer, where it comes from, and for each exclusionary criterion whether it is green or red. Do not decide: present me the completed sheet with your reading and your doubts, and I will decide. Reply in my language.

You are asking it to read the two files, to evaluate whether the survey is sufficient and ask for what is missing before answering (this clause is repeated in every prompt of the course; it is the heart of the method), to fill in the sheet saying where each answer comes from, and not to decide: the decision is yours.

  1. Answer its questions. If it did not ask you any, be suspicious and ask it what it assumed.
  2. When it presents you the sheet, read it and decide.

Before sending the prompt for the close: your decision in one line (go, no-go, or go under conditions, each condition verifiable and no more than three), and the answers you gave to its questions in this same session, because the prompt asks it to include them.

  1. Paste it the closing prompt:

Decision: [go / no-go / go under these conditions: …]. Write the final sheet to docs/ELIGIBILITY.md with the date, who decided, the answer to every criterion with its source, and the conditions if any. Include the questions you asked and my answers.

Completeness check. Open docs/ELIGIBILITY.md and ask yourself: do the two exclusionary criteria have an explicit answer and a source? Is there any criterion answered with "probably"? (If there is, the agent had to ask.) Does the decision have a date and a name? Can the conditions, if there are any, be verified? Are the questions it asked and your answers there?

Deliverable. docs/ELIGIBILITY.md, one page.

Typical mistakes. Writing the survey "complete" so that the agent does not ask: you miss the exercise and, worse, you get used to doing the model's job. Answering "go" with five conditions: it is a "no-go" in disguise. And the most common one: skipping the lesson because "it is obvious that it is a go". What is obvious is what does not get written, and what does not get written is what gets argued about later.

In the guide project. The agent should ask, at a minimum, whether the interface is going to be web or mobile (because X2 depends on that), whether there is a previous system even if it is an Excel, and which message channel is going to be used (WhatsApp has requirements that email does not). The sheet ends in "go", with one reasonable condition: having the message channel defined before step 12.

Lesson 1 — The working agreement

What you need to understand. The agent arrives at every session with no memory of the previous one. It is like a new collaborator every morning. The working agreement is what you tell it on the first day and what it is going to re-read every day: how you work, what you do not tolerate, what level of autonomy you give it. Without an agreement, you are going to repeat the same things to it every session, and each time a little differently. I wrote it for the first time after two weeks of repeating myself Ref. 1.

Each of the six minimum rules has a failure behind it: "my time is sacred" prevents it from consulting you on every detail; "zero debt" prevents the "I'll fix it later"; "exact scope" prevents it from adding things "while it is at it"; "documentation in the same commit" prevents old manuals; "never report as done what was not verified" prevents the "done" that was not; "speak my language" prevents it from answering you in English because the prompt was in English.

The task.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands:

  • Your working preferences, thought about for five minutes and written down in a short list (on a piece of paper, or in docs/MY_PREFERENCES.md): do you prefer it to ask you a lot or a little? In what language? What level of autonomy do you give it (commits on its own, deployment to test on its own, production never)? What drives you up the wall when working with other people? You are going to tell it when it asks; the list is so that you do not improvise.
  • The project folder with the agent opened there. Nothing else: it is the project's first file.
  1. Paste it this prompt:

Before we write any code, we are going to set the working agreement between you and me. Write docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md with at least these rules, each with one line explaining what it prevents: (1) My time is sacred: do not ask me what you can resolve on your own, and when you do ask, ask in prose, with your recommendation. (2) Zero technical debt: whatever breaks is fixed at the root, with its regression test, in the same session. (3) Exact scope: no more and no less than what was asked; if you see something else, log it as a pending item, do not do it. (4) Documentation is updated in the same commit as the change. (5) Never report as done something you have not verified; always separate what you verified from what you believe. (6) Speak my language. Add any rules you think this project needs, marked as proposals. If you need to know something about how I work in order to write this, ask me.

You are asking it for the six rules with the failure each one prevents, rules of its own marked as proposals so that you accept or reject them, and to ask you about yourself.

  1. Answer its questions. Read the proposals: accept the ones that are useful to you, reject the ones that are not, always with a one-line reason.

Before sending the prompt for the close: your verdict on each of the agent's proposals, written down: which ones you accept [X, Y], which ones you reject [Z] and why, in one line each. It goes into the gaps of the prompt, and you are the one who fills them in.

  1. Close with:

I accept rules 1 to 6 and your proposals [X, Y]; I reject [Z] because [reason]. Finalize the agreement with today's date and a version number. Then add to the project file the instruction to read this agreement at the start of every session.

If there is no project file yet (you make it in lesson 2), it is going to create a minimal one with that instruction; that is fine.

Completeness check. Are the six rules there, each with the failure it prevents? Is any of them written as an aspiration ("try to…")? Does the project file order it to be read at the start of every session? Did it ask you at least one question about how you work?

Deliverable. docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md with date and version, hooked into the project file.

Typical mistakes. Accepting twenty rules because they all sound good: nobody respects twenty; six or eight get respected. Leaving a rule that says the agent may change the agreement: no. And the quietest one: never testing whether it reads it. In lesson 2 you are going to see how.

In the guide project. A rule of its own that the agent tends to propose for Turnera, and that is worth accepting: "I never send a real message to a patient from an environment that is not production; in test, messages go to a fake inbox". It is step 12 peeking in early.

Lesson 2 — The project definition, the semantic tree and mandatory reading

What you need to understand. The project file (PROJECT.md, or CLAUDE.md, or whatever name your tool uses) is the map the agent reads at the start of every session. It has to be short: what the system is, for whom, what it is NOT, the modules, the decisions taken with their date, and the order to read the agreement. What happened each day does not go there: it goes to the living state (docs/STATUS.md) and to the pending-items register. One of my systems let this file grow to more than nine thousand lines; in that state, nobody reads it, and neither does the model Ref. 41.

But the map has a second piece, and it is the one that does the most work: the semantic tree. It is the system broken down as a tree: the domains at the first level, and under each one its actors, its processes (the use cases you named in the intake, with actor, trigger, steps, outcome and exceptions), its entities with their attributes and their states, its screens and its external integrations. It is the domain model of requirements engineering as it has always been, put in the form of a tree so that the model reads it. From each node will hang the pending items and the decisions that touch it. Its value is twofold: it gives the agent the exact context of the node it is going to touch, and it gives you a way to verify, without reading code, that the system the agent understands is the one the business asked for. If the tree has a state the business never named, the model invented it, and you are going to see it before it builds on it.

The "what it is NOT" section is still the most important one and the one everybody leaves empty. What is not written is what the model is going to invent when it runs into a gap.

The task.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands:

  • docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md, completed with what you learned in lesson 0 (the answers to the agent's questions) and with the business survey: who uses the system, what problem it solves, a section "what the system must NOT do" even if with two items, the use cases the owner names in their own words, the entities with their states, the drawings of the screens even if they are photos of a piece of paper, the external integrations, and the decisions already taken, with their date. If you used an intake method (interviews, use cases, user stories, prototypes), say which one and paste the result.
  • docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md, closed (lesson 1).
  • If there is a previous system, read access to its code or to its documentation, written down in the survey.
  1. Paste it:

We are going to define the project in writing. Read the business survey in docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md and the working agreement in docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md. Evaluate whether the survey is sufficient to describe the system, its users, its domain and its boundaries; if it is not, ask me for what is missing before writing anything. Then write two things. First, PROJECT.md, three hundred lines at most, with: what the system is and for whom; what it is NOT; the main modules as a list; the decisions already made, each with its date; and the instruction to read this file and the semantic tree at the start of every session. Second, docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md: the system as a tree, with the domains at the top level and, under each one, its actors, its processes (the use cases, each with actor, trigger, steps, outcome and exceptions), its entities with their attributes, states and transitions, its screens, and its external integrations. Mark every node you inferred rather than read in the survey, so that I can confirm or correct it. Separately, create docs/STATUS.md for the living state (what is being worked on, what is open): that file changes every day; the other two change only when the system changes. Reply in my language.

You are asking it to ask before drafting, for a short project file with the "what it is not" section, the semantic tree with its complete structure and with the inferred nodes marked, and the living state in another file.

  1. Read PROJECT.md in full. It has to fit in five minutes. Correct whatever is not true or not yours.
  2. Walk the semantic tree as if you were the client. Look at every node marked as inferred and confirm or correct it. Look for missing actors, entity states the business does not use, screens you did not ask for.

Before sending the prompt for the close: PROJECT.md read in full by you, the tree walked node by node with a verdict for every inferred node, and the list of corrections written down for the [list] gap of the prompt. If the list is empty the first time, distrust your reading.

  1. Close with:

Apply these corrections: [list]. Finalize PROJECT.md and docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md with today's date. Add to the working agreement two rules: the change history never goes into PROJECT.md (it goes to docs/STATUS.md or to the pending-items register); and every pending item must hang from a node of the semantic tree, and the tree is updated in the same commit whenever a block adds or changes a node.

  1. The reading test. Close the agent's session and open a new one. Without telling it anything else, ask it: "What must this system not do? And which node of the tree does the schedule screen hang from?". If it answers you with what PROJECT.md and the tree say, the hook works. If it answers you anything else, it is not reading the map: check how your tool loads the project file.

Completeness check. Can PROJECT.md be read in five minutes? Does it say what the system does NOT do? Does the tree have every actor you know, and does every entity have the states the business uses, no more and no less? Did you confirm or correct every inferred node? Does every decision have a date and who took it? Did the agent ask something before drafting? Is the living state in another file? Did it pass the reading test?

Deliverable. PROJECT.md (three hundred lines at most), docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md with the nodes confirmed, docs/STATUS.md, and the reading test passed.

Typical mistakes. Pasting into PROJECT.md the whole specification you already had: it is the programme, not the specification. Leaving "what it is not" empty. Accepting the tree without walking it: the model infers states and screens with a naturalness that is frightening, and every invented node is functionality it is going to build without anyone having asked for it. And not doing the reading test: it is the only way to know that steps 1 and 2 are acting and not just existing.

In the guide project. In Turnera's "what it is NOT" there should be, at a minimum: it is not a medical records system (it does not store medical data), it is not a payment gateway (it uses a payment link from a provider, it does not process cards), and it does not send marketing messages (only confirmations and reminders). The tree has a "Schedule" domain with the actors receptionist and practitioner, the processes "book appointment", "cancel appointment" and "look up patient", the entities Appointment (with states pending, confirmed, cancelled, no-show) and Patient, and the screens "day view" and "patient record"; a "Messages" domain with the process "send reminder", the entity Message and the integration with the WhatsApp provider; and a "Payments" domain with "ask for deposit" and the integration with the payment provider. If the agent adds a "rescheduled" state to the appointment, ask it where it got it from: the practice manager did not name it. Maybe it is needed, maybe not; you decide, not the agent.

Lesson 3 — Size before designing the architecture

What you need to understand. There are decisions that, taken badly, cost weeks: which database, whether the calls to the database are synchronous or asynchronous, how many people can use the system at the same time. And they all depend on numbers that only the business has: how many appointments a day, how many messages per appointment, how many years the data is kept. The model cannot infer those numbers; it can infer what is derived from them (rows per year, size in five years). That is why the prompt requires it to distinguish primary data from derived data, and to ask for the missing primary figures instead of estimating them. When the model estimates a primary figure, it does it with the same straight face as when it computes; and you have no way to tell the difference by the tone. Not having done this in time cost me an entire stage of re-engineering Ref. 15.

There is also a kind of decision that is always worth taking: the asymmetric ones, cheap if they turn out unnecessary and very expensive if they are missing. The canonical example is making the interface with the database asynchronous from day one even if the first database is a file: if you never change database, one await was superfluous; if you do change, you saved yourself rewriting everything.

The task.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands and with the owner:

  • docs/VOLUME_SURVEY.md: sit down with the owner (or with yourself, if the project is yours) and write the main entities (the ones in the semantic tree) and how many there are, how many are added per month, how many events per entity, how many people operate at the peak and when the peak is, how long the data is kept, whether there are exceptional loads, what growth they expect. For every number, write down where it comes from: "the practice manager said so", "counted in September's notebook", "assumption". Leave some at "assumption" on purpose: you are going to see what the agent does with them.
  • PROJECT.md and docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md, closed (lesson 2).
  • If there is a previous system with a database, a read-only access so that the agent can pull real numbers, with the connection string outside the repository.
  1. Paste it:

Before proposing any architecture we are going to size the system. Read PROJECT.md and the volume survey in docs/VOLUME_SURVEY.md. Distinguish primary data (facts only the business knows) from derived data (what can be computed from the primary data). Lesson 5 — The plan is the pending items, and the detailed specification lives in each one

What you need to understand. There is no "plan" on one side and a "task list" on the other: the list of pending items is the plan. But there are two more ideas, and they are the ones that distinguish this method from any old task list.

The first: the detailed specification lives in each pending item. In the classical method there was a requirements specification document, long, that aged before it was finished. Here it is broken down: every pending item carries the node of the semantic tree it hangs from, the user story or the use case that originates it in the client's words, the detailed description, the applicable business rules with their decision and their date, the data that goes in and out, the screens affected, the edge cases, the acceptance criteria in the form "given, when, then", and how it is verified. Reading the specification of a pending item is the closest you are going to get to reading code, and it is exactly the right distance: that is where you detect that the agent understood something else, before it builds.

The second: closed pending items are documentation. When a pending item is closed, it stays with its specification, the decision that originated it, the evidence with which it was closed, the commit and the date. The queue of closed pending items is the functional history of the system, and from there come the changelog and the new sections of the manual. And the golden rule for the agent: whatever appears outside the list is written down as a new pending item, with its specification, not done.

Lesson 8 — Producer/consumer symmetry, and the anti-pattern register

What you need to understand. When you change one end of the system (the server, the database, a robot), the other end (the screen, a counter, a report) has to change in the same commit. If it does not, they diverge, and the user sees one number on one side and a different one on the other Ref. 26. The principle that prevents it is simple to state and hard to sustain: every list has a single owner. The possible states of an appointment, the columns of a report, the reasons something is waiting: one single part (the producer) defines them and all the others read them from there Ref. 27. The semantic tree is the first place where those lists are declared. As soon as there are two copies, one of them is out of date; the only question is which.

The second half of the lesson is the anti-pattern register (GOTCHAS): the mistakes you have already paid for once, written down with their smell, their root cause, their fix and how to detect them earlier Ref. 2. Do the states of the entities match between the code and the semantic tree? Does every GOTCHAS entry have the four parts? Did anything that was already recorded happen again?

Deliverable. docs/PRODUCER_CONSUMER_MAP.md, the routine in the agreement, and docs/GOTCHAS.md (even if it starts with a single entry).

Typical mistakes. Building the map once and never looking at it again: it has to grow with every new contract. Recording anti-patterns as anecdotes ("once it happened that…") without the "how to detect it earlier" part. In the guide project. Smell: "the 11 pm appointment shows up tomorrow". Root cause: two time zones. Fix: a single one, on the server. Detection: a test with an appointment at 11:30 pm.

Lesson 9 — Measure before building, with an instrument that is itself verified

What you need to understand. The model produces claims with a number in them ("there are about 120 queries of that kind") and they sound exactly the same whether they were measured or estimated. You cannot tell them apart by tone. The only way is to demand the instrument: "what did you measure it with? show me the command". The rule of the step is that no number that decides anything comes from an impression: it comes from a meter that lives in the repository, that has a test checking it against a known case, and that you can run yourself.

And there is something more, which I learned the hard way and which is the reason I took so long to formalise a rule I took for granted: meters lie too. In one of my systems, a meter read zero because it did not see part of the code, and another said 27 when there were 44 Ref. 35, Ref. 36. Hence the audit in both directions: from the code to the measurement (is everything that should be counted, counted?) and from the measurement to the code (does everything counted actually exist?).

In a small project, "measuring" is modest: counting how many queries a screen runs when it loads, how long the monthly report takes, how many messages were sent yesterday. You do not need a ratchet or a budget per path. What you do need is a command behind the number.

The task.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands:

  • The hypothesis, in one sentence. In Turnera, a good one: "the day view runs one database query per appointment it shows" (the classic N+1 loop). If it turns out not to be true, even better: you will see how a false hypothesis gets verified. It goes into the gap of the prompt, together with the quantity to measure.
  • A known case for the meter's test, one where you know the answer in advance ("with zero appointments it has to count exactly the baseline queries").
  • Access to what is being measured (the database read-only if it is real data, the log) and the meters that already exist in the repository, named.
  1. Paste it this:

Before building anything, we are going to measure. The hypothesis is: [the day view runs one database query per appointment shown]. Write a meter (a script that lives in the repository) that measures [the number of queries the day view runs for a day with N appointments], and a test for the meter that checks it against a known case where the answer is known in advance. If measuring requires access or data you lack, ask me; do not approximate silently. If some part can only be approximated, mark it as approximate and tell me the margin. Run the meter, show me the number together with the command that produces it, and audit it in both directions: from the code to the measurement (is everything that should be counted, counted?) and from the measurement to the code (does everything counted actually exist?). Only then propose what to build. Reply in my language.

You are asking it for the meter in the repository, the meter's test, to ask for what it lacks instead of approximating, to mark what is approximate, the number with its command, the audit in both directions, and the order: first measure, then propose.

  1. Run the command it gave you, yourself. It has to give you the same number. You do not need to understand the script; you do need to execute it.
  2. If the hypothesis was true and the agent proposes fixing it, ask it for a budget.

Before sending the prompt for the budget: the measured number, run by you with the command it gave you (it has to give you the same), and the margin you are willing to give, decided by you: a little more than what was measured, not double just in case.

  1. Paste it:

That way, if someone puts the loop back in three months from now, the test suite catches it.

Before sending the prompt for rule five: docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md, and a meter that already exists for every quantity a plan might assert; if there is no meter, first the opening prompt of this lesson.

  1. Add rule five to the agreement, for when a plan comes with numbers:

Add this routine to docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md: "Before executing a plan that asserts numbers, verify every number with the meter. If any does not match, stop and tell me; do not execute on a wrong number."

Completeness check. Did you run the meter and get the same number? Does it have a test, and against which known case? Is there any number in the report without a meter behind it? Is what is approximate marked? Is there a budget with a test?

Deliverable. A meter in the repository with its test, a number with its command and date in docs/STATUS.md or in the pending item, and (if it applied) a budget protected by a test.

Typical mistakes. Accepting the number without running the command: half the value of this lesson is in you running it. A meter that reads zero: it is almost never zero; it is almost always failing to see something. And letting the agent "fix while it measures": the order is the method.

In the guide project. If there was a loop, the numbers are 12, 52 and 202; it gets fixed, they become 3, 3 and 3, and the budget is set at 4 with a test that fails if the day view runs more than 4 queries for any number of appointments.

Lesson 10 — Honest verification and proportional reporting

What you need to understand. If you are not going to read code, the agent's report has to be trustworthy, and that does not happen on its own. The model was trained to be helpful and complete, and a report that says "I could not verify this" feels less helpful than one that says "done". So you have to explicitly ask it for the bad news, the blind spots and what it did not do. If you do not ask, it does not give them to you; not because it hides them, but because it does not consider them part of the answer. I once had an authentication error reported to me with the label "success" Ref. 43.

The structure that makes a report honest is three blocks: verified (with the proof next to it), believed (what it thinks works and why it did not test it), and blind spots (what it cannot see from where it is). And one rule: bad news first.

The task.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands:

  • docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md, where the routine goes.
  • The habit of writing down, in every session, the list of what you asked for: the prompt requires it to cover every item one by one, and you have to know how many there were.
  1. Add the reporting routine to the agreement:

Add this routine to docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md: "When you report, separate three blocks: VERIFIED (what you tested, with the command and its output), BELIEVED (what you think works but did not test, and why not), and BLIND SPOTS (what you cannot see from where you are: another environment, real data, the user's browser). Cover every item I asked for, one by one, even when the answer is 'I did not do it'. No claim without its proof next to it. Do not say 'done': say what is done and how you know it. If something went wrong, say it first."

  1. Ask it to close the next pending item and read the report against the checklist below. The first time, almost certainly, the "blind spots" block will be empty. Ask it: "what can you not see from where you are?". There has to be something (your browser, the production environment, the real data). If it insists there is nothing, you insist too.
  2. Practise the "still broken". Ask it for something small, check in the test environment, and if for any reason you do not see the change (it is common: the deployment takes a while, or was not done), tell it "still broken".

Before sending the prompt: your verification written down (at what URL you looked, what version the system shows, what you did, what you expected, what happened) and the commit or the version the agent said contained the fix, copied from its report. Without that, "still broken" is an impression.

  1. Paste it:

Before diagnosing again, compare the version running where I see the problem with the version that contains your fix. Tell me both. If they differ, the problem is publication, not code.

Half of the "still broken" cases are fixes that are in the repository and not in the place you are looking at Ref. 45. This clause saves you hours.

Completeness check. Are the three blocks there? Did you pick a "verified" claim at random, does it have its proof next to it, and could you reproduce it? Does it cover every item you asked for? Is the bad news at the top? Did it ever say a bare "done"?

Deliverable. The routine in the agreement and three consecutive reports with the three blocks, one of them with a real blind spot.

Typical mistakes. Reading only the "verified" block and skipping the other two: the other two are the ones that protect you. Accepting "it should work" inside "verified": that is "believed" in disguise. And getting used to ever longer reports: an inflated report is covering something up with volume.

In the guide project. A good closing report for PND-004 (cancel an appointment) in Turnera: "VERIFIED: the cancellation test passes (1/1, proved red against the version that deleted); regression 6/6; end-to-end of the 'cancel' flow green, with the row marked in the database; in the test environment, appointment 17 of 3 November shows as cancelled by 'reception1' at 10:42 (screenshot). BELIEVED: the monthly report will count cancellations correctly, but I did not test it because the report is PND-011 and does not exist yet. BLIND SPOTS: I cannot see how the 'cancelled' state looks on the receptionist's phone; test that yourself."

Lesson 11 — The periodic audit as input to the plan

What you need to understand. Every so often you have to look at the whole system with fresh eyes, and what is seen does not go into a report that gets filed: it goes into the list of pending items. The important part is "fresh eyes": the one who audits must not be the one who built, because they share their assumptions. A model that built a module "knows" that the password written in the code was provisional; a model that sees it for the first time reports it for what it is. That is how two administrator doors the builder had left in a single day were found Ref. 40. You do not need another company: another session, with another instructions file, that did not take part is enough.

The task.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands:

  • A new session of the agent on a clean copy of the repository (clone it into another folder). If your tool allows it, give it a different instructions file that only says "you are an auditor; you did not build this system".
  • Read-only access for that session to the test environment (database, logs), with read-only credentials kept outside the repository.
  • The scope decided by you for the gap of the prompt, and the previous audit, if there was one.
  1. Paste it this:

Audit this system for [integrity, security, performance and code quality]. Read-only: change nothing except writing your report to docs/AUDIT_[date].md. If auditing some part requires access you lack (the database, the log, an environment), tell me and mark that part as not audited; do not pass it as fine. Go through all of the code, not a sample. For every finding: where it is (file and line), what the risk is, what evidence proves it (the command, the query, the number), and its priority: P0 must be fixed now, P1 soon, P2 when possible. Give a grade to every dimension you audited. End with the list of P0 findings turned into proposed pending items. Reply in my language.

You are asking it for read-only, to declare what it could not audit instead of approving it by omission, all of the code and not a sample, every finding with location, risk, evidence and priority, grades per dimension, and the P0s already as proposed pending items.

  1. Read the report. Pick two findings and ask the auditor to show you the evidence (the command, the line). It has to be able to.

Before sending the prompt for the close: the report read by you, the two findings verified with the auditor, and the builder's session open (this prompt goes to the builder, not to the auditor), with the date of the file in the gap.

  1. Go back to the builder's session and paste it this:

Read docs/AUDIT_[date].md. Open one pending item for every P0 and P1, citing the finding. If you disagree with any of them, tell me with the evidence against; do not ignore it.

  1. Repeat this lesson before the first deployment to production (lesson 13) and, afterwards, every one or two months.

Completeness check. Does the report say what it could NOT audit? Do two findings picked at random have file, line and reproducible evidence? Does every P0 have its pending item open? Will the grades be comparable with the next audit? Did a session other than the one that built do the audit?

Deliverable. docs/AUDIT_[date].md and the P0/P1 pending items opened.

Typical mistakes. Auditing with the same session that built: it will confirm its own assumptions. Accepting a report with no P0 at all on a system older than a month: it is almost never true. And filing the report without opening the pending items: there the audit was good for nothing.

In the guide project. Turnera's first audit usually finds: patient data in the log (phone numbers printed when sending messages: P0); the patient search by name without an index (P1, it will show at 5,000 patients); the appointment form not validating that the date is in the future (P1); and some dependency with a known vulnerability (P1). Four new pending items, all with file and line.

Lesson 12 — Fail-closed, locks and zero silent harm

What you need to understand. Software, by default, goes ahead when in doubt: if the validation fails, the code continues; if the service does not respond, everything is assumed to be fine. It is "fail-open", like a door that opens when the power goes out. The method inverts the default: when in doubt, no. Every consequential action (charging, sending, deleting, approving) carries a lock, has a written way back, and every new automation starts in dry-run mode, logging what it would have done, until you enable it. The locks I put in my first system are the model Ref. 28.

This lesson comes before building the features that act on the world. In Turnera, before the reminders and the deposit, which are the third block.

The task.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands and with the owner:

  • docs/CONSEQUENTIAL_ACTIONS_SURVEY.md: what the system does or will do that charges, sends, approves, deletes or notifies someone outside; and for each action, what happens if it is done twice, what happens if it is done wrong, how it is undone, who should be able to do it. The processes of the semantic tree give you the starting list. The prompt names it "the survey of consequential actions", with no path: tell it where it is.
  • docs/SEMANTIC_TREE.md, up to date.
  1. Paste it this:

Read the survey of consequential actions, and go through the code looking for everything that charges, sends, approves, deletes or notifies outwards. Compare the two lists: if the code has actions the survey does not mention, or the other way round, ask me before going on. For every action propose: whether it is reversible, which lock it gets (explicit confirmation, double safety, dry-run mode, daily limit, a human in the loop), and what the way back is if it goes wrong. General rule: on any doubt or failure along the way, the action is NOT executed and the reason is logged. Present me the table; I confirm lock by lock. Reply in my language.

You are asking it to compare what the business said with what the code does (that is where the actions nobody remembered show up), to ask about the differences, and for every action its reversibility, its lock and its way back, with the fail-closed rule written down.

  1. Confirm lock by lock, with the owner.

Before sending the prompt for the close: for every row of the table, your confirmation or your change, written out for the gaps of the prompt; no row without a verdict, because an unconfirmed lock is a lock that does not get implemented.

  1. Close with:

I confirm locks [list]; change [X] to [Y]. Implement them with one test per lock that proves that, under the doubt condition, the action does NOT happen (proved red against the version without the lock). Record the final table in docs/LOCKS.md. And for any new automation: dry-run mode first, logging what it would have done, until I enable it.

  1. Now open the third block (the reminders, the deposit) with lesson 5, build it with the full loop of lessons 5 to 10, and leave it in dry-run mode. Watch the "would have sent" log for a few days before enabling real sending.

Completeness check. Is every action that charges, sends, approves or deletes in the table? Does every lock have its red test? Does every irreversible action have a written way back? Did the new automations start in dry-run mode, and did you see the log before enabling them?

Deliverable. docs/LOCKS.md, one test per lock proved red, and the automations in dry-run mode with their log.

Typical mistakes. Enabling real sending "to test with a message to my own phone": dry-run mode exists precisely for that. A lock that can be bypassed with a "for testing" parameter: that parameter will end up set in production. And not looking at the "would have done" log: it is the only window to see a mistake before it reaches a patient.

In the guide project. Turnera's lock table: send reminder (not reversible; lock: idempotency per appointment, a single send per appointment and channel, limit of 200 per day, dry-run mode first; way back: none, hence the limit); charge deposit (reversible with a refund; lock: explicit confirmation by the receptionist, amount set by the practitioner; way back: refund through the payment provider); cancel appointment (irreversible for the patient; lock: confirmation and a record of who did it; way back: recreate the appointment); delete patient (irreversible; lock: double confirmation, only the practice manager, and only if there are no future appointments; way back: none, hence double). Each lock with a test that shows that, without the lock, the action happened.

Then propose your scope in three levels: read-only and diagnosis; configuration changes with my approval; consequential actions behind locks. I choose the level. Your credential acts as the person who created it: everything you do is logged under that person's name. Reply in my language.

You are asking it for the map before operating, for the gaps in the map to be declared, three levels of scope so that you pick one, and the credential in a person's name.

  1. Pick the level (to begin with, always the first: read-only and diagnosis).

Before sending the prompt for the close: the map and the three levels read, the level chosen, and one concrete action outside that scope thought up by you, so that the agent attempts it and you see the rejection in the log.

  1. Close with:

Level [1: read-only and diagnosis]. Write in docs/RESIDENT_AGENT.md the map, the scope, the credential (never its value), and the procedure to request my approval. Then prove, with a real attempt recorded in the log, that something outside your scope is rejected.

The task (retrospective, always).

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands:

  • The numbers only you have, in docs/RETROSPECTIVE_INPUTS.md or ready for the chat: how many hours you put in (from your calendar, not from memory), how much you spent on the AI tool (the invoice), how much continuous integration quota you used, and the start and end dates.
  • The list of incidents as you remember them, with an approximate date, to contrast with what the agent pulls out of the pending items and the commits.
  • docs/PENDING.md with the closed items documented and docs/DECISIONS.md, which are the source of the numbers.
  1. Paste it this:

[The project] is over. Do the retrospective with numbers, not impressions: what it cost (tokens, quota, my time), how many pending items, which ones took the most rounds and why, which incidents happened and which step of the ladder each falls into, and which step failed or was missing. If you lack the data for any figure (my time, for instance), ask me for it or mark it as an estimate; do not invent it. End with a concrete proposal to change the ladder: which step is modified, how, and which incident justifies it. Write it to docs/RETROSPECTIVE_[date].md. Reply in my language.

You are asking it for numbers with a source, every incident mapped to a step (an incident that falls into none is a missing step), to ask you for the numbers it does not have, and a concrete proposal to change the ladder.

  1. Read the proposed change. If it seems valid to you, send it to the Urazuke repository as an improvement proposal (see CONTRIBUTING.md): that is how the method learns from every project.

Completeness check. Does the map say what the agent can NOT see? Did you see in the log that an action outside the scope is rejected? Does the credential act on behalf of a person? Does the retrospective have numbers with a source, or adjectives? Does every incident fall into a step? Is there at least one concrete proposed change?

Deliverable. docs/RESIDENT_AGENT.md (if applicable) and docs/RETROSPECTIVE_[date].md.

Typical mistakes. Giving the resident agent a high level "so it can solve everything": start with read-only and raise it when there is evidence. A retrospective that says everything went well: nothing ever goes entirely well; it is written for you, not for the method. And not sending the proposed change: step 15 closes when the ladder changes, not when the document gets written.

In the guide project. Turnera's retrospective, in a typical course: six weeks, about forty hours of the student's time, twenty-eight pending items, the ones that went round the most were the messaging ones (time zones and the WhatsApp provider); two incidents, one in step 8 (the "no-show" state existed on the screen and not on the server, nor in the semantic tree) and another in step 13 (a deployment to production with the test configuration, caught by the end-to-end check after the deployment). Proposed change: that the deployment routine of step 13 require showing the configuration difference between test and production before promoting, not just "what changes".

Operational quality stretch: lessons 16 to 21

These six lessons are the ones I used to do without naming them, and that a reader asked me, with good reason, to write down. Four are done in week 5, together with lessons 13 and 14 and before the first deployment to production: observability, persistence, vulnerabilities and performance. Two are set up in week 6, the week of the first deployment, and run on their own from then on: the two monitorings. If you are a beginner, each of these six step contracts has an "If you are a beginner" paragraph that explains its words in everyday language; read it before the lesson.

Lesson 16 — Observability and traceability, with a configurable level

What you need to understand. When something fails in production, you are going to want to know what happened without opening the code and without the agent "having a look": searching for a number and having the complete story of that action appear, from the screen to the database. That is a structured log record with a correlation id. And you are going to want to raise the detail of what the system writes, for a minute, while you investigate, without redeploying: that is the configurable level. Traceability is the other half: every consequential action (charged, sent, deleted) ends up in a list the application cannot edit, with who, when and what. Without this, the resident agent of lesson 15 operates blind and the monitorings of lessons 20 and 21 have nothing to look at Ref. 16.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands and with the owner:

  • docs/DEPLOYMENT.md (lesson 13), with how the configuration per environment is read: the level is going to be one more variable.
  • docs/LOCKS.md (lesson 12): the list of actions that have to end up in the audit trail.
  • In docs/INITIAL_SURVEY.md, the list of which data are personal (in Turnera: the patient's name, phone number, email and identity document), written with the receptionist in charge. The log records have to mask them; it is a legal obligation.
  • Your decision about where the log records live and how long they are kept. For the course: a rotated file on the server, ninety days.
  1. Paste it the opening prompt of the contract of step 16 (in docs/peldanos/), exactly as it is.
  2. When it shows you the identifier of a real request, search for it yourself in the log records of the test environment: it has to appear on the screen, in the backend, in the database and in the call to the message provider.
  3. Raise the level to debug yourself, do an action, look, lower it. If you had to deploy, the step is not climbed.
  4. Search for "Juan" (or any test name) in the log records: it must not be there.

Before sending the prompt for the routine: docs/OBSERVABILITY.md read by you, and the level raised and lowered by you once.

  1. Add to the agreement the routine of step 16 (in the contract, "Routine"), wrapped in "Add this routine to docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md:" like the others.

Completeness check. Did you follow an identifier through every layer yourself? Did you change the level without deploying? Is there any personal data in the log records? Did the cancellation of an appointment in the test environment end up in the audit trail with who and when? Is docs/OBSERVABILITY.md enough for you to find a record on your own?

Deliverable. Structured log records with a configurable level, the identifier propagated, the audit trail of the consequential actions, docs/OBSERVABILITY.md, and the routine in the agreement.

Typical mistakes. Accepting "logs" that each module writes differently. Leaving production at debug "for now". And the identifier not reaching the nightly reminders job, which is exactly where incidents get lost.

In the guide project. The identifier of "cancel appointment 17" appears in the click, in the server, in the row marked in the database and in the email to the professional. The audit trail says "reception1 cancelled appointment 17 at 10:42 from the day's schedule; outcome: ok". The receptionist in charge raises the level to trace to see why a reminder was late, finds it, and lowers it.

Lesson 17 — Optimization of the persistence layer, measured before and after

What you need to understand. The database is where systems die slowly: fine with a hundred appointments, badly with a hundred thousand. An index is like a book's: without it, the database reads the whole table to find an appointment. An N+1 loop is making a hundred queries to show a hundred appointments instead of one. This lesson applies lesson 9 to the database: measuring every query with a meter that has a test, changing what the numbers say, measuring again on the same data, and leaving a budget with a test so that it does not come back Ref. 18, Ref. 37. It is not rewriting the database or changing the engine: that was lesson 3.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands:

  • docs/SIZING.md and docs/VOLUME_SURVEY.md (lesson 3): the volumes to load.
  • The test environment with data at the expected volume: for Turnera, ten thousand appointments and thirty thousand messages over a year, generated by a script the agent leaves in the repository (ask for it as a prior pending item, with the shape of the semantic tree).
  • docs/PRODUCER_CONSUMER_MAP.md (lesson 8), up to date.
  • A known case for the meter's test: the professionals table has three rows.
  1. Paste it the opening prompt of the contract of step 17.
  2. Read the list of proposed changes the way the receptionist in charge would read a list of repairs for the practice: do you understand what each one gains and what risk it has? Approve or reject them one by one. Redo one calculation by hand (rows per month of the appointments table).

Before sending the prompt for the close: the list with your verdict per change and the redone calculation that matches.

  1. Paste it the closing prompt of the contract of step 17, with the approved list.
  2. Run a meter yourself before and after, with the two commands it gave you.

Completeness check. Do your runs match the table in docs/PERSISTENCE.md? Is there any query left that reads a whole table in a critical flow? Does every table have a written retention? Do the budgets have tests (ask it to exceed one and show you the red)?

Deliverable. The meters with their test, docs/PERSISTENCE.md with before and after, the budgets with their test, and the suite green.

Typical mistakes. Measuring with the skeleton's ten records. Accepting "we had better migrate to another database". And improving a number while breaking the monthly spreadsheet that read that table: the map was missing.

In the guide project. The day's schedule was making one query per appointment (the loop of lesson 9): 52 queries for 50 appointments become 3. The search for patients by name read the whole table: an index brings it down from 800 milliseconds to 4 with ten thousand patients. Retention: the messages are archived after a year; the appointments, never. Budget: 4 queries for the schedule, with a test.

Lesson 18 — Scanning and fixing vulnerabilities

What you need to understand. A vulnerability is a defect somebody can use to get in, steal data or make the system do what it should not. The audit (lesson 11) looks for them with judgement, every so often; this lesson looks for them by machine, all the time, in four layers: the dependencies (other people's pieces that your system uses, with their known defects published), the code (static analysis), the secrets in the repository's history, and the server's configuration. And one rule: upgrading a dependency is a change, and it goes through the loop; "I bumped the version to close the finding" without a regression suite is the most common way of breaking production on a Friday Ref. 41, Ref. 23.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands:

  • Continuous integration running (lessons 4 and 13), and how much quota you have left: the scan adds minutes.
  • docs/ENVIRONMENTS_SURVEY.md with the server, the ports and who administers the domain.
  • docs/AUDIT_[date].md (lesson 11), so that it does not repeat what is known.
  • Your P0 criteria, in two lines: for Turnera, whatever lets somebody in without a credential, whatever exposes patient data, whatever allows sending messages or charging without control.
  1. Paste it the opening prompt of the contract of step 18, with your criteria in the slot.
  2. Read docs/SECURITY_[date].md. Pick two findings and ask it for the evidence. Does it say what it did NOT scan?

Before sending the prompt for the close: the list of approved fixes; for every dependency upgrade, the awareness that it is going to run the regression suite and the end-to-end suite.

  1. Paste it the closing prompt of the contract of step 18.
  2. Ask it to slip in on purpose, on a branch, a dependency with a known vulnerability: the chain has to go red.

Completeness check. Is the last run of the scan green and from what date? Does every P0 have a pending item closed with evidence? Is the scan a gate in docs/GATES.md? Do you know when the next scheduled scan runs?

Deliverable. The four-layer scan in the chain and on a schedule, docs/SECURITY_[date].md, docs/SECURITY.md, the P0s and P1s fixed, the gate.

Typical mistakes. A scan that is "clean on the first try" on a system more than a month old: a layer was missing. Ten dependencies upgraded in one commit. An "accepted" P0 with no pending item and no date.

In the guide project. The Turnera scan finds: a dependency of the PDF generator with a known vulnerability (P1, it is upgraded with a regression suite and end-to-end suite); the header that prevents the schedule from being embedded in another site, absent (P1, one line of configuration, with a test); a test key of the payment provider in the repository's history (P0: it is rotated, even though it is a test one); and a database port open to the world on the test server (P0: it is closed). The scan is left as a gate and runs on Mondays.

Lesson 19 — Performance tests at volume and the breaking point

What you need to understand. Lesson 3 sized the system with calculations; this one checks them with the system running. A load test simulates many users at once. You look at throughput (actions per second), latency (how long each action takes; the median and the 95th percentile, which is what the ones who complain see) and the error rate, at the expected volume and at "times ten". And then you raise the load until something breaks: the breaking point is not "it gets slow" but "the database connections ran out at 180 users". That number is the most useful of the stretch, because the alert thresholds of lesson 20 come out of it Ref. 12, Ref. 44.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands and with the owner:

  • docs/SIZING.md with the expected volume and the "times ten": for Turnera, two users at the peak and forty appointments a day; times ten, twenty users and four hundred appointments.
  • The critical flows for the prompt's slot: booking an appointment, seeing the day's schedule, searching for a patient, the nightly sending of reminders.
  • The tolerance of the receptionist in charge for the most used screen (the day's schedule): "if it takes more than two seconds, the girls complain".
  • The test environment with the data at volume (lesson 17) and, if it differs from production in resources, how it differs noted in docs/ENVIRONMENTS_SURVEY.md.
  • The observability of lesson 16 switched on.
  1. Paste it the opening prompt of the contract of step 19, with the flows and the tolerance in the slots.
  2. Read docs/PERFORMANCE_[date].md with the tolerance alongside: does the day's schedule fit in two seconds at the expected volume? And at times ten? What broke first and at which load?
  3. Decide with the receptionist in charge up to which volume the bottlenecks get fixed now: if they are thinking of selling it to other practices, the "times ten" is now; if not, with a date.

Before sending the prompt for the close: the report read, the decision about the "times ten".

  1. Paste it the closing prompt of the contract of step 19.
  2. Run the script yourself at the expected volume with the command it gave you: the numbers have to look similar.

Completeness check. Does the most used screen fit within the tolerance, or is there a pending item with the number? Does the breaking point say what broke and at which load? Are the thresholds of docs/PERFORMANCE.md below the breaking point and above the expected one? Is the test a gate and is the first run archived?

Deliverable. The load script with its test, docs/PERFORMANCE_[date].md, docs/PERFORMANCE.md with thresholds and gate, and one pending item per bottleneck.

Typical mistakes. "It holds the times ten with no problem" without a breaking point: it did not raise the load enough. A script that hits the API and not the screen. Fixing bottlenecks during the test, "while we were at it".

In the guide project. At the expected volume, the schedule responds in 300 milliseconds; at times ten, in 1.4 seconds; the nightly sending of 400 reminders takes 50 seconds. The breaking point: at 180 concurrent users the connection pool (10) runs out and the actions start failing. Thresholds: an alert if the schedule goes over 1.5 seconds or if the connections in use go over 7. Pending item: the nightly sending in batches of 50, because the receptionist in charge does want to sell it.

Lesson 20 — Monitoring of resources and performance, with a cyclic report

What you need to understand. Lesson 19 says how the system behaves on one day; production is every day, with the data growing. To monitor is to look by machine, all the time, at the usage of resources (processor, memory, disk, connections, the message provider's quota) and at the response time of every function and every query, and to warn when something goes out of range: above a threshold (the ones from lesson 19) or very different from the baseline (what it had been giving). An alert warns at the moment; the cyclic report is read calmly every week or every month. Two rules: an alert nobody reads is a defect of the alert; and a threshold is not raised to silence an alert without a measured reason written down Ref. 37, Ref. 39.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands and with the owner:

  • docs/OBSERVABILITY.md (16), docs/PERSISTENCE.md (17) and docs/PERFORMANCE.md (19) closed.
  • The alert channel decided with the receptionist in charge: who it reaches and through what. For the course: your email and your messaging.
  • The cycle: monthly for Turnera; weekly if there were money at stake every day.
  • The deviation you consider an alarm: 30 % over the average of the last fourteen days.
  • Read-only access for the monitoring to production, with the credential outside the chat.
  1. Paste it the opening prompt of the contract of step 20, with the channel, the cycle and the deviation in the slots.
  2. Ask it to exceed a threshold on purpose in the test environment: the alert has to reach you through the channel.
  3. Read the first report: every out-of-range event has to have a cause or a proposal.

Before sending the prompt for the routine: the first report read by you.

  1. Add to the agreement the routine of step 20, wrapped in "Add this routine to docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md:".

Completeness check. Did the provoked alert reach you? Does the report have every deviation with a cause or a proposal? Is there a pending item for every deviation you decided to attend to, with the number as the criterion? Did any threshold go up since lesson 19, and with what reason? Does the report look at the message provider's quota?

Deliverable. Collection with history, alerts, the cyclic report, docs/MONITORING.md, the routine in the agreement.

Typical mistakes. Alerts every day that nobody reads. Three reports in a row saying "everything normal". A threshold raised "so that it stops warning". Personal data stored "to diagnose".

In the guide project. Turnera's first monthly report says: disk at 40 %, memory stable, the day's schedule at 320 milliseconds median (baseline), the patient search up from 4 to 9 milliseconds (the table is growing; within range), and the message quota at 60 % (proposal: warn at 80 %). One deviation: the receptionist's monthly report took 11 seconds on the 1st (proposal: precompute it at night; pending item).

Lesson 21 — Error monitoring and cyclic report

What you need to understand. A system in production produces errors nobody reports: the receptionist who saw a blank screen and tried again, the nightly job that was cut short. This lesson captures them in the three places where they hide (the backend, the jobs and the browser), groups them (a thousand times the same error is one entry with a counter), warns about the new ones and the ones that grow, and produces a report with the error rate per flow (out of every hundred appointments booked, how many failed). Two rules: no error without a pending item, and there is no such thing as a "known error" without a pending item that says why it is tolerated and until when Ref. 43, Ref. 22.

Before sending the prompt, with your own hands:

  • docs/OBSERVABILITY.md (16): the errors already come out with an identifier, a version and an actor.
  • docs/LOCKS.md (12): the errors in consequential actions are an immediate alert.
  • The channel and the cycle of lesson 20, and the number of occurrences per hour of a known error above which you want an alert: for Turnera, ten.
  • Where the errors are stored and for how long, without personal data.
  1. Paste it the opening prompt of the contract of step 21, with the channel, the cycle and the number in the slots.
  2. Provoke an error yourself in the browser, in the test environment (an impossible date in the appointment form, for example): it has to appear in the capture with your identifier, and the alert has to reach you if it is new.

Before sending the prompt for the routine: the first error report read by you, and docs/PENDING.md at hand.

  1. Add to the agreement the routine of step 21, wrapped like the others.
  2. With the first report: open the corrective pending items for the new errors, each with its reproduction, and close them with the routine of lesson 7 (the test that reproduces it in red).

Completeness check. Did your provoked error reach the capture, the alert and the report? Is there any recurring error with no pending item and no reason with a date? Does a "fixed" error from the report have its test in red in its pending item? Is the error rate per flow there, and compared with the previous period? Is there any personal data in the capture?

Deliverable. The centralized and grouped capture, the alerts, the cyclic report with the rate per flow, docs/MONITORING.md complete, the routine in the agreement, and one pending item per error.

Typical mistakes. "Zero errors in a month" with real users: the capture does not see the browser or the jobs. An error with a thousand occurrences and the comment "known, no impact". Errors that get "fixed" by restarting. And reading the error report without cross-reading it with the resources one: the slow query of the one is the timeout of the other.

In the guide project. Turnera's first report has three errors: one in the browser (the schedule fails if the professional's name has an apostrophe; 14 times; pending item, test in red), one in the nightly job (the message provider returned an error one night; 1 time; pending item to retry with a log record), and a "known" one from the nephew in the monthly spreadsheet (pending item with a date: it is replaced by the report of lesson R2 in part 2). Error rate: 0.3 % in "book appointment", 0 % in the rest.

The go-live checklist

When the twenty-two lessons are done, the system meets this list. It is the operational definition of "production grade" in Urazuke, and it can be used as the final checklist, with the owner, without opening the code:

  1. There is an eligibility sheet with a decision, date and name (S0).
  2. There is a working agreement the agent reads in every session, and it was proved that it reads it (S1, S2).
  3. The project file can be read in five minutes and says what the system is NOT; there is a semantic tree confirmed by the owner, and every pending item hangs from a node (S2).
  4. There is a sizing with primary data with its source, derived data with its arithmetic, and the "times ten" scenario (S3).
  5. The skeleton was deployed empty in the first week, with the integration and deployment chain, and the deployment was run twice (S4).
  6. Every change has its pending item with a detailed specification, and every closed pending item keeps its specification and its evidence: it is the documentation of what was done (S5).
  7. Every business rule has an owner, a date and the exact words, in the commit, in the pending item and in the register (S6).
  8. Every important test was seen red before green; every flow has its end-to-end test that verifies the backend from the screen; the full suite was run at the close of every block and before production (S7).
  9. Every list has a single owner; there is a producer/consumer map; there is an anti-pattern register with automatic checks (S8).
  10. Every number that decided something has a meter with a test that you can run (S9).
  11. Every report separates verified, believed and blind spots (S10).
  12. There was at least one audit by a different session, and its P0s are pending items (S11).
  13. Every consequential action has a lock with a red test and a way back; the automations went through dry-run mode (S12).
  14. The tests run once per commit; the configuration is outside the repository; the rollback was tested; no secret lives in the repository or in the chat without being rotated (S13).
  15. There are three manuals with date and version, assembled from the closed pending items; a person who does not program followed the recovery one; the owner did the acceptance testing on every flow (S14).
  16. There is a retrospective with numbers and a proposed change to the ladder (S15).
  17. The log records are structured, with a level configurable without deploying, with an identifier that follows each action from the screen to the database; the consequential actions are kept in an immutable audit trail; no personal data in the log records (S16).
  18. The real queries were measured at the expected volume, corrected with a before and an after, and there are budgets with a test per critical path (S17).
  19. The vulnerability scan runs on every push and on a schedule, it is a gate, and there is no open P0 (S18).
  20. There is a load test with its test, run at the expected volume and at "times ten", with the breaking point written down and the thresholds derived from it (S19).
  21. Resource usage and response times are watched with alerts and a cyclic report, and every deviation has a cause or a pending item (S20).
  22. The errors are captured in the backend, the jobs and the browser, they are grouped, they are reported per cycle with the rate per flow, and none is left without a pending item (S21).

If all twenty-two say "yes", the system is ready to go live. If any says "no", you already know which step to climb again.

And then: part 2, the inherited project

This first part teaches the method on an empty folder because that is the clearest way to learn it. But in real life you are almost never handed an empty folder: you are handed a system that already runs and you are asked to "fix this" or "add that". Part 2 of the course takes the same practice on another timeline, with an application a nephew built them one summer and that nobody understands in full, and teaches the normalization stretch of the evolutive-corrective path: eight lessons to recover the inputs the method takes for granted, build the safety belt and order the debt, and two more to correct the first bug and build the first new functionality with the full method.

Annex A — How to assess (for whoever teaches the course or self-assesses)

A simple rubric, per lesson, with three levels:

  • Not climbed: the deliverable is missing, or some completeness check says "no".
  • Climbed: the deliverable exists and every check says "yes".
  • Climbed with evidence of judgement: in addition, the student can tell of a case in which the agent did something wrong in that lesson (assumed, invented, over-reported) and how they caught it. That is the real goal of the course: not the artefact, but the reflex.

What is not assessed: the quality of the code. Nobody reads it. What is assessed is the quality of the evidence.

Annex B — Adapting the course to each tool

The prompts are the same in any tool. What changes is where the routines that the course adds to docs/WORKING_AGREEMENT.md go:

  • In Claude Code, the routines go in the project's CLAUDE.md (or the agreement is included from there), and they are read on their own in every session.
  • In Cursor, they go in .cursor/rules/, as always-active rules.
  • In Codex CLI or Gemini CLI, in AGENTS.md or GEMINI.md at the root.
  • In Forge, the agreement and the routines are doctrines, the step prompts are injected on their own when each gate is crossed, the pending-items register is native and the semantic tree is the project's knowledge graph. The course with Forge is the same course with less copying and pasting.

The reading test of lesson 2 holds for all of them: if a new session does not know what the system does NOT do, the tool is not loading the file where you put the agreement.
